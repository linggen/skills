# Now · 4: I Shot the Last Free Throw Shaking, and on the Winter Solstice Night I Counted My Own Heartbeat

Zero point six seconds, down by one, two free throws: what hundreds of pairs of eyes in the hall were waiting to watch were the hands of a player with a one per cent chance of getting on court.

Five days before, those hands had still been accurate.

From the third of December he had been shooting free throws into the north wind on the open-air court beside the stadium, two hundred a day. For the first three days Ahe counted at the side of the court; from the fourth day, all of Room 617 turned out: Specs stood under the basket fetching the ball, and Old Cai stood by the line passing it, reciting a dish from Canteen One with every pass, to "build him up", he said. By the fiftieth, Canteen One's dishes had run out and he switched to Canteen Two's. The open-air court's lights went off at ten, and when they did the three of them walked back in the dark, Old Cai still reciting: "Guobaorou."

A few days later, Sweet Potato from behind the canteen's back door turned up too. The tuft of hair on its forehead stood on end; it squatted outside the wire fence watching for a long while, somehow got through, and sat down under the basket. When the ball hit the rim and bounced out, it shot off, *whoosh*, to fetch it in its mouth. The ball was too big to hold, so it nosed it instead, all the way back to the free-throw line, right to Shen Mang's feet, and sat, its tail wagging into a blur, waiting to be praised. Specs said, "It fetches faster than me." From that day the basket belonged to Sweet Potato, and Specs went to the side of the court to learn vocabulary. There was only one problem: the shots that went in, it chased too, and when it caught them it wouldn't give them back.

His hands split in two places from the cold, and he wrapped them in plasters and kept shooting. One night, after the lights went off and Old Cai and Specs had gone, he shot ten more alone in the dark. He couldn't see the rim clearly, only a black ring; at the moment the ball left his hand, how the fingertips should flick, his hand knew before his eyes did. Six of the ten went in. By the end, he knew with his eyes shut how the fingertips should flick as the ball left: two thousand-odd shots, and his hand had remembered.

Ahe came to watch once, bundled in a down jacket, standing outside the wire fence with her notebook spread against the mesh. He shot ten, and she didn't watch whether they went in. She watched his mouth. "Breathed out on that one." "That one too." "Not that one." When he'd shot ten she closed the notebook: "Whether it goes in is your hands' business. Whether you breathe out is yours." Then she left. Specs said from under the basket, "It's not the shots she's counting." Shen Mang said, "I know."

---

The fourteenth of December, Monday, six in the morning, and Room 617 was still dark. He laid two fingers on his wrist and counted for a minute against the stopwatch: sixty-six. On the morning of the fourteenth of September, it had been seventy-six. He sent sixty-six to Ahe, and she replied: "Noted. Sixty-six."

After sending it, he tapped through Linggen's daily task while he was at it. After a whole term, those two things in the morning were like brushing his teeth; he didn't have to think.

Two in the afternoon, the fifth floor of the lab building, the anatomy lab.

He'd long since stopped minding the smell of formalin. This term, in this room, he had seen the chest, seen the abdomen, seen a leg layer by layer from outside in. Ms Liu Guizhi had taught them all term without saying a word; two more classes, and the white sheet would be drawn over her and not lifted again. Today they were looking at the deepest layer of the abdomen, the one against the spine.

The woman teacher who spoke very softly pointed her tweezers at a small piece of something yellowish and soft on top of the kidney, like a little hat the kidney wore askew. "The adrenal gland. Inside the hat there's a core that does one job: it releases a particular thing into the blood." The tweezers moved inward, to either side of the spine. "Who does it take orders from? From this string."

On either side of the spine ran a thin line, a small knot at every segment, lined up from the top all the way down into the pelvis, fine and even, like a string of beads.

"This string runs from the neck to the pelvis, one small knot to a segment. When a person gets a fright, the brain gives the order and the whole string moves at once: the heart speeds up, the pupils open, the palms sweat, the spit stops, blood is sent from the gut out to the arms and legs; and the core in the hat squeezes another handful into the blood, and pushes the whole lot to the top." She paused. "It knows only one sentence: something's about to happen. What thing, it doesn't ask."

She called them up one at a time, gloved, to run a finger lightly along the string. When it was Shen Mang's turn, he pressed down: it didn't feel like flesh, more like a row of little knots tied on a thin cord, hard, segment by segment. Pressing on that row of little knots, he thought of the two free throws at the scrimmage in October: dry mouth, shaking hands, his heart racing frighteningly. So this was where the thing in charge of all that lived. Breathe in for four, out for six, as Ahe had taught him; she called that putting on the brake. So the brake was fighting this string for control.

Old Cai, next in line behind him, pressed his gloved finger on it, pulled back, pressed again, and asked quietly, "Miss, have I got one of these too?" The teacher said, "Everybody has. Since the day you were born, it hasn't rested one day." Old Cai looked down at his own stomach, as if learning for the first time that such a thing lived in there.

"Miss," Shen Mang put up his hand, "can it tell the difference between... say, an exam and a tiger?"

Someone in the back row laughed. The teacher didn't. She looked at him; she knew him, the one who'd asked the most questions all term. "It can't," she said. "It's faster than your brain. Before your brain has worked out what's happening, it's already acted." She put down the tweezers. "The pathway that makes it slow down is a separate one, and it isn't on the table today. Physiology covers it in detail next term."

Shen Mang took off his gloves, and his palms were all sweat. He thought: this string's coming on court with me on Saturday too.

The bell went. When the white sheet was drawn over, Shen Mang stayed standing a little longer. He had always remembered what Ms Liu had left them: tell the children not to be afraid, and to learn properly. In his heart he said to the white sheet: Ms Liu, not being afraid: I've studied it. I just haven't learned it yet.

---

Five in the evening, the winter-training baseline tests. The gym was still having its floor replaced, so the tests were on the open-air court. The north wind blew the nets out sideways. Ahe, bundled in her down jacket, notebook open on her knees, took them one thing at a time: weight, body fat, the thousand metres, free throws.

The thousand metres came first. When the whistle went he didn't charge; for the first two hundred he stayed honestly in the middle, two steps out, two steps in, using his belly, not his shoulders. At four hundred his legs began to feel heavy, and he breathed out first, breathed out clean, and the breath came in by itself. At six hundred he passed two people, at eight hundred one more. In the last hundred he dug out every bit of strength he had left, still breathing out, breathing out rough and loud.

He ran the thousand metres in four minutes eight seconds. On the eleventh of September it had been four fifty-eight. Ahe stopped the stopwatch and didn't say good or bad, only read out: "Four oh eight." He was bent over, panting, panting deep, breath upon breath; hearing the number, he straightened up, his face burning in the north wind. From September to December, in three months, his thousand metres had got fifty seconds faster.

The scales said two kilos and a bit more than September. Ahe pinched the back of his arm, pinched under his shoulder blade, three times, and he didn't flinch once.

Last came free throws, ten shots. Old Cai passed at the line, Specs stood under the basket, and Sweet Potato sat at Specs's feet; it didn't chase the ones that went in any more, only the ones that hit the rim. Ten, and he made seven.

In Ahe's notebook there was only one line: "Shen Mang. Free throws: 7 of 10."

She shut the notebook: "Of the bench, you're the most accurate."

He was pleased first, and when the pleasure was done his heart gave a lurch: the most accurate on the bench, which meant that if it really came to that... He didn't dare think any further.

The next night he dragged Old Cai and Specs out to the open-air court and told them to shout. Two people couldn't make the noise of eleven, so Old Cai clapped his hands like firecrackers going off, and Specs pinched his voice into a stand-crowd chant: "Num-ber—ze-ro—" Sweet Potato had no idea what they were doing, thought something was wrong, and barked at the basket, *woof woof*, louder than the two of them put together. He stood on the line and shot ten, shot with them shouting, his hands too fast to rein in, just like the October scrimmage. But one thing was different: on all ten, he breathed out. How many went in, he didn't count. On the way back Specs said, "You didn't count today." Shen Mang said, "It's not the shots she counts."

---

On the sixteenth the gym's new floor was finished, and the college team practised back inside for the first time. The smell of varnish stung the nose; with the lights on, the floor shone enough to show your reflection. In the last half hour of practice, Ahe shut her notebook: "Free throws. One person on the line, everyone else round him, shouting."

Since November, the whole team had stood on the baseline every day watching him shoot ten, and they were used to watching; shouting was new. Big Liu went first. Eleven people surrounded him clapping and stamping, someone banging two shoe soles together, a din fit to shake the sky. Big Liu made six of ten, and when he'd finished he yelled at the crowd round him: "You're not shouting loud enough! Louder and I'd make eight!" The captain, Xing Bin, went up and made eight of ten without a word. When it was Shen Mang's turn, he stood on the line, and before he even had the ball his ears went red.

Eleven people started shouting at once. Old Cai wasn't on the team, but he was squatting by the door watching the fun, and he joined in with a yell of his own: "Number Zero, go on, shoot!"

He shot ten, each faster than the last, and the breath he'd remembered last night, this time he even forgot to breathe out. Of ten, only two went in.

On the open-air court, into the north wind, alone, he made seven of ten; on the new floor, with eleven people round him shouting, only two of ten. The same hands, the same ball, and the rim hadn't moved an inch.

After everyone had gone, he sat alone at the side of the court undoing his laces. Ahe came over, finished writing that line in her notebook, didn't show it to him, and only said, "The five you're short aren't in your hands."

"Then where are they?"

Ahe thought, and didn't answer. She tucked the notebook under her arm, walked to the door, and turned back: "On the nineteenth, if it comes to that, they'll be shouting just like this too."

That night, Room 617 ran an experiment. The idea was Specs's: "You sit still. We shout. Watch your fitness band." Shen Mang sat cross-legged on the lower bunk without moving, and the number on the band stayed level and steady. Old Cai cleared his throat, leaned in to his ear, and with all the strength of his life, spraying half his face with spit, bellowed: "Number Zero! One per cent!"

Shen Mang didn't even blink. The number on the band jumped and jumped, up to a hundred and twelve.

The three of them looked at the number for a long time. Specs said, "You haven't run a step, and your heart's going like you've climbed five flights of stairs. One shout, and it's the accelerator floored." Old Cai said, "I've got a good voice."

Shen Mang ignored them. He stared at that hundred and twelve and thought of the string of little knots on the dissection table two afternoons before: something's about to happen, what thing, it doesn't ask. The stands or the wilds, it can't tell them apart. So it wasn't his hands that weren't good enough; it was his body thinking something was about to happen.

"Then how do you switch it off?"

Specs pushed his glasses up: "You can't. The teacher said in anatomy two days ago: it's faster than your brain."

"Again," said Shen Mang. "This time I'll breathe out."

Old Cai leaned in again, and again bellowed. Shen Mang shut his eyes, breathed in for four, out for six, breathed out slowly, so slowly that Old Cai had finished his second shout and the breath still wasn't all out. The three of them stared at the band. The number dropped a stretch, dropped to about halfway, and stopped dropping, and stayed there, jumping and jumping.

"It brakes one stretch," said Specs. "It doesn't brake clean."

"And the stretch that's left?"

Specs thought, and didn't answer. That night Shen Mang lay on the lower bunk with the band long since off, but he felt that leftover stretch still jumping in his chest, lying there with him.

---

The seventeenth, Thursday, first period in the morning was still Introduction to Acupuncture and Tuina. Teacher Yan didn't give a dictation before the end; she only wrote one line on the board: "22 Dec (Winter Solstice), timetable change: last dictation before the final. The twelve regular meridians. Write the names. Write the courses." The stub of chalk tapped "courses" twice: "Not one missing." Specs said quietly beside him, "Five days left." The names he'd learned back in September; how many could he do with the courses as well? Shen Mang counted under the desk: nine. The other three, like Saturday's final, were up ahead waiting for him.

In the afternoon he went to the Rare Books Department again. Granny Ge was tracing characters in pencil on a borrowing card, and the moment he came in she looked at him over her reading glasses: "There's a match coming, isn't there? Your face is white as paper."

"Granny Ge, tell me: when a person gets nervous, what can they do not to be?"

Granny Ge put down her pencil, thought, and began. She started from a Song-dynasty edition she'd repaired when she was young, went on to the paper being bamboo, to which month bamboo should be cut in, to the bamboo grove on the hill behind her old home, to the year a badger came into the bamboo grove... Shen Mang listened patiently for twenty minutes, and the badger still hadn't been caught. Better to rely on himself than on others, he thought: right now his head was full of free throws, so turn it round, think of nothing at all. That had to work.

He sat down at the table, closed his eyes, and tried to think of nothing. In the first second, he thought of the words "think of nothing"; in the second, of that "1%" on the confession wall; in the third, of Ma Xiaobao's big squeaking shaker cup; by the fifth he was already standing on the free-throw line, hundreds of pairs of eyes in the stands all fixed on his hands. His eyes flew open, and he was in a cold sweat.

Granny Ge didn't look up: "The more you try not to think, the more you think. I was the same when I was young."

He asked Granny Ge to bring out the case of *On Nourishing Life* from September, and turned to the page about breathing out. Last time he'd only looked at the margins; this time he read the text:

"[Whenever you move the qi, take the qi in through the nose and breathe it out through the mouth, faintly, drawing it out long. This is called the long breath.]{典=yangxing-changxi}"

The breath he let out before releasing a free throw had a name, then. In the margin beside this line, under "Tried it. Out longer than in, and the heart did slow," there was another pencil note in the same hand, small and hurried, so faint it had almost gone:

"Tried it the night before the exam. Hands still shook. But I put the brush down."

Shen Mang looked at that line a long time. The hands still shook, but the brush went down. He didn't know what exam the person who wrote it had sat, or whether they'd passed. He only knew that decades ago someone had been afraid just like him, and, afraid as they were, had put the brush down.

When he went to collect his bag, he crouched again in front of the low bookshelf by the door, drew the first volume of the notebooks out of that thick case, and turned to a page near the back: "Nineteen, third day of the ninth month, the clifftop. Saw a door, with writing on it. Afraid." Below it, on a new line: "When afraid, first let out a long breath."

So the old man had been afraid too. He copied the two lines into the notes on his phone, and when he'd copied them, copied "first let out a long breath" again on its own.

Granny Ge said from the doorway, "What time's the match on Saturday?"

"Seven in the evening."

"Oh. Seven." She pushed her reading glasses up and went back to tracing her characters.

That night, Old Cai's computer went on strike. He wanted to print out the charts of the courses of the twelve meridians to learn them, and when he clicked, the circle spun for ten minutes and then a line of red text popped up: insufficient disk space. Old Cai clutched his laptop and wailed: "Five days left, and I can't even print the charts."

Shen Mang took it, opened the "Master" tab in Linggen, and had it scan. When it finished it listed a table, and the top line was a folder of forty-odd gigabytes called "New Folder (3)", containing two thousand-odd episodes of cultivation-novel audiobooks. Old Cai clapped a hand over the screen: "Not that one! That's my Dao heart!" Specs said, "Delete the others." Three games installed and never opened, a heap of temporary files from who knew what year; once those were deleted the computer ran as if oiled, and the charts printed. Old Cai held the warm sheets in both hands: "I've got a spirit root, and now I've got a master. All I need is a cave abode."

Specs, on the bunk opposite, looked at the list on the screen and said, out of nowhere: "End of September a report came out of Cambridge, signed by Hinton and Bengio. It says if AI can do its own AI research and change itself, there could be an intelligence explosion: a year's progress done in five weeks."

Old Cai said, "Isn't that just washing its own marrow and cleansing its own bones? Five weeks in seclusion, ascends in one night, and leaves all of us mortals down below."

Shen Mang looked at the little icon on the screen. In November it had told him he could only add ten per cent a week. Outside the window it was dark, and he thought: and five weeks after that?

Old Cai installed a Linggen of his own there and then. For the next few days, what he watched on his phone at night wasn't audiobooks but a book in Linggen called *The Nine Cauldrons*, and at midnight he was still turning over on the upper bunk: "This Nine Cauldrons, it's so good, I can't stop reading." Shen Mang, on the lower bunk, was learning the Lung Meridian, and didn't respond.

---

At noon on the eighteenth, in Canteen One, he ran into Ma Xiaobao. Ma Xiaobao had three trays in front of him, eaten down to the trays, and was wiping his mouth on his way to the counter, apparently for a fourth. Passing Shen Mang's table, he stopped a moment, picked an egg off Shen Mang's tray, and weighed it in his palm. The egg knocked lightly against his palm, twice: that hand was shaking. In a December canteen, there was a film of sweat on his forehead. Shen Mang looked at the hand and thought of the night of the power cut, when he had felt in the dark Ma Xiaobao's wrist jumping fast and erratic, and of that white bottle. He opened his mouth, meaning to ask, and in the end didn't make a sound. Ma Xiaobao put the egg back: "Number Zero, tomorrow I'm under the basket. Where are you?" Before Shen Mang could answer, he laughed first. "Oh, right. The bench. Bench is good. Bench doesn't wobble."

Shen Mang looked down and peeled his egg. Ma Xiaobao had walked two steps when he turned back: "Oh, and tomorrow night there's a livestream, it's up on the school's official account. Whole school'll be watching. Nothing to it."

Shen Mang peeled and peeled until he'd dug a hole in the white.

That afternoon was the last practice before the final. When everyone had gone, Ahe walked to the free-throw line. He was standing there with the ball in his arms, not shooting.

"Two thousand-odd in the north wind," she said. "Have your hands remembered?"

"My hands have remembered."

"Then when it comes to it, don't think about your hands." She tapped his chest with her pen. "There's only one thing you think about: breathing out. Two bounces, breathe out, bend the knees, raise the hands. Hands' business, leave to the hands. What you know well, you can do even shaking; what you don't, you can't do even when you're not."

"What if my hands won't listen?"

"Then I write down: hands wouldn't listen." She clicked the pen cap on. "What you've been training isn't not shaking. What you've been training is shooting while you shake."

He stood on the line and did as she said: two bounces, breathe out, bend the knees, raise the hands. In. Nobody was watching, so it didn't count whether it went in; but in that moment he felt it: as he let out the breath, his hands moved by themselves, without waiting for him.

Back in Room 617, Old Cai was lying on the floor with a flattened delivery box in front of him, a black marker in his hand, writing out four big characters stroke by stroke: Fellow Daoist Shen, Steady. When he'd finished he held it up to show him, a black smudge still on the tip of his nose. Specs glanced over from the bunk opposite: "Tomorrow I'm sitting next to you and pretending I don't know you." Old Cai said, "I went over 'Steady' three times."

At ten, his mother posted a message in the parents' group, with a link: "Tomorrow at seven, livestream of the school basketball final. Number 0 is my son." In under ten minutes there were thirty-seven "Go for it!"s underneath, one "He's grown so tall", and one asking: "Is that the one who ran the thousand metres?" His mother replied: "That's him."

Shen Mang went "Nnnnngh—", pulled the quilt over his head, and kicked his legs about under it. Now it was perfect: hundreds of people in the stands, thousands on the confession wall, and on top of that a hundred-odd aunties and uncles in the parents' group, and his dad. When he'd kicked enough, he lay still under the quilt, and only then understood that what he was afraid of wasn't just missing. It was missing just when all these people happened to be watching.

Near midnight, he messaged Ahe: "If it really comes to that and I miss, what then?"

At her end "typing..." went on a long time, stopped, typed again, stopped again. At last came one line: "Then I write down: missed." After a while, another: "What's written down doesn't get lost."

He read those two lines many times. Then he went to his notes and found the line he'd copied on Thursday: When afraid, first let out a long breath. Under the quilt he let out a breath, a long one, and when he'd let it out, the light of the phone still lit the inside of the quilt, lit it until the battery died.

---

The nineteenth of December, six fifty-two in the evening, and the smell of varnish on the new floor still hadn't gone. The stands were full, and the aisles were full of people standing too, more standing than sitting. On the bench, Ahe had set out a row of paper cups, twelve, upside down, and the last one had "0" written on it; it hadn't been turned up once since he sprained his ankle in November.

He had already been to the toilet three times. As he went out the third time, Ahe didn't look up: "That's the third time." He hadn't known she counted that too.

Back from the third trip, he looked for people in the stands. In the top row, Granny Ge, in blue cloth oversleeves, was knitting a sock, her reading glasses hanging on their chain; she told the person next to her she hadn't come for the basketball, she'd come to see "what that boy who comes every day to copy books actually does". Old Cai in the front row was holding up the cardboard: "Fellow Daoist Shen, Steady". Specs sat next to him with his face turned the other way, really as if he didn't know him. Shen Mang smiled at them, a smile like toothache.

Behind the scorers' table sat Coach Lei; today he was referee-in-chief, and his whistle had been swapped for a pen. In the other half, the Chinese Pharmacy school was warming up. Every time Ma Xiaobao dunked, the stands cheered. On the fifth dunk he landed, looked over at the last seat on the Acupuncture and Tuina bench, and raised the bottle of mineral water in his hand; he hadn't brought the shaker cup today. Shen Mang looked down at his own laces. They weren't undone. He tied them again.

At six fifty-five Ahe read out the five starting names, then the seven substitutes, and when she reached the last one, "Shen Mang", she didn't pause or speed up; she read it like anyone else's. Then she turned to a new page, and as she flipped past a page from October his sharp eyes caught a line: "Shen Mang. Why play basketball. A: wants to run a bit faster. Half believed." Before his ears had finished burning, she had written: "19 Dec. From 11/9, today is day one hundred."

"What about a hundred days?"

"The first two months, what grows is the nerves: the hands learn. Around three months, that's when the body itself grows." She clicked the pen cap on. "On the eleventh of September, you were sick by the side of the track."

In the parents' group, his dad sent a photo: the old TV at home showing the livestream, his dad with one leg up on a little stool, a knee brace wrapped round the knee, a plate of sunflower seeds beside him. Underneath, two words: "In position." Shen Mang looked at the knee brace. That leg hadn't touched an accelerator since the second of November.

Seven o'clock, tip-off. The Chinese Pharmacy team wore new jerseys with gold lettering, a gift from Ma's Pharmacy, and Ma Xiaobao held the paint and bounced back anyone who came. On the third possession, Big Liu jumped under the basket, Ma Xiaobao jumped with him, half a head higher, and swatted the ball into the stands, into a girl's popcorn bucket in the third row, and up went a cloud of popcorn. The Chinese Pharmacy stands roared. At the end of the first quarter, Chinese Pharmacy led by nine.

In the second quarter, the Acupuncture and Tuina captain clawed back points on his own, shooting and driving, and cut it by two. He was a man of few words, and played with few words too: no celebrating, no roaring; when he scored he ran straight back, and as he passed the bench he brushed the back of his hand along the row of paper cups, as if counting. When he reached the last one, the back of his hand paused a moment, and went on. At half time, Acupuncture and Tuina trailed by seven.

Nobody spoke in the changing room. Shen Mang went to the toilet a fourth time, and as he washed his hands he looked up at the mirror: the face of the person in the mirror was as white as one of Granny Ge's borrowing cards.

In the third quarter, a ball rolled over the baseline and stopped at Shen Mang's feet. He bent to pick it up and pass it back, and hundreds of pairs of eyes in the stands followed the ball and landed on him all at once. His hand shook, the ball slipped away and bounced off the referee's foot.

The stands burst out laughing: "Number Zero! Chance of getting on just went up to two per cent!"

He sat back on the bench, red all the way to the roots of his ears. Shen Mang, oh Shen Mang, you can't even pass a ball back, and you want to take free throws. At the side of the court Ahe bent her head and wrote a line; what that line said, he didn't dare think.

In the second half of the third quarter, Big Liu picked up his fourth foul and was subbed off, and sat next to him, panting so hard his shoulders heaved. Number Nine went on and held the fort five minutes, scored nothing, and got dunked on by Ma Xiaobao. The Chinese Pharmacy crowd held up their phones to film it, shouting as they filmed: "Another one!"

He sat on the bench. His mouth was dry, and still dry after he drank; his palms were cold, and in the cold they sweated; sitting perfectly still, he could feel the blood vessel in his neck throbbing beat by beat; the noise of the court went far away in waves, as if someone were slowly turning down a radio. The string of little knots he'd seen on the dissection table was all in him now, segment by segment, every one awake.

In the fourth quarter, Big Liu went back on. Four fouls; he didn't dare risk another, and could only block with his body, and every block, Ma Xiaobao knocked him back a step. The score bit back point by point. In the last two minutes, a three from Acupuncture and Tuina tied it; Ma Xiaobao turned under the basket and banked one in, took a hit, made the and-one, and was three ahead again; Big Liu gritted his teeth and hooked one in, pulling it back to one. Everyone in the stands was on their feet; the only one still sitting was Granny Ge, her sock knitted to the heel. Last ten seconds, sixty-one to sixty-two. Big Liu spun towards the basket, Ma Xiaobao stepped across, and the two collided in mid-air. Whistle. Big Liu landed with his left foot right on Ma Xiaobao's shoe, his ankle turned out, and he sat down on the floor holding his foot. Ma Xiaobao froze a moment, then crouched down first, reached out to support his ankle, and said, "You OK? You OK?" He didn't sound like an opponent.

The referee called a foul on Ma Xiaobao: two free throws to Acupuncture and Tuina. And at that moment, there was only zero point six seconds left on the clock.

Big Liu couldn't stand up.

"The injured player can't shoot," said Coach Lei, standing up behind the scorers' table. "The substitute shoots."

A hum went round the stands. In the front row, Specs pushed his glasses up and said to Old Cai, "I looked it up. An injured player's free throws are taken by the one who comes on for him. It's in the rules." Old Cai said, "So who's going on?" Specs didn't answer. He looked at the last seat on the bench.

Ahe was already on her feet. She wrote a "0" on the substitution slip and handed it to the scorers' table.

Big Liu was helped past the bench, past that last seat, breathing hard, and said only two words: "Number Zero."

Shen Mang didn't move.

A string of reasons lined up in his head at once, each more respectable than the last: cold hands; not warmed up; Number Nine had three years more shooting practice than him; he was only there to make up the numbers, he shouldn't be going on at all. The last one to surface was in his dad's voice, the line from the video call in September: if you can't outrun it, walk away.

His backside, on that bench, seemed to have grown roots.

He looked up and saw the row of paper cups. Eleven had all been turned up, wet at the bottom. The last one was still upside down, its "0" black, facing him.

He reached out and turned it over, took a sip of nothing, put it down. Then he stood up. His legs were soft; he stood up anyway.

From the bench to the free-throw line, a dozen-odd steps. In those dozen-odd steps the gym seemed suddenly to grow huge. The lights were too bright, the stands too high, and on every side there were phones, each one lit, like patch after patch of eyes. The players had lined up along both sides of the lane; Ma Xiaobao stood in the spot nearest the basket, hands on his knees, and cocked his head at him. He glanced at the sideline: Ahe wasn't looking at him; head down, pen point resting on the paper, as if waiting.

Someone in the Chinese Pharmacy stands started chanting, then a dozen joined in, and it found a beat: "One per cent! One per cent!" He thought of the sixteenth, eleven people round him shouting. This time it was hundreds. Ma Xiaobao, under the basket, nodded at him and said, not loudly: "Take your time, Number Zero. No rush."

The referee handed him the ball. By the rules, once you had the ball you had five seconds to shoot. Only one thought was left in his head: quick, quick, get rid of it, get rid of it and you won't have to stand here. The ball in his hands was heavier than all two thousand-odd he'd ever shot, the pebbled leather digging grain by grain into his sweaty palms. He didn't even bounce it. Bent his knees, raised his hands—

*Clang*: off the front of the rim, and out.

Half the stands groaned, half cheered. The Chinese Pharmacy bench leapt up. His ears went *hummm*, and he couldn't hear anything; he only saw Ma Xiaobao's mouth moving.

He had one free throw left.

---

The referee took the ball back and waited for both sides to line up. These few seconds the rules had left him.

He stopped thinking, and did something else instead: in for four, out for six, breathing out so slowly it was as if he meant to pour everything in his belly out clean. Ahe had said in October that this was putting on the brake; on the night of the sixteenth he had seen it for himself on the band: it brakes one stretch, it doesn't brake clean. His hands were still shaking. But he had done this two thousand-odd times in the north wind, done it till he didn't need to think. What you know well, leave to the body, and it can be done even shaking.

The layer of humming in his ears went down a little, and he heard a sound, very small, very close: at the sideline, Ahe's pen, *click*, pressed once.

She was going to write it down. In or not, she was going to write it down.

The fear was still there, and not one person had left the stands. But fear, it turned out, wasn't something you pressed down. It was something you carried with you.

He bounced the ball twice, bent his knees, raised his hands. His hands were trembling, and he didn't wait for them to stop.

The ball struck the back of the rim, bounced up, came down on the rim, rolled once round the iron hoop, and half round again. The "One per cent" from the stands broke off halfway.

The ball dropped in.

His hands were still raised, and two words came out of his mouth, so small only he could hear them: "Done it!"

---

Zero point six seconds left. Chinese Pharmacy inbounded from the baseline, Ma Xiaobao caught it at half court, turned and threw at once; the ball flew most of the length of the court, hit the top of the backboard, and bounced out of bounds.

Five minutes of overtime, and Acupuncture and Tuina won by three, not one point of them his. He'd been subbed off as soon as he'd taken the free throws, and sat on the bench gripping that turned-up paper cup until the final whistle. Big Liu sat beside him with an ice pack on his ankle, and every time they scored he thumped Shen Mang's thigh with his fist. At the final whistle Big Liu hooked an arm round his neck and, panting, said the longest sentence he'd said to him all term: "The second one, you shot that yourself." Old Cai vaulted down from the stands, held up his "Fellow Daoist Shen, Steady" and ran a lap of the sideline, and was turned back by security.

At the sideline Ahe finished writing the last line, closed her notebook, looked his way, said nothing, and nodded once. Then she cleared the bench, putting the paper cups one by one into a rubbish bag, and when she got to the last one, the one crushed into a ball, she looked at it, didn't throw it away, smoothed it flat, and put it in her down jacket pocket. Coach Lei came round from behind the scorers' table and shoved the match ball into his arms: "For Number Zero."

The stands had mostly emptied. Chinese Pharmacy had just lost the final. Ma Xiaobao sat alone on the new floor undoing his laces, saw Shen Mang standing a few steps away holding the ball, and even smiled at him: "Number Zero, that last free throw, not bad. Losing one game's nothing. Next year we'll win it back." But his hands were shaking, shaking out of his control, just like yesterday noon weighing the egg: the lace wound twice round his fingers and wouldn't come undone. On the third try, the hand stopped in mid-air, and Ma Xiaobao's whole body tipped backwards and fell onto the floor.

He hadn't tripped, and he hadn't slipped. His whole body had gone soft at once, like a sack of grain sliding off the back of a truck.

Shen Mang was only four steps from Ma Xiaobao. In those four steps as he ran, what he thought of wasn't any class. It was the night of the power cut in November, Ma Xiaobao's wrist that he had felt in the dark: back then Ma Xiaobao's heart was already beating too fast to count, and in the middle of the beating it would skip one. Ma Xiaobao's heart had been wrong even then. He knelt and pressed two fingers to Ma Xiaobao's neck: beat chasing beat, like someone inside pounding on a door with his fist, pounding and pounding, then a gap. Ma Xiaobao's eyes were still open, his lips grey: "...My heart... it's jumping out..."

"Coach Lei! Call 120!" His voice cracked. "Ahe! The yellow box on the wall by the door, get it, quick!"

Ahe had already dropped the rubbish bag and was running for the door, her ponytail flying.

Shen Mang put the match ball Coach Lei had just given him under Ma Xiaobao's calves to raise his legs, so the blood would run back to his heart and head. Then he knelt again at Ma Xiaobao's shoulder, put two fingers back on his neck, bent close, and said, looking into his eyes: "Xiaobao, look at me. Don't breathe in yet. Breathe out, longer than you breathe in, all the way to the bottom." He breathed out first himself, long, just like that breath on the free-throw line. Ma Xiaobao breathed out after him, panting halfway; he didn't hurry him, but led him breath by breath, just like the night of the power cut. His fingers stayed on Ma Xiaobao's neck counting the beats: at first too fast to count, a while later still too fast to count, and a while after that, countable. Twenty-three in ten seconds; ten seconds more, nineteen. It was slowing.

Ahe knelt on the other side and opened the yellow box. Inside was an automated defibrillator: "Pads on first. Ready, just in case." One pad on the left, one on the right. The machine spoke, a level woman's voice: "Analysing heart rhythm. Do not touch the patient." Everyone's hands came up. The machine spoke again: "No shock advised."

When the ambulance arrived, the heart under Shen Mang's fingers had slowed. Still fast, but one beat was one beat now. The stretcher was lifted, and the doctor riding with it asked, "Has he taken anything?"

Ma Xiaobao lay on the stretcher looking at Shen Mang. His eyes were begging: don't tell.

On the night of the power cut in November, Shen Mang had felt Ma Xiaobao's white bottle on the bathroom floor, and put it back, and even said to Ma Xiaobao, "It's this dark, I didn't see a thing," which was as good as promising to keep it quiet for him. But now, if the doctor didn't know what Ma Xiaobao had taken, he couldn't treat him. Shen Mang didn't hesitate for a moment. He said to the doctor:

"He's got some medicine in a white bottle, the label's all in a foreign language; I've seen it by his hand. I didn't see him take it with my own eyes, but his hands shaking and his heart going fast and irregular, that started last month. There's a brown bottle in his schoolbag too."

When he'd said it, he bent his head and said to Ma Xiaobao: "Xiaobao, this one, I can't keep quiet for you any more."

Ma Xiaobao closed his eyes. The stretcher went out of the door.

Room 617's lights went out. At eleven, Coach Lei posted in the team group: "Ma Xiaobao's OK. Kept in overnight for observation." Underneath, a row of "Got it". Shen Mang lay on the lower bunk, his hands still trembling a little, trembling all this time; on his two fingers there still lingered Ma Xiaobao's heart, pounding beat by beat like someone hammering on a door. If, on the night of the power cut, he hadn't said that "didn't see a thing"; if, yesterday noon, when that hand was knocking the egg, he had spoken up— He turned the two things over, and turned them back, and couldn't turn up an answer. In the dark, Specs suddenly spoke: "The machine said no shock advised. That's a good thing." Old Cai said from the upper bunk, "Tonight you made one of two free throws, and you scooped a person back up off the floor." Shen Mang said nothing. He raised the still-trembling hand in front of his eyes. In the dark he couldn't see anything, but he knew it was trembling. In September he had held it up like this too, looking for those twelve invisible rivers. Tonight he wasn't looking. He just held it up and let it tremble.

---

On the twentieth he went to the Rare Books Department again and asked Granny Ge to bring out the case of *On Nourishing Life*. At the foot of the "long breath" page there was one more line in pencil, fainter than anywhere else: "Winter solstice night, sat still. Didn't see." The pencil broke off there, and there wasn't another word after it. He returned the case, drew out the first volume of the notebooks, and this time read from the first page: the leaky ladle, the fall into the valley, the goose feather; turned a page, and found an entry he hadn't read: "Autumn, aged thirteen, went south with Uncle. South of Mount Meng, above the Si River, there is an abbey." He paused on that entry a while, then slid the book back into its case.

He counted the days: the winter solstice was Tuesday.

When he returned the case, he heard Granny Ge explaining to Little Liu the librarian: when the power went off in November, the climate-control machine broke, and it had only been fixed a couple of days ago; the library said that once it was fixed someone had to stay a night to watch whether the temperature and humidity held steady; the books couldn't stand another mishap. She was seventy-two, and she'd taken the night on herself: "Tuesday. The solstice." Shen Mang, listening beside her, said he'd come and keep her company. Granny Ge looked at him over her reading glasses, and nodded.

---

The solstice was a Tuesday. In the morning Teacher Yan had changed the timetable to give the last revision class before the final; she went through it straight from the book, and as usual before the end there was a dictation: the twelve regular meridians, write the names, write the courses. At the first dictation in September, he'd handed in a blank sheet. This time he wrote out nine. One he got tangled, drawing the Hand Taiyin Lung Meridian all the way down to the foot. Beside it Teacher Yan wrote in red: "Good imagination." And at the top of the paper she wrote a mark: not high, but there. Looking at those red words, his face went green, then white. Specs looked at that line and, to be fair, said, "That's not all wrong. It's a shortcut." Shen Mang folded the paper and put it in his pocket. Nine was nine; in September it had been none.

At noon there were dumplings in Canteen One, and the queue stretched from the stairs out of the door. He sat down opposite Ahe, didn't touch his chopsticks, and started taking money out of his pocket first: a ten, a five, a one, a fifty-fen coin, two ten-fen coins. He laid them out on the table one by one in a row, then felt around in his pocket a long time and came out with a bus card, a meal card, and a ball of paper: the morning's test. "Sixteen seventy," he said. "Ten fen short." The egg debt from September had been waived on the day of the retest, and he was paying it anyway. Ahe looked down at the row of money, turned back to that line from September, and put the money into a little envelope piece by piece; the ten fen, she didn't mention. Then she wrote one word at the end of that line: "Cleared." When she'd written it she picked up a dumpling, dipped it in vinegar, and ate it. Shen Mang had known her a whole term, and she counted even how many times he went to the toilet; this was the first time he'd seen her let ten fen go.

By five in the evening it was fully dark. When Old Cai heard he was going to spend the solstice in the Rare Books Department, he sat up on the upper bunk, deadly serious: "Solstice, at the hour of zi, one yang returns. In the novels, that's the hour you open your heavenly eye." Specs said, "The first half's true. Tonight's the longest night; after today the days get longer one by one. The heavenly eye is from your novels."

He got to the Rare Books Department at eight. Granny Ge was sticking a sheet of paper on the wall: a spray of plum blossom, nine flowers, nine petals each, not one coloured in. With a red pencil she carefully coloured in the first petal of the first flower: "The Nine Nines Chart for Dispelling the Cold. From the solstice you count the nines, one petal a day, and when all eighty-one are coloured, the rivers have thawed and the willows are green." She sat down and poured two cups of hot water. Shen Mang helped her work: every hour on the hour he went to the corner to look at the machine's gauges and wrote down one number each for temperature and humidity. As he wrote, he felt a bit like Ahe.

At ten she got up to go into the inner room, and turned back at the door: "Things you can't see, don't strain to look at. Listen first."

He took down a copy of *A Study of the Eight Extraordinary Meridians* and turned to a line: "[The tunnels of the inner landscape: only one who looks inward can illuminate them.]{典=qijing-yinqiao}" The inner landscape, the landscape inside the body; only someone who looked inward could light it. But to look inward, what did you look with? He shut the book and couldn't work it out. At half past eleven he switched off the desk lamp.

It was dark without edges.

At first there was nothing. His head, though, was busy: the ball rolling that one and a half times round the rim, the "Cleared" in Ahe's notebook, Ma Xiaobao's eyes closing before the stretcher went out of the door, Specs saying "shortcut". One after another, as if someone were running a slideshow in his head. Then the slide changed: that night of the power cut in November, this same room, just this dark, and in the dark another person's panting. That night it was Ma Xiaobao who was afraid of the dark. This time, there was only him in the room.

The moment that thought came, his back went cold first. All round were bookshelves he couldn't see, behind the bookshelves walls he couldn't see, and beyond the walls, earth. The dark had weight; it pressed in layer on layer and lay on his chest. His breathing went shallow, fast.

He did as Granny Ge said: listen first.

The first thing he heard was the climate-control machine. Up, down. When he'd listened a long time, he heard that it wasn't one constant note: a little higher going up, a little lower going down, and between them a very light *tick*. The second thing was his own breathing: cool going in, warm coming out. The third he at first thought was still the machine: a very dull, very soft *thud*, not outside his ears but inside his chest, one, one, much faster than the machine.

He didn't feel his wrist, but he knew what it was. That burst of fear just now, it was all here.

He didn't try to deal with the fear. He only did as on the free-throw line: let out one long breath, and another. The *thud*, *thud*, as he breathed out, after a good while, grew looser beat by beat, slower beat by beat, like someone who had been running too hard slowly coming down to a walk. He had never so clearly watched his own heart slow down.

The dark was as dark as before, but it didn't press any more.

He listened elsewhere. His stomach was empty and dragging down: hungry. His two feet were cold, cold at the tips of the toes, though the soles were warm. There was a tight spot in his right shoulder, probably from the free throws. These things had been there all along, reporting to his brain every moment without stopping; he'd lived eighteen years, and only tonight for the first time had he listened to them one by one, like taking a roll call.

He wanted to test whether his listening inward was accurate. He felt in the dark for his phone, squinted to set one minute, and turned it face down on the table. Not touching his wrist, just listening, one, two, three... The phone buzzed: fifty-eight. He set another minute and, two fingers on his wrist, counted properly: sixty-three. Five out.

He didn't hurry to count again. First he let out two breaths, breathed those slides in his head clean away, then listened. The *thud* rose up out of his chest a little clearer. The phone buzzed: sixty-one. Fingers on the wrist: sixty-two. One out.

The first time he had been guessing. This time, he had really heard it.

He laid on his upper lip a wisp of down he'd pinched out of Old Cai's pillow, its tip just touching under his nostrils, and breathed out very slowly, very finely, so finely the breath seemed to seep out of his body bit by bit. In September, a wisp just like this had been blown out through the crack of the window with one breath. It didn't move. He breathed out again. It still didn't move.

Later, before his closed eyes there was a very fine grey, and in the grey a point of light, very small, a little above the middle, not flickering, not going out. He opened his eyes: the room was dark, and there was nothing. He shut them: it was still there. He didn't chase it, nor try to think what it was. He only listened to his heart: not fast, not slow. A person underground, and out of the dark comes a point of light from nobody knows where: the September him would have been scared into grabbing for the lamp long ago. Now he wasn't afraid at all. It was more like meeting someone he knew, in the dark.

At six twenty, the folding bed in the inner room creaked, and Granny Ge came out, went first to the corner to look at the machine's gauges, muttered "Steady", and put a tick at the very bottom of the form. On Shen Mang's upper lip that wisp of down was still lying.

Just before dawn, he wrote in his notes: "Winter solstice night, sat still. Heard it." Granny Ge saw him to the door: "Back there in the dark, your breathing was fast and shallow, and then it wasn't any more." Shen Mang was taken aback: "You weren't asleep?" "I'm seventy-two. I don't sleep deep." She pushed the door open a crack. "The one before you sat up a whole night like this too. When he came out, he didn't look as well as you." He wanted to ask who the one before was, but the door had already shut.

Outside, it wasn't yet light. At the foot of the library steps squatted a reddish-yellow bundle, a tuft of hair standing up on its forehead, who knew how long it had waited in the cold. When it saw him it stood up and shook the frost off its coat, didn't bark, came over, and pushed its head into his palm. He crouched and stroked its head. His palm was warm.

He stood up, and only then saw a black car parked by the road at the foot of the steps, headlights off, engine still running. The door opened and a man got out, about fifty, in a dark overcoat, holding a brown paper envelope. He walked to the foot of the steps and stopped, and the dog bared its teeth at him.

"Shen Mang? I'm Ma Xiaobao's dad." He set the envelope on the bottom step. "Your dad's knee, I've heard about it. Meniscus. He'll need an operation." He paused. "On Monday the drug regulators are coming to the school to question you. What you told the doctor that day in the gym: you've remembered it wrong."

Shen Mang asked, "How's Xiaobao?"

Ma Xiaobao's dad looked at his watch: "Out of hospital, I suppose. I haven't had a chance to go and see."

The envelope was thick, and the flap wasn't sealed.
