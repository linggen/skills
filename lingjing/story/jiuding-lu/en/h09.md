# Chapter Eight: One Nine-Turn Pill, a Wager on His Life / Alone on the Cliff, He Lays the Foundation

In the sixth year, Shen Xiaoman was seventeen and at the seventh layer of Qi Condensation. By day he took errand tablets from the Stewards' Hall, and tended the furnace and ground medicine for Pill-master Min in the pill house. At his waist hung a dark red copper sword called Matchstick; on his feet he wore a pair of lu-hide shoes called Tailwind. With the wind behind him, one step was ten zhang.

That spring, for the first time, Pill-master Min didn't set him grinding but tested him on the book first. Xiaoman had copied *The Gold Elixir* three times, and recited it at night in the woodshed too, till he knew it better than *The Breathing Classic*. Pill-master Min sat by the furnace, eyes never leaving its mouth, and said offhand, "'Now gold elixir, as a thing': next line."

Xiaoman said, "The longer it burns, the finer its changes. Gold goes into the fire and a hundred smeltings don't consume it; bury it, and to the end of heaven it won't rot."

"Meaning?"

"The longer a pill burns in the fire, the better."

Pill-master Min said, "Half right. If burning long were all it took, no pill-master under heaven would need to learn: set the fire going and take a nap." He tapped the furnace wall with the tongs. "What matters is the next line. *The Gold Elixir*{典=baopu-jinhuo} says gold goes into the fire and a hundred smeltings don't consume it. Before burning long, it first has to stand the burning. What can't stand it is ash after one burning; what can stand it is one turn more after each. Pills are like that, and so are ingredients." With that he tipped a bundle of sweet flag onto the bench and said, "Pick out of this bundle the ones fit for medicine."

Xiaoman squatted by the bench a long time and picked out the seven or eight thickest, longest stems, and brought them over in both hands. Pill-master Min didn't even look. From the heap he drew out a thin one, thin as a chopstick, and measured down it with his fingernail: "Within one inch, nine joints. This one is worth your whole handful." He laid the sweet flag in Xiaoman's palm. "Plants are like people: the more winters they've come through, the closer the joints. A one-year plant is a plant. One that's come through ten winters, growing in a crack in the rock, is medicine. When you look at an ingredient, first look at how many winters it's come through, then at whether it's grown thick."

From the cupboard he took a box, and in it lay a length of solomon's seal, black and shining, heavier than any Xiaoman had seen: "Qi-Gathering Pills use solomon's seal as the sovereign; everyone knows that. Half of a furnace of pills, good or bad, is in the fire, and half is in the sovereign. Change the sovereign for one with years enough on it, change the minister, assistant and envoy to match, and the pill goes up a grade." He took up a brush and wrote four lines on a sheet of yellow paper and pushed it across: Sovereign, ten-year solomon's seal; minister, nine-joint sweet flag; assistant, poria; envoy, fleeceflower root. "This recipe is called Nine-Joint Qi-Gathering. I've made it ten years, and one furnace in ten comes out good. When you're grinding, keep it in mind." With that he pushed the length of solomon's seal across too. "This piece is your grinding pay for the month."

That night in the woodshed, following the yellow paper, Xiaoman fed the Rice Bucket the solomon's seal, three of the nine-joint sweet flags he'd picked out, a handful of poria and a piece of fleeceflower root, one by one. The Rice Bucket chewed very slowly, chewed a while, burped, and the burp carried the clean smell of sweet flag, which set Xiaozheng sneezing. Just before dawn the lid sprang up and three pills rolled out, three lines each, rounder than before, brighter than before, sitting heavy in his palm, dragging down. He ate one, and the vat in his dantian rose a full span; he sat in meditation till noon the next day before he had that breath pressed solid.

That winter he passed the eighth layer. When he drew a breath in, the smoke of the three sticks of incense at the door of the grass hut on the clifftop leaned towards him.

---

Every year on the eighteenth of the tenth month, Zhou Heng didn't open his ledger all day, and didn't break the night-watch candles either; every New Year's Eve he went once to the main hall, and every time came out with the same words: "Next year, I'll come again." Xiaoman didn't ask.

Ahe planted her little strip of earth every year, and every year the ears came up mostly empty. Elder Qu had set him only one rule: "Errands, go ahead. Mount Meng tablets, you may not take. That man from the Taiyi Palace, the one who hurt you at the Si River ferry, knows which village your family is in and which door is theirs. Taking your Pa and Ma would be no use to him; what he wants is the thing on you. But he knows you'll go back to that door sooner or later, and he need only wait around there. Go back alone and you're delivering yourself to him." Errands he took as before; and every time he took a tablet and went out, the steward who called names in the Stewards' Hall gallery would give the book one more look. Once outside the mountain gate, it always felt as if a pair of eyes rested on the back of his neck; he'd look round, and there was only wind. The man from the Taiyi Palace hadn't shown himself once in five years, as if he really were waiting for him to find the cauldrons. Letters from home came twice a year, Mother's writing as small and close as ever: the roof doesn't leak now; your Pa's knees hurt on cloudy days, and on clear days too. Not one single room in the inner sect came free, and he still slept in the bed nearest the door of that room in the West Gallery. The Grand Contest was held every year, and for the first few years he didn't go up on the stage. He wasn't yet at the ninth layer, and if he won the Foundation Pill he could only carry it about, and draw the whole abbey's eyes.

Above the seventh layer, Master Chu's lecture hall added two things: spirit sense, and lightness of body.

"Spirit sense isn't the eyes, and it isn't the ears," said Master Chu. "In the *Zhuangzi*{典=zhuangzi-xinzhai}, Confucius teaches Yan Hui: 'Don't listen with the ears but with the heart; don't listen with the heart but with the qi.' The ears hear only sounds; the heart knows only what it already knows. Qi is empty, and only what's empty can hold things. Let a thin layer of the spiritual power in your dantian out, like casting a net over the river: when it touches something living, the net moves." He had the whole hall close their eyes, let a folded paper sparrow out of his sleeve to fly round the beams of the lecture hall, and told them to find it with their spirit sense.

"There are three things to remember about spirit sense." He walked up and down the aisle with his hands behind his back. "One: when you sweep someone, they know it. It's cold, like a draught of cold wind brushing the back of your neck. Two: someone higher in cultivation than you, you can't sweep through; you only sweep a fog. Three: where spirit qi is thick, there's light everywhere, and hiding a man in it is like hiding a lantern in a lantern shop." Before he'd finished speaking, a senior sister in the front row whipped round and, with a *smack*, slapped the man behind her: his spirit sense hadn't settled over the sparrow, it had settled, cold, over the back of her neck. Master Chu didn't lift his eyelids: "The first point, I've just covered."

The whole hall sat with eyes shut, brows knotted, faces red from the strain. Before Xiaoman had let his spirit sense out at all, he wrinkled his nose and sniffed twice, then once more: the paper was freshly cut, the paste was from the pot the kitchen had boiled that morning, with a little alum in it. Without opening his eyes he raised a hand and pointed up: "It's on the east beam."

The lecture hall went quiet a moment. The paper sparrow was perched on the east beam, and at his pointing it flapped and flew off to the west. Someone burst out laughing, *pff*, and a whole row laughed after him; the ones who'd been straining all that time opened their eyes and banged the desks in fury: "He smelled it!"

Master Chu came down the aisle and rapped his desk once with the ruler: "Shen Xiaoman." Xiaoman opened his eyes: "Teacher, I found it." Master Chu said, "What did I tell you to find it with?" Xiaoman said, "...Spirit sense." Master Chu said, "And what did you use?" Xiaoman said nothing more. Master Chu's goatee twitched up: "Put your nose away. One day there'll be someone standing opposite you who has no smell, and we'll see what you smell then." He turned to go back, took two steps, and without turning his head added: "Pill-master Min calls you Shen Little Dog. He wasn't wrong." The hall laughed again.

Xiaoman had no choice but to let his spirit sense out like a good boy. Seventh-layer spirit sense spread to the size of a room, a thin layer; it touched the sparrow, and the spirit sense gave a faint quiver.

"Lightness of body is something else," said Master Chu. "Put a cushion of spiritual power under your feet and you're three-tenths lighter; above the seventh layer, going with the wind, you can glide a short way. The *Baopuzi*{典=baopu-chengqiao} speaks of riding the qiao. It says when an eagle flies high, 'it only stretches its two wings straight out, never flaps them again, and goes forward of itself.' The wings are spread and still, and it goes forward by itself, because there's qi underneath holding it up. You can't be held up that high. If you can hold up your own two feet, that's enough." Above that came escaping: fire escape, earth escape, water escape, dissolving your whole self into the element, ten zhang in a breath. "That's ninth-layer business. It eats spiritual power ferociously: one vat, three escapes, and you've hit the bottom. An escape talisman is someone else sealing that move onto paper for you."

After class that day, Xiaoman tried it on the clifftop. He put a cushion of spiritual power under his soles, and one Tailwind step went two zhang further than usual. When he landed he couldn't stop, and went headfirst into the fork of the old pine, and hung there, unable to get up or down. Qin Yan happened to come up the cliff just then, and stood under the tree holding her sword and watching him a long while, then said, "You can put the cushion on, but you can't take it off?" Xiaoman said, "The teacher only taught how to put it on." Qin Yan said, "Come down first. I'll teach you how to land. Toes touch first, spiritual power comes off at the heels, same as sheathing a sword." That afternoon there was no sword bout. He jumped down out of the pine thirty-odd times.

---

In the seventh year he was at the eighth layer. What Elder Qu taught that summer, called a form, was really a sentence. Xiaoman asked him, "Master, the moment the lu drew breath to open its wings, why was it sure to come?" Elder Qu said, "However fierce a thing is, it can't stay fierce long. It spends all its strength on one roar, one ram, and when it's spent, it has to catch its breath. That breath is what you wait for." He recited a line: "The *Daodejing*{典=laozi-piaofeng} says: 'A whirlwind doesn't last the morning; a cloudburst doesn't last the day.' A gale can't blow a whole morning, a storm can't pour a whole day. When heaven and earth use all their strength they can't keep it up long, let alone a beast." He lit a candle and set it on the bluestone: "Blow on it." Xiaoman puffed out his cheeks and blew hard. The flame lay down flat along the candle, down to a line, and just wouldn't go out; he blew till he was out of breath, and the flame stood up, the same as ever. Elder Qu said, "You can't blow it out. You blow till you're out of breath, and it's still there. Settling the wind isn't standing still. It's waiting for it to finish blowing."

That year, Master Chu lectured in the hall on formations. "A formation is a spell that needs no one to hold it," he said. "A seal needs hands to pinch it, a talisman is one fewer every time you use it; a formation carves the road into the ground and buries a few spirit stones to feed it, and when the man's gone, it's still there." That ring of blue-white light on the deadly cliff in the secret realm had held Ma Xiaobao and his five fast. Whoever laid it might have been dead for hundreds of years.

"To break a formation, you must first know how a formation fools you." He turned and drew a circle on the board. "The *Sunzi*{典=sunzi-qizheng} says of war: 'The surprising and the straight give rise to each other, like a ring with no end. Who can exhaust them?' The straight is what's put out for you to see; the surprising is what's hidden. One straight, one surprising, head joined to tail, and you have a ring with no beginning. A trapping formation traps people with exactly that ring: you think you're walking forward, and the road under your feet has long since turned, and you walk and walk and end up where you started." He touched the circle with the ruler. "But a ring is joined up. Anything joined up has a joint. The place where the surprising turns into the straight, the road has been forced round. That is the formation's eye. Find it, take one step across, and the formation is broken."

Someone asked how to find it. Master Chu said, "A formation can fool the eyes, it can fool the feet, but it can't fool dead things. Work it out yourselves."

On the floor in the middle of the lecture hall he set out seven broken spirit stones and laid a small trapping formation one zhang square, and had people go in and walk it. The first one in turned on the spot for a stick of incense and sweated buckets, thinking he'd walked two li. The second turned for half a stick and was sick. When it was Xiaoman's turn, he stood at the formation's mouth and didn't hurry to step in. First he bent and picked up a stick of charcoal from the floor. Inside the formation, he walked three steps and drew a line on the brick; walked three more and drew another. Father had said: in the mountains, when a ghost walls you in, don't trust your eyes, don't trust your feet, trust the marks you leave yourself. At the seventh line he stopped. On the brick ahead there was already a line, the first one he had drawn himself; but it was on his left, and he'd been turning right the whole way. Between the two marks lay one joint between the bricks. He lifted his foot and stepped across that joint. The light on the seven spirit stones went dim all together.

Master Chu stroked his goatee and looked at him a good while: "Who taught you that?" Xiaoman said, "My Pa." Master Chu said, "Your Pa breaks formations?" Xiaoman said, "My Pa's a hunter. When you get lost in the mountains, that's how you walk." The hall roared with laughter. Master Chu didn't laugh. He bent down, picked the seven broken spirit stones up one by one, and said, "All of you, remember this clumsy method of his. The clever methods break three formations in ten. The clumsy one breaks nine."

From the eighth layer to the ninth, Elder Qu taught advancing and retreating. It was autumn. He touched the broom handle to the ground and said, "In the *Book of Changes*{典=zhouyi-xun}, the first line of the Xun hexagram opens with two words: 'Advancing, retreating.' The whole line is: 'First six: advancing, retreating; the steadfastness of a warrior is fitting.'" He wrote the words on the bluestone with the broom handle. "Advancing and retreating, back and forth, is a heart that can't make itself up. The one whose heart is made up is the warrior: one cut, all his spiritual power, with no half kept back for retreat." He pointed to a pine branch at the cliff edge as thick as a bowl's rim: "Cut it."

Xiaoman cut it. The sword went in and stuck fast in the branch and wouldn't come out: he had pressed in the greater part of a breath and kept back the lesser part, afraid that if he cut through to nothing he couldn't recover. Elder Qu said, "You kept a breath for yourself." Xiaoman pulled the sword out and cut again, and this time pressed all the spiritual power in his dantian into the sword, keeping nothing back. The branch dropped with a *crack*; the sword went through, and he went through after it, couldn't stop, and lurched two steps forward before he stood. There wasn't a drop of spiritual power left in the vat, and his legs were soft as noodles. Elder Qu said, "That's advancing and retreating." He looked down the cliff and added, in a voice so low it could barely be heard: "The sword of the Xun-four palace doesn't fear slowness, doesn't fear softness. It fears only advancing and retreating. Go halfway in and think of going back, and that cut has fallen apart before it lands." A pause. "...The sword is like that. Other things are like that too."

At the time Xiaoman didn't understand what that added sentence was about. Before the twelfth month of that year he was at the ninth layer, and between breath in and breath out you couldn't hear a seam. With a ninth-layer Matchstick, when a cut went out, a thread of sparks flicked off the sword tip, fine as the kind that pop out of a stove's firebox. He didn't know what that was, and Elder Qu didn't say.

That autumn, Pill-master Min set a low stool for him in front of the little furnace in the corner of the pill house. It was the abbey's side furnace. The furnace wall had cracked once and been plastered over with clay; its floor tapped a fine crack branching off the pill house's earth-fire vent, a fire as small as a lamp wick, and it made only three pills a firing. Pill-master Min said only one thing: "Grind your own ingredients, watch your own fire. If you scorch them, grind them up for mixing." The ingredients were the pill house's leftovers: solomon's seal and milkvetch left over from grinding, stacked bundle on bundle in the corner. The first furnace, all three scorched. The second, two scorched, and one never turned, a black lump. The fifth gave one pill, blue-white, one line, crooked and wobbly like a child's writing. He took it in both hands to show Pill-master Min. Pill-master Min picked it up, turned it once, smelled it, and said, "There's fire in it." And: "Edible." It was the longest praise he'd heard from Pill-master Min's mouth in five years. From then on, when he pushed pills into Ahe's and Sun Ergou's hands he no longer needed an excuse: the man who tended the furnace in the pill house having a few pills in hand surprised no one. Whether they came from his furnace or the Rice Bucket spat them out, nobody asked.

After that, the little furnace in the corner of the pill house had a wick-sized fire lit every night. He made them slowly, no more than three or four firings a month, and the scorched ones he ground up for mixing as before. By the twelfth month, of three pills a firing, only one scorched; when spring came, all three came out, one line each, and the lines were straight. Pill-master Min, at the main furnace, sniffed, didn't turn his head, and said, "Fire's too low." Xiaoman said, "I'm afraid of scorching." Pill-master Min said, "Afraid of scorching, and you'll be one-line all your life. Martial at head and tail, civil in the middle. You can hold the civil fire in the middle, but you don't dare put down the martial fire at the two ends." He came over, put one hand on the furnace wall and poured in a breath of spiritual power, and the wick-sized fire leapt up half a foot with a *whoosh*, and dropped back. "That big. Remember that." That firing, for the first time, Xiaoman made two lines. By the summer of the eighth year, that little furnace gave two-line pills three firings in ten; and when Pill-master Min opened the main furnace, he had Xiaoman stand at the furnace mouth to watch the fire, a job that before in the pill house only Pill-master Min had done himself. The pill-house furnaces belonged to the abbey, and what he made there was craft; back in the woodshed, the pills he ate himself he still left to the Rice Bucket.

---

That year in the twelfth month, Shopkeeper Qian carried his board up the mountain as usual and set up his stall under the old locust tree. The top few lines of the board were still inner sect; the very bottom line said: **Shen Xiaoman: fifty to one.**

Someone asked, "He's ninth layer and still fifty to one?" Shopkeeper Qian clicked his abacus and said, "A mixed spirit root taking first place: not in three hundred years." Another click. "Four years he hasn't been on the stage. The Shen Xiaoman the whole abbey remembers is the fourth-layer one, covered in blood, hanging in a tree. Who's seen him fight at the ninth? What nobody's seen, I write up big."

Ahe squeezed up to the scale pan and put down one copper. Shopkeeper Qian raised his eyelids: "Miss, you bet on him once five years ago." Ahe said, "And lost." Shopkeeper Qian said, "Betting again?" Ahe said, "He hasn't been on the stage in four years. I had nowhere to bet." She took the slip, tucked it away, and left. That year, the bets on Shen Xiaoman came to one copper in all.

On the eighth of the twelfth month in the fifth year, the wind was from the north. In his first bout Xiaoman drew a fire-type eighth-layer man from the inner sect. He went up on the stage, and the first thing he did was not look at the man but at the flag at the edge of the training ground: it was pulling south. Wind first, then deer; with no one behind him to say it, he said it to himself. He stood on the bluestone slab on the north side and didn't budge. The senior brother's fireballs came one after another, and he caught them with earth walls, one fireball, one wall shattered, the stage full of smoke; and the smoke went with the north wind, all of it falling south, falling into the senior brother's own eyes. The senior brother coughed and stepped back half a step, coughing up tears and snot by the handful, and by the seventh cough he'd backed to the stage edge. Only then did Xiaoman walk over, put out a hand, and push him off.

The three bouts in the middle, one he won on his feet, one on counting, one because the man opposite got rattled on his own, and not one of them was pretty. His opponent in the final was a perfected ninth-layer man from the inner sect who went all out from the moment he stepped up, metal blades one after another. What Xiaoman could dodge, he dodged; what he couldn't, he followed. Following the wind, his sword clung to the metal blade and led it aside, half its force spent, and the other half still ploughed him back half a foot, the thick earth under his feet holding him steady. The few he couldn't lead aside, he gritted his teeth and took. When the stick of incense was half burned, his left side had a gash under the ribs, and blood ran down his trouser leg onto the stage. The senior brother sent his metal blades breath by breath: one blade, one breath. Just like Lu Fang that year. What he waited for was the breath. He waited till the eleventh blade, when all the senior brother's spiritual power was in the blade, and the light on his chest thinned for an instant, and Xiaoman's Matchstick went in: not into the man, into that breath. The sword point rested against the front of the senior brother's robe, and stopped. The senior brother looked down at the sword point, looked a long time, let go of the seal he'd half made, and said, "I yield." The steward turned the gong stick twice in his hand: "Shen Xiaoman wins."

This time there were no pig dragonflies, and no mirrors. Five bouts, fought one by one, properly, and the people below the stage all said it was dull. Only Xiaoman knew that if that cut in the final had come an instant later, the one backing off the edge would have been him. The gash under his ribs took seven stitches and half a month to heal. Zhou Heng turned the blue-cloth register to a new page, took up his brush and wrote the three characters "Shen Xiaoman", and the brush stopped a moment: on three hundred years of the register, the first time it had written the name of a mixed spirit root. The Abbot put the jade box in his hand, the seal stamped with the great seal.

Shopkeeper Qian was packing up his stall under the old locust tree. Ahe laid the slip on the scale pan and said not a word. Shopkeeper Qian sighed, untied his cloth bag and counted coppers into the scale pan one by one: "One, two, three..." He counted very slowly, and with each copper the corners of his mouth sagged a shade further. By twenty, seven or eight people had gathered round; by forty, there was a ring of them, and his fat face had crumpled into a bitter gourd. At fifty, Shopkeeper Qian's hand stopped at the mouth of the bag a moment, then took out one more copper and set it on top: "The stake."

Ahe gathered the fifty-one coppers into her handkerchief, took out her little notebook and turned to the page from five years before. There was only one line on that page: **One copper. Interest separately.** She added a stroke underneath.

Shopkeeper Qian swung the board onto his shoulder, walked two steps, then turned back and jerked his chin at the bow on Xiaoman's back: "That wood: found out its name yet?" For five years he had carried his board up the mountain every twelfth month, and every time he saw Xiaoman this was the first thing he said. Xiaoman said, "No." Shopkeeper Qian said, "No hurry."

That night in the woodshed Xiaoman broke the seal. When the lid came open the smell of medicine was bitter and rank: one line, with two or three black spots at the edge of the line, one-turn, low grade. The Rice Bucket took one sniff; the little beast on its lid turned its nostrils towards him and blew out a little puff of black smoke, *pff*, and the lid shut with a *snap* and ignored him after that. He begged half a bowl of rice water, stuck the seal back on, and put it, jade box and all, in his storage pouch: the one [Foundation Pill]{注=foundation-pill} in all the world whose origin he could explain out loud.

The next day he passed the north-west corner of the main hall just as Pill-master Min came out of the pill house, and the two came face to face. Five years, with a furnace between them, one watching the fire and one watching him. Pill-master Min's nose twitched (a man who had kept a furnace thirty years had a nose sharper than his eyes) and his gaze rested a moment on the storage pouch at Xiaoman's waist. He said nothing, and walked on.

In those five years, by the Zhang River, every year on the eighteenth of the tenth month, a boat was pushed out, and in the boat sat a girl in a wedding dress. Five years, five boats. The Sunken Cauldron Abbey was ten days' road from the Zhang, and nobody in the abbey knew their names. Zhou Heng knew which day it was.

---

At the layer check in the twelfth month of the seventh year, Sun Ergou was at the fourth layer. A layer-zero mixed spirit root, of whom Master Chu had once said drawing in qi would take five years at least; in these eight years Xiaoman had every so often pushed an oilpaper packet under his desk, and he'd eaten them one by one, eaten one and sat half a month, and not wasted one. That day Master Chu held his wrist twice as long as anyone else's before he gave out the two words. Zhou Heng opened the inner sect's register and added the three characters "Sun Ergou". After New Year he moved into the West Gallery, stood in front of Xiaoman's bed for a long time and said, "S-senior brother Shen, from now on I-I'm inner sect too." When he'd said it, he cried again.

In the eighth year, as soon as the autumn wind rose, a date was added under the third line of the board on the lecture-hall door. The Foundation Establishment lecture was given only once a season, and only those above the seventh layer were allowed in; and it wasn't Master Chu who taught it, but Martial Uncle Ning of the Scripture Library.

Martial Uncle Ning looked about sixty, thin and tall, his back a little stooped, his grey robe washed white. His left sleeve was empty, tied off with a hemp cord. Thirty years ago he had tried to break through to Core Formation and failed, and lost an arm, though he kept his whole perfected Foundation Establishment cultivation. Since then he had kept the Scripture Library and wouldn't say a handful of words in a year. This one lecture, though, nobody in the abbey could teach better than him: how Foundation Establishment is reached, he knew; and how it isn't, he knew too.

At this lecture not one person in the hall dozed off. Martial Uncle Ning put a brick on the lectern with his right hand, but didn't speak of the brick first. He opened Wu Chongxu's *True Principles of Heavenly Immortality*{典=wu-zhuji} and read out: "To lay means to accumulate and increase by degrees. The foundation is the root of cultivating the yang spirit, the place where spirit is settled and breath is stilled." When he'd finished, he saw that every eye in the hall had glazed over, and patted the brick: "Put plainly, it's building a house. Qi Condensation is carrying earth onto the plot, and however much you carry, it's only a heap of loose earth. Foundation Establishment is tamping that heap down solid. First, you have to carry enough earth. The vat in your dantian: when one breath in makes one breath spill out, and nothing more will go in, then it's enough. You call it 'the peak of the ninth layer'; in the register it's written in four characters: ninth layer, perfected. Anyone not there yet, eating a Foundation Pill is eating it for nothing."

Martial Uncle Ning took a handful of soil from the flowerpot on the windowsill, scattered it beside the brick, and said to the front row: "Ma Xiaobao. You come."

Those years hadn't gone very smoothly for Ma Xiaobao. The Ma family had sent pills and medicine up the mountain for five years, and he had eaten his way up layer by layer to the seventh, his face blotch upon blotch; still eleven osmanthus cakes a day. On the day they came out of the secret realm, he'd said Laonian "climbed himself"; and ever since, he slept with the lamp lit. Now he set his osmanthus cake on the desk, went up, and slapped his palm down on the loose soil, and the soil flew all over the lectern; then he slapped his palm on the brick, and the brick didn't stir, but his palm went red all over.

Martial Uncle Ning said, "Eating plenty of pills is carrying plenty of earth. Earth that isn't tamped scatters at one slap. And what scatters is you."

The whole hall wanted to laugh, and not one dared laugh aloud. Martial Uncle Ning counted on the fingers of his one hand: one Foundation Pill awarded a year, one-turn, low grade. "A pill-master in the human world who makes one furnace of five-turn pills ought to pack his things and get ready to ascend." In the back row, Xiaoman pressed his heart through his clothes. "In the three hundred years since this abbey was founded, some ten thousand people have come through the mountain gate, and those who reached Foundation Establishment: about a hundred." Having got that far, he opened an old book, looked at it a long while, and murmured: "...The first hundred years took half of them."

Nobody in the hall heard anything important in that. But Xiaoman heard it: the first hundred years, half; the next two hundred, half. Like a bowl, and the rice ladled out of it less every year.

Martial Uncle Ning shut the old book, his empty sleeve swung, and he said, "So a Foundation Pill isn't a reward. It's a wager."

The word "wager" had just fallen. Ma Xiaobao was still standing at the edge of the lectern, the hand he'd slapped red clenched into a fist. He looked once towards the back row, just once, then drew his eyes back, picked up his osmanthus cake from the desk, and went back to his seat. In these five years, the words he'd said to Xiaoman came to fewer than ten.

After class Xiaoman sat a while on the steps of the lecture hall, his hand pressed to his belly. The vat Martial Uncle Ning spoke of, he had felt every day this month: one breath of spirit qi in, and one spilled out, and no more would go in. Ninth layer, perfected: he had reached it already. What was left was that one word, "wager".

Just then a Mount Meng tablet went up on the wall of the Stewards' Hall.

The tablet had come on from the county, and it was written long: Black Pine Ridge on Mount Meng: since summer began, woodcutters, pedlars, and men of the Ma household, seven people gone. The Ma family's dogs put their tails between their legs at the broken stele, and not one will go in. Day and night, people at the mouth of the ridge hear a baby crying. Commission: thirty taels of silver, paid by the Ma family. Underneath, a line of small writing: Qi Condensation disciples to look and report back. Not to go deep.

Xiaoman stood in front of that tablet a long time. Steward Deng looked up from the threshold: "Mount Meng. You're a Mount Meng man?" Xiaoman said, "Shi'ao Village. Under Black Pine Ridge." Steward Deng looked at the bow on his back and said, "What the tablet says is look." Xiaoman took the tablet down and said, "I'll show it to my master first."

Elder Qu took the tablet on the clifftop and looked at it a long time, so long that a length of ash broke off in the incense burner. He gave the tablet back to Xiaoman and said, "Go." And: "When you get to the village entrance, don't go into the village."

On the way down the mountain he went first to Shopkeeper Qian's shop as usual, and sold two pills. The two of them, Xiaoman and Ahe, walked for three days. Sun Ergou didn't come. The thing on that tablet had eaten seven people, he was only at the fourth layer, and going would be throwing himself away; Xiaoman didn't say it out loud, and Sun Ergou said it first himself. At the road into the mountains, a woodcutter squatted on his own threshold and watched them go by without a word; his brother was the first of the seven. Ahe hardly spoke the whole way. Every so often she took out the little notebook from inside her robe and glanced at it. Only at the mouth of the ridge did she shut it and say, "That thing on the tablet that cries day and night: I've looked it up. It's in the book." Xiaoman said, "What is it?" Ahe said, "The [heyu]{注=heyu}. From the Eastern Mountains in the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*{典=shanhai-heyu}: shaped like a hog with a human face, its voice like a baby's, it eats people." After Spirit Herb Valley, she had read the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* from beginning to end, and written every man-eating thing in her notebook.

The broken stele still stood at the mouth of the ridge. The stones round it were all grown over with moss, but not one bit grew on it; it was clean, as if someone wiped it every day. Only two characters were left on it, perfectly clear: Do Not Enter. Xiaoman stood before the stele a moment. Seven years ago, Father had stopped at this "Do Not" too. He didn't stop long. He stepped across. Ahe stepped across after him.

The trees in the forest were as tall as ever, the bark still cold, the pine needles so thick they made no sound. Xiaoman followed the marks from seven years ago: the knife cuts had grown into the bark, but he could still read them, and the first crooked one still didn't count. At the twenty-seventh, the narrow slope that hugged the cliff, he stopped and looked down the cliff once. At the foot of the cliff was that valley.

The crying started at dusk. First one cry, far off; then nearer, cry after cry, drawn out long, a crying that made your heart ache. Ahe's hand clenched in her sleeve: "...It really does sound like a baby." Xiaoman said, "That's no baby." He could hear it: a baby's crying comes out of its chest, and this was scraped out of a throat. Ahe said, "It's in the valley. It's waiting for someone to come down."

They didn't go down. Ahe looked at the cliff a long time and said, "It can come up; we can't go down. If we go down, we're on its ground." At the narrowest point of the narrow slope, cliff wall on one side and a pine tree on the other, Xiaoman made a seal and raised an earth wall in the middle, chest high, blocking the path. Ahe squatted two steps behind the wall, her right hand's seal long since ready, fingertips pointing at the pine roots. Xiaoman went up the crooked-necked pine at the top of the slope, the short flying sword held between his fingers, Matchstick on his back. Ahe said, "When it comes up, it hits the wall first. The wall stops it once, I tangle it once, and you—" Xiaoman said, "I watch its spiritual power." From up the tree he added, "When the wall cracks, you get back." Ahe didn't agree to that.

When it was full dark, the crying stopped. It stopped for a long time. Then, at the foot of the cliff, light rose.

It was the grass in the valley. Xiaoman remembered that grass: at every step the blades lit up, a ring of faint light spreading out from your foot. Now ring after ring of light rose from the valley floor, each nearer than the last, like someone walking towards them across water. Whatever was treading in that light, they couldn't see; but with every step it took, a patch of the valley lit up. Ahe said quietly, "Count them." By the time Xiaoman counted the forty-first ring, the light had reached the foot of the cliff.

It came up. The cliff face was almost sheer, but it went as if on level ground, four hooves clawing into the cracks in the rock, coming up bound by bound. A hog, bigger than an ox, all yellow bristle, its tail bright red, dragging behind it like a flame. But that face: that face was a man's. Eyebrows, eyes, nose, mouth, not one missing and not one right; each of them looked like something you'd seen somewhere, and all together they were no one's face at all. It climbed up onto the narrow slope, smelled the people behind the earth wall, opened its mouth, and cried.

Hearing that cry up close, Ahe's fingertips trembled once, but the seal held. The human face charged at the earth wall, and with a *boom* the wall split down the middle; across the half wall, that face was less than three feet from her. She didn't back away. With that very charge the vines came out from under the pine roots, green vine after green vine boring out of the cracks in the rock, winding round its hind legs, winding round its tail, hauling back. It strained, and a vine broke; one broke, and two more bored out. And in the middle of that straining it cried again.

Xiaoman's seal was long since made. The flying sword went first for its eyes. It turned its head aside, and the sword grazed past that human face without leaving even a white mark. This thing's hide was very thick, as if wrapped in a layer of something. He called the flying sword back, and didn't send it a second time. He watched its spiritual power.

He saw it: when the crying scraped out of its throat, all the spiritual power in its body surged into that one cry, and the layer of light on its neck thinned for an instant. Gaps in stone and gaps in trees he had watched for years; a gap in a living thing, this was the first time he'd seen one.

Xun is entering.

The third time it opened its mouth, Xiaoman came down out of the tree. Not jumping: falling, going with that cry, like a leaf going with the wind. Matchstick came out of its sheath, three feet of dark red copper, and he pressed all the spiritual power in his dantian into the sword, keeping nothing back. Advancing and retreating: a whole autumn cutting pine branches on the clifftop, and this was the first time he'd turned it on something that ate people. The sword point aimed under the human face, at the place the crying came out. In the instant that layer of light thinned, the sword went in, went in without a sound, like wind boring through the crack of a door.

It didn't cry again. It flung its neck and flung Xiaoman off, sword and all, against the cliff wall, and he rolled down the slope. By the time he got up, it was still on the narrow slope with his sword stuck under its neck, turning in circles, charging the half earth wall one moment and the vines the next, like a blinded ox. Ahe was pressed flat against the cliff wall, her hand still holding the seal, fingertips on the pine roots, her face white as paper. Xiaoman shouted, "Run up the slope, quick!" Ahe climbed towards the top of the slope. It turned round and went for her.

Xiaoman didn't chase it. He stood on the row of stones at the very outer edge of the narrow slope, his back to the drop, and looked at the wind once. The wind was pouring from the ridge down into the valley, and hadn't stopped all night. He poured what little spiritual power was left in his dantian into his heels, and took a step back.

The two "gills" at his heels opened with a *pff*, and his whole body left the cliff, back first, sliding down the face into the valley. Ten zhang, and he landed on a rock halfway down the face; another step back, another ten zhang; three steps, and he landed on the glowing grass of the valley floor, his dantian so empty he could hardly stand. It saw. It came down off the cliff, not climbing but leaping, four hooves clawing the cracks, bound after bound, coming after him. The valley was its ground, and on its own ground it meant to kill him. The grass in the valley lit up ring by ring, going down towards the valley floor, the rings coming slower the brighter they grew.

Xiaoman took out a Qi-Return Pill, bit it to pieces and held it under his tongue, waited till that breath of spiritual power had poured back most of a vat, and only then stood straight and walked into the valley. It was ahead of him, walking slower than he was; at the deepest part, the ring of light stopped, glowed a long time, and slowly went dark. The valley was as quiet as seven years ago. He knew the way.

The shallow hollow on the valley floor, where seven years ago a little fox with eight broken tails had curled up: now there lay across it a hog bigger than an ox, a human face turned up to the sky, Matchstick's hilt sticking out from under its neck. White bones were heaped at the edge of the hollow; Xiaoman didn't look at them closely. He pulled out the sword and wiped it with grass.

An empty den always gets something moving in. That was the only thought in his head. He cut out one of its tusks, put it away, and climbed back up.

Up on the cliff, Ahe sat between two heaps of broken earth, waiting for him. She had him turn round, held one hand over his back, and sent blue light from her fingertips into it, thread by thread. There was a film of sweat on her forehead, and her hand hung there without moving, as it hung over the three grain stalks at the end of her strip. The big black-and-blue patch on his back healed in the time it takes to drink a cup of tea.

At dawn the two of them each ate a Spring-Return Pill: the Rice Bucket's.

At dawn they came down off the ridge by the western spur. Below the spur was Shi'ao Village, seventeen households, seventeen threads of cooking smoke. Xiaoman stopped on the spur. On the threshing floor was a man limping along with two water buckets on a pole towards the house, three steps and a rest. He watched the man go in at that door. The door's pivot had been hung crooked, and you had to lift it a little to open or shut it; his Pa lifted it. The door shut. Ahe, beside him, was looking at another door: Old Zhou's fence, with no one behind it. Neither of them went down. Ahe said, "Aren't you going to go and see?" Xiaoman said, "No. The man from the Taiyi Palace knows which door is my family's, and he's waiting round here for me. If I go down alone I'm delivering myself to him. Master said: when you get to the village entrance, don't go into the village." He pulled his bow tighter onto his back. Ahe looked once more at that fence, and said, "Then I won't go back either."

Back at the abbey, Steward Deng weighed the tusk in his hand on the counter, then looked at the two Qi Condensation disciples, one with half his robe torn off, one still white in the face, and after a long while said, "What the tablet says is look and report back." Xiaoman said, "We had a good look. We're back to report." Steward Deng turned the tablet over, took up his brush and wrote "Done" on the back; thought a moment, and nailed a little board underneath with two names on it. No line drawn. Thirty taels of silver came to nine spirit stones in the abbey's reckoning, four each; the extra one, the two of them took down the mountain and bought Sun Ergou a packet of the roast chestnuts he loved.

In the eighth month, Elder Qu had him come up to the clifftop.

That time Elder Qu taught him the Xun four a whole afternoon, going through all four forms from the beginning: settling the wind, following the wind, entering the gap, advancing and retreating. Elder Qu showed the moves with the broom handle and Xiaoman followed along with Matchstick. Halfway through, Elder Qu stopped and asked him, "When is the wind hardest to deal with?" Xiaoman thought and said, "When it's blowing a gale." Elder Qu shook his head and said, "When you don't know which way it's going to blow."

When the sun was going west, Elder Qu had him sweep up the fallen leaves on the clifftop the way he had on the day he became his disciple.

Xiaoman drew the warmth in his dantian into the sword and gave it a flick. The fallen leaves did rise, and arranged themselves in the air three by three, nine squares in all, the middle square empty; then they fell, thick and fast, and fell into a character: Xu, the Xu of Xu Province. That character was missing its middle stroke too.

Xiaoman looked at the character and his face burned. His five doors were all open, all five elements complete, metal, wood, water, fire and earth, not one missing, and yet that one stroke he couldn't fill for the life of him.

But Elder Qu said nothing. He only crouched and traced the place where the stroke was missing, lightly, with his finger, traced it several times, and then said: "...Missing in the same place as mine." After a while he said, "No hurry. You haven't laid your foundation yet." He swept the fallen leaves together with the broom handle and said, "The *Daodejing*{典=laozi-hebao} has two lines: 'A tree you can't get your arms round grows from a tip as fine as a hair; a terrace nine storeys high rises from a heap of earth.' These nine layers of yours were heaped up errand by errand, sword form by sword form. Slowly heaped. But they won't fall down."

The sun went down. Elder Qu glanced at him and said, "Ninth layer. Full." Xiaoman nodded and held up nine fingers to show his master.

Elder Qu didn't look at his fingers. He looked at the white line of the Si River below the cliff, looked a long time, and said, "That time at the ferry, you came up on your hands and knees." Xiaoman said, "Yes. I crawled up." Elder Qu said, "Qi Condensation legs can't outrun a Foundation Establishment sword." And: "Have you got the *Yellow Court* by heart?" Xiaoman nodded. Elder Qu said, "What you've learned is the Outer Scenery. The Inner Scenery, once you've laid your foundation, you'll see for yourself." And: "When are you going to break through?" Xiaoman said, "The third of the ninth month." Elder Qu said, "Why the third?" Xiaoman said, "On that day I'll be six days short of six years through the gate. I want to do it before the six years are up." Elder Qu said, "Why the hurry?" Xiaoman thought, and said honestly, "...So I can hurry back and tell my Ma."

That was half the truth. The other half he didn't say: that line in Zhou Heng's old ledger on that New Year's Eve: the eighteenth of the tenth month, the Zhang River, Sister. And what the Abbot had said: whoever goes has to have Foundation Establishment. Count back ten days' road from the eighteenth of the tenth month and it was the eighth of the tenth; once he'd laid his foundation he wanted a month to steady it before setting out, and counting back again brought it to the start of the ninth month. The third would catch both ends.

Elder Qu didn't laugh. He looked at the sea of cloud below the cliff, looked a long time, and then said, "Which pill will you use?"

Xiaoman's heart gave a lurch, but his mouth answered very quickly: "The abbey's."

The moment the words were out, he knew it was wrong. Elder Qu's hand, which had been resting on his knee, slowly clenched, clenched till the knuckles went white. A wind rose on the clifftop, and the smoke of the three sticks of incense at the door of the grass hut all leaned the same way.

Elder Qu said, "Sit down." Xiaoman was already sitting, but somehow he sat up straighter.

Elder Qu said, "More than forty years ago, I took a disciple." Xiaoman made no sound.

Elder Qu said, "He was from Xu Province, like you. His family was poor, like you. When he came through the mountain gate, climbing the stone steps, at the hundred and sixtieth step his legs gave out, and he sat there panting, panted for a stick of incense, then stood up and went on climbing. That year he was the only one who sat down and got up again. I saw it from the three hundredth step, and I remembered him. The first thing I ever said to him was, 'That rock on the stream is very slippery.'"

"He'd read books, the son of a poor scholar in town; he could keep accounts, he had a careful mind, but his legs weren't steady. The day he came up, his feet were caked in mud, and when he reached the topmost step he bent down and scraped his soles clean before he stepped onto it. Of three thousand-odd children, he was the first. In his first year through the gate, at the sect's Grand Contest, an outer-sect disciple, he took first place and won a Foundation Pill, just like yours: one-turn, low grade, jade box, seal. I took him on, taught him the sword, taught him seven years. In seven years he trained to the ninth layer, perfected."

"In seven years he swept the steps for me three hundred times," said Elder Qu. "I told him to sweep, and he swept."

"He was slow at the sword," said Elder Qu. "He trained it line by line. The Xun-four palace he trained three years, till he could sweep up nine squares of fallen leaves and have them fall into the character Xu, missing the middle stroke, the same as mine. He said, Master, that stroke, I'll fill it in once I've laid my foundation."

"In the winter of the sixth year he was at the eighth layer, just about to reach the ninth. Someone in town sent a letter up the mountain: his father was ill, and badly," said Elder Qu. "He brought the letter and asked me whether he should go back. I said, the ninth layer's right in front of you; break off this breath and you'll have to train another year. Break through first and then go back. It's only a month there and back."

"He listened to me, and didn't go down the mountain," said Elder Qu. "In the twelfth month he broke through to the ninth layer. The second letter came too: his father was already buried. He read the letter to the end, folded it and put it inside his robe, said nothing, and the next day came to sweep the steps as usual."

The clifftop was very quiet. The three sticks of incense had burned down a length, and the ash fell into the burner without a sound.

"In the seventh year he was at the ninth layer, perfected, and he ate the pill," said Elder Qu. "He tried all night. At dawn I went into the room to see him. He was sitting there whole and sound, a few black spots come up on his face, pill poison, which a few months of meditation would clear; not a scratch on him, the ninth layer still the ninth layer. Only he hadn't laid his foundation."

"I told him, never mind, a pill only adds a tenth more chance anyway; next year at the contest go and win another. He shook his head and said, 'Master, ten more pills would be the same. Last night, at the very last step, I saw my father. He was lying on the kang, his face towards the door, waiting for me to come back. I can't get past him.'" Xiaoman's throat tightened. He wanted to say something, and couldn't get out a single word.

Elder Qu said, "To cultivate immortality, first cultivate the heart. Qi Condensation runs on pills and hard work, but the gate of Foundation Establishment checks your heart first. When you break through, all your spiritual power has to be twisted into one strand and rammed upwards. If there's one thing in your heart you can't get past, there's a crack in your heart, and when the spiritual power hits the gate it all leaks away through that crack. The *Baopuzi*{典=baopu-jishan} says: 'If your store of good deeds is not yet full, though you take the medicine of immortals, it will do you no good.'"

He stopped a moment, then went on: "The book says it's a matter of 'good'. I've lived a good many years, and from what I've seen, it isn't all like that. Outside these mountains there are plenty who kill without blinking, and they lay their foundation and form their core all the same. They don't feel they owe anyone anything, so there's no crack in their hearts. The ones who get stuck at this gate are precisely people like Zi'an."

"He sat on the clifftop three days," said Elder Qu. "On the fourth day he came and told me he was going down the mountain. I asked him what for. He said, to go back to town and take over his father's village school and teach children to keep accounts; his father had taught all his life, and he'd never once stood in for him for a day. He said, whenever this debt is paid off, that's when he'd come back." When he got to here, the corner of Elder Qu's mouth actually moved, as if he meant to smile, but he didn't. "When he left, all he took was an old robe, and the three opening pages of the *He-Luo Sword Formula* he'd copied himself. The bamboo slips stayed in the abbey; he copied them stroke by stroke onto paper and took that. He said he'd have no use for a sword in town, but those nine numbers were pretty, and he'd take them to teach the children sums: fifteen across, fifteen down, and the children would like it." Xiaoman's hands slowly clenched on his knees.

Elder Qu said, "When he had nothing to do, he liked reciting that line in the *Zhuangzi* about breathing out and in, for fun. I said, what do you recite that for? That line of Zhuangzi's mocks people, mocks the ones who breathe out and in every day just to live a few more years. He said he knew. He was mocking himself."

By now Xiaoman understood everything. He remembered Mother under the oil lamp, her finger on those eight characters in *The Breathing Classic*, stopping, and saying, "This line... your grandfather used to recite it." He remembered Mother laying out nine marked chestnuts in three rows: "When your grandfather taught me sums, the first thing he taught me wasn't adding and taking away, it was this." He remembered Mother saying, "That book, afterwards I don't know where it went." He remembered what she said before she blew out the lamp: "Your grandfather said a person has to go upward in this life."

So that book had never been a book. It was three pages Grandfather had copied himself, it was a sword, it was a step a man hadn't finished walking.

Elder Qu stood up and went into the grass hut, and after a while came out holding a sheet of red paper, folded into a neat square, the corners gone yellow. He laid the red paper on the bluestone and unfolded it. It was a discipleship card, exactly like the one Xiaoman had written, except that the square for the name had been soaked through with water and the ink had run, run into a blot, and not one character could be read.

"The day he left, it was raining," said Elder Qu. "He didn't take this card. He left it on the bluestone by the door. By the time I came out, it was already wet through." After a pause he added, "That day I went to the stewards' office and struck out his line in the contest register. My sleeve was wet, and it dripped on the paper."

Xiaoman looked at that blot of run ink for a long time. He thought of Zhou Heng's blue-cloth register, the dead-straight line struck through the first-place entry, and the name blurred under it. His grandfather's name had run in both places.

"Du Zi'an," said Elder Qu. "His name was Du Zi'an."

Mother had never told him what Grandfather was called. Xiaoman said the three characters over once in his mouth, and kept them.

Elder Qu said, "I always thought he'd died out there somewhere. All these years, not one letter." He turned his head and looked at Xiaoman. "That day you recited the rhyme without looking at the bamboo slips, and got every word right, so I asked you: who is your mother."

Xiaoman opened his mouth, his voice terribly hoarse: "...My Ma is the daughter of the schoolmaster in town."

Elder Qu said nothing. Xiaoman went on, "My grandfather... died before I was born. Taught all his life." And: "Ma says he taught one boy who passed the exams and went to the county town and never came back to see him."

Elder Qu said, "How did he die?" Xiaoman said, "Ma says it was a sudden illness. The day before he was still teaching in the schoolroom, and in the night he was gone." Elder Qu said, "He went down the mountain at twenty. Before you were born, he was only just past forty." He said the two numbers over once, and said slowly, "A man at the ninth layer of Qi Condensation can live to a hundred and twenty. He doesn't die of a sudden illness." Xiaoman froze. This, he had never once thought about.

On the clifftop the wind had stopped. Elder Qu sat there without moving, like a stone that had stood on the clifftop for many years. After a long, long time he put out his hand and pressed it once on Xiaoman's shoulder, pressed it hard. Then he stood up, took the broom from the corner by the wall, and walked off down the mountain. After a few steps he stopped with his back to Xiaoman, and said:

"That pill. Eat it."

Xiaoman looked up. Elder Qu said, "The pill wasn't wrong, and it wasn't wrong back then. What was wrong was what I said: 'break through first and then go back.' Before you eat it, turn over the things in your heart one by one and look at them. If there's any one you can't get past, go and settle it first, then come back and eat it." He stopped a moment, and went on: "The time at Mount Meng, I was the one who told you not to go into the village. I stopped Zi'an once back then, and this year I've stopped you once again. It's the same mistake. Before you lay your foundation, go home once. Cultivators call this cutting off the mortal world. When I was young I thought cutting off meant one stroke, two pieces, and never looking back. After Zi'an left I slowly understood it isn't like that. It's going back, sitting by your Ma's stove, eating the meal she makes to the last bite, saying all the things you've held in your belly these years, and letting her take a good look at you. When everything that should be said is said, and everything that should be seen is seen, then however far you go after, your heart is steady, and you won't need to look back three times for every step. I'll go with you." When he'd said all this, he shouldered the broom and walked down the mountain, step by step.

That night Xiaoman lay on his bed in the West Gallery with his eyes open. The voice in the wooden tablet hadn't spoken of its own accord for a long time, but now it did: "The old man's right this time. The pill only pushes the door open for you. The step inside the door, you have to take yourself. If there's anything weighing on your heart, turn it over tonight and look at it. Fear counts as one." After that there was no more sound.

Xiaoman turned over. The senior brother past forty on the bed opposite lay with his face to the wall, not yet asleep. This senior brother had stopped at the ninth layer, perfected, for eleven years. Everyone in the West Gallery knew his story: his first Foundation Pill, he ate, and didn't lay his foundation; four years later he traded his life on red tablets for a second, and again didn't lay it. For a third, he'd saved up enough spirit stones three years ago, and never went to buy it. When people asked, he said, "Wait a bit longer." Xiaoman had heard it before and taken it for idle talk. Only tonight did he understand: what the senior brother was waiting for wasn't a pill. The senior brother was afraid. Afraid that the third time he sat down, he still wouldn't lay it.

He pressed his hand on his own heart; it was beating faster than usual. A man like Grandfather, ninth layer, perfected, pill eaten, hadn't got through; the senior brother opposite had eaten two, and hadn't got through. For the first time a thought came up in him: what if I can't get through either? Once that thought had come up, he couldn't push it down again.

The next evening, Elder Qu laid the broom across the edge of the cliff and told Xiaoman to stand on it. Only then did Xiaoman learn that this broom his master had swept the steps with for fifty-six years could fly. From the Sunken Cauldron Abbey to Mount Meng, he had walked it once, and it took three days; the broom carried master and disciple both in the time it takes to eat a meal. Just as it was getting dark, the two of them came down on the western spur. Below the spur was Shi'ao Village, seventeen households, seventeen threads of cooking smoke. Elder Qu planted the broom on the ground and said, "I'll stand on this spur. You go down and stay a night, and come back at daybreak." Xiaoman said, "Master, what if the man from the Taiyi Palace comes?" Elder Qu said, "If he comes, he'll have to get past this broom of mine first."

Xiaoman went down off the spur and walked up to that door. The pivot was still crooked; he lifted it a little and pushed the door open. His Pa was sitting by the stove rubbing his knees. He looked up and saw him, and his hand stopped on his knee and didn't move for a long time. The bowl in his Ma's hand dropped to the floor, didn't break, and spun twice on the ground. The year he left he was twelve and came up to his Ma's shoulder; now his Ma had to tilt her face up to see his eyes.

That night the three of them sat in the kitchen, and none of them went to bed. His Ma felt him over from head to foot and asked whether he got enough to eat on the mountain, whether he was cold in winter. His Pa didn't say much. He only took the bow from his back and ran his hand down the string from top to bottom, and said, "This string wants changing." The roof really didn't leak now; the row of basins in the corner that used to catch the rain had all gone to pickling salted vegetables.

In the small hours, when his Pa's snoring had come round for the third time, Xiaoman told his Ma about Grandfather, piece by piece: that Grandfather was called Du Zi'an, that as a young man he had gone up to the Sunken Cauldron Abbey and been his master's disciple; that the old book which couldn't be found afterwards was three pages of sword formula Grandfather had copied himself; that the reason Grandfather never laid his foundation was that when Grandfather's father was gravely ill, he was up on the mountain and didn't come back. When his Ma had heard it all, she said nothing for a long time. Then she said, "No wonder. Every Qingming your grandfather went to sweep the grave, and he'd kneel in front of it half a day, and whoever went to pull him up, he wouldn't get up." And: "When he taught me those nine numbers, he said, say them over and your heart won't be in a muddle. So he was saying that to himself."

Xiaoman said, "Ma, in a few days I'm going to lay my foundation. Once I've laid it I have to go on a long journey, and I won't be back for a year or two. The road ahead goes further and further, and the days I can come back will only get fewer. Master had me come back this once so I could settle what's tugging at my heart." His Ma said, "Your grandfather said a person has to go upward in this life. When I was young I talked back to him. I said there are enough people going upward; somebody at the foot of the mountain has to keep the stove lit. And I still say the same now: you just go upward, and your Pa and I will keep this stove lit. Don't fret about home. If you keep fretting, you won't keep your footing."

At daybreak, Xiaoman knelt in the yard and knocked his head to the ground three times for his Pa and Ma. His Pa saw him to the door and, holding on to it, said, "I never fixed this pivot. If I set it straight, I was afraid you wouldn't know it when you came back." Xiaoman went up the western spur and didn't look back once. Elder Qu was still standing where he'd stood the night before, a film of dew on the head of the broom. He gave Xiaoman a look and asked, "The things in your heart: settled?" Xiaoman said, "Settled. The tug's still there, but it doesn't trip my feet now." Elder Qu nodded and said, "That's enough."

---

On the second of the ninth month, at the foot of the wall in the east corner of the herb garden, Ahe's grain came into ear. Of the three, two ears were empty; only one had filled. She pinched it off and squatted at the end of the strip counting three times: seventeen grains, every one black. She put them back in the bamboo tube and sealed it with wax.

That night Xiaoman came down off the cliff, walked all the way to the mountain gate, and counted down those three hundred stone steps; at the hundred and sixtieth he sat down. Hundreds of years of feet had worn the step into a hollow, like a bowl. Uncle had said he too sat on this step in his day, sat for a stick of incense, and went down the mountain to learn the abacus. Grandfather in his day had sat on this step too, panted for a stick of incense, and stood up again, and climbed all the way to the top. And in the end he had gone down the mountain all the same.

He had two things inside his robe. One was the wooden tablet, warm. The other was the Nine-Turn Foundation Pill, pressed at the very bottom of his storage pouch for five years and only taken out tonight, wrapped in oilpaper, cool to the touch. When his master asked which pill he'd use, he had lied. He wished he could run back to the clifftop, set the Rice Bucket in front of his master, and tell him everything of these five years, piece by piece. But he couldn't. The first word Yinyue taught him was "hide"; and for the first time in his life that word felt so heavy. Whether hiding things from his master counted as something he couldn't get past, he didn't know.

He took the scroll of *The Breathing Classic* from inside his robe and by the moonlight turned to the back. There was one bamboo slip he had always skipped before, not understanding it, but now his eye caught on it at once: "[Calm and still, empty and without desire, and the true qi follows]{典=suwen}." Think of nothing, want nothing, and the true qi comes of itself. He sat cross-legged on the step, counting breaths, practising that "think of nothing". At thirty he thought of the three days Grandfather sat on the clifftop; at fifty he thought of his master's hand clenched white, and the breath fell apart of itself. He tried three times, and three times it fell apart past fifty.

Just before dawn, he spoke quietly to the wooden tablet on his chest: "Yinyue. I'm a bit scared." The wooden tablet didn't answer. He went on, "...My grandfather was scared too, I suppose."

Still the tablet didn't answer. But after a while it grew warm for a moment, warm very, very faintly, as though someone very far away had reached out through a very thick quilt and patted him.

---

The third of the ninth month was clear. Elder Qu had gone down the mountain first thing to sweep the steps. Xiaoman stood on the clifftop looking down, and could see the three hundred stone steps at the mountain gate, and a small grey figure, bent over, sweeping stroke by stroke. Those three hundred steps he had swept for fifty-six years; today, he swept them especially long. Xiaoman knew his master didn't dare come up and watch. That night long ago, his master had waited outside the room.

Xiaoman sat down cross-legged on the bluestone in front of the grass hut. In his hand he held the Nine-Turn he'd got from the Rice Bucket in exchange for a radish; in his dantian, as a bed, was the thick earth grown from that handful of breathing soil in the secret realm; his five doors had long been open; and every meridian in his body had been chiselled through, night after night, by Yinyue sleeping on his heart. It was a good pill. But a pill can only get into the dantian, not into the heart; and that night long ago Grandfather had lost at the heart.

Grandfather's pill hadn't been wrong either. He had that business of his father weighing on his heart, and couldn't take a single step past it. If Grandfather failed, he could still go down the mountain and teach children sums. If he failed, he'd have nothing all his life but a pair of Qi Condensation legs, and the man from the Si River ferry waiting outside the mountain gate.

He held the Nine-Turn Foundation Pill in his palm, and didn't put it in his mouth. His master had said: before you eat it, first turn over the things in your heart one by one and look at them. He closed his eyes. The first thing to turn over was last night's thought: Grandfather didn't get through back then, the senior brother on the bed opposite didn't get through; what if I can't either? Once that thought rose, the hand holding the pill began to shake. From somewhere a fog rose in front of his eyes, spreading up layer on layer. In the fog he saw a door. It was wooden, old, half the paint gone from the frame, and on the door was pasted a sheet of paper with a few characters on it. He couldn't make out one of them, but he felt that those characters must say "Do Not Enter". Behind the door, he knew, was a village school, and in the school sat a man at the ninth layer, perfected, who couldn't take one step further up, reciting a line of the *Zhuangzi* that mocked himself. If he pushed the door open and went in, he'd sit there with that man, sit there all his life.

Run. A voice in his heart said it. The voice spoke very softly, and very reasonably: put the pill away, stop at Qi Condensation, you can still live, you can still go home and teach children sums. Running's no shame. Ma said so.

He very nearly put the pill back in his storage pouch. He remembered what his master had said sitting on the bluestone: go halfway in and think of going back, and that cut has fallen apart before it lands.

He didn't argue with the voice. He did only the oldest thing, something he had known since his first winter of breathing out and in: the breath held tight in his chest, he let out the way Ma squatted by the stove blowing on a few coals, fine and long, a breath out longer than the breath in. His heart was jumping like a lamp flame in the wind; with that breath out the flame dropped a little, and didn't flap so wildly. The fear was still there, the hand still shaking; but that voice was no longer the only voice in his heart.

The fog didn't lift, though, and out of the fog another door rose. This one he knew: the frame was pine his Pa had cut, the pivot hung crooked, and you had to lift it a little to open or shut it. Shi'ao Village, his own family's door, the one he had watched from the western spur as his Pa lifted it and shut it. Outside the door was a field of snow, and in the snow stood a man, all in white, hands behind his back, not moving, looking in at the door: the same as the one who followed Ma San from house to house that winter, stopping at each. The man at the other end of the mirror had long known which door was his family's.

The voice came again, and this time it was more reasonable still: stop at Qi Condensation and go home. Go home and guard that door.

He very nearly believed it again. But when he let the breath out to the bottom, he saw it clearly: stop at Qi Condensation and go home, and that man would still be standing outside the door. A Qi Condensation cultivator couldn't stop him, and a man teaching children sums could stop him still less. When that time came, there'd simply be one more person inside the door who couldn't stop anybody.

But just then he remembered another door. A door his Pa had told him about. When his Pa was young he took a wild rabbit to Grandfather's village school, and there was writing pasted on the door. His Pa couldn't read, and was afraid it said "No Idlers Enter", so he stood at the door for a whole two hours. After standing two hours, his Pa went in anyway. Later his Ma said, "What it said was 'No Lessons Today'." When Xiaoman got to that, he actually smiled.

His Pa couldn't read, stood two hours, and went in anyway. His Ma could read, and married a man who stood two hours before he dared go in at a door. Grandfather's door, it turned out, his Pa had gone through long ago; and what was written on it had never been "Do Not Enter". Being afraid had never been a reason not to go in.

He wasn't running any more.

The line he hadn't managed to do on the stone steps last night, however hard he tried, somehow did itself now: there was nothing in his heart. Once his spirit settled, the breath settled with it, not long, not short, not advancing, not retreating: the half line Master Chu read out of the book wasn't in the book after all, it was here. The fog in front of his eyes dispersed thread by thread. The hand holding the pill stopped shaking too.

Xiaoman opened his eyes and put the Nine-Turn Foundation Pill in his mouth. He didn't chew it at first. He held it a while, and said in his heart: Grandfather, this step, I'll take for you. When he'd said it, he swallowed.

The pill melted the moment it touched his tongue, and a cool stream slid down his throat, like swallowing a mouthful of well water. At first there was no sign of anything. He sat for a stick of incense and heard only the pine wind below the cliff, gust after gust, and thought: don't tell me the Rice Bucket has produced a good-looking piece of junk this time.

Just as he was wondering, the sky suddenly darkened. He looked up, and overhead, who knew when, a cloud had gathered. It wasn't big; it covered only this one clifftop, grey shot with white, turning slowly, turning faster and faster, until it had turned into a great upturned pot. All around, the wind stopped at once, the pines went silent, the birds stopped singing, and even the sound of the waterfall below the cliff was as if muffled by a cotton quilt. A thread of light came down through the heart of the cloud, not to one side or the other, and shone right on the top of his head.

At that moment, from the bottom of the water vat in his dantian, things began to rise. Not spiritual power, something heavier, lump by lump, like stones, floating up from the bottom of the vat and sinking again; and as they sank they stacked together, one, two, ten, and built a terrace at the bottom of his dantian. The five doors, metal, wood, water, fire and earth, lit one after another, and with each that lit the terrace was tamped one layer firmer.

He had heard the senior brothers in the West Gallery say that this tamping of Foundation Establishment takes off a layer of skin: the bones crack joint by joint, the meridians feel threaded with red-hot wire, and the pain makes you bite your teeth till they bleed. But this pill was different. At every tamp, only a coolness spread out from his dantian, all the way to his fingertips and toes. His bones cracked softly, once, the flesh loosened with them, and something like a stream of cool water ran through his meridians and washed the rust in them clean away. He sat there without the least pain, without even one drop of sweat. The terrace built up layer on layer, and from start to finish it never wobbled once.

As the terrace reached its top, *The Yellow Court Classic*{典=huangting} that Elder Qu had tossed him came up out of his belly by itself, line by line: "Above, the Yellow Court; below, the Gate of Origin; behind, the Hidden Tower; in front, the Gate of Life." When he learned it by heart before he had taken them for four place names; only now did he know they were four places in his own body, above, below, behind, in front, each one bracing that terrace, like the four piles you drive when you build a house. Once the four piles were set, the terrace was steady.

His eyes were closed, and yet for the first time he could see. That water vat had never been a vat; it was a lake, and the bed of the lake was black earth, the handful he swallowed in the secret realm. The terrace, the five doors, the four piles, one by one, all of them were inside his own body. And there was a channel, starting at the edge of the lake and winding away to somewhere he couldn't see, chiselled very fine, very even, and on its walls, one after another, fine claw marks. He knew whose claws they were. Only now did his master's words sink in: "What you've learned is the Outer Scenery. The Inner Scenery, once you've laid your foundation, you'll see for yourself."

So this was **Foundation Establishment**. But the terrace had settled, and the coolness wasn't used up. It welled up from under the terrace, surge after surge, and the water in the lake rose with it, rose till it was level with the edge of the terrace and could rise no more, and stopped. The greater part of the pill's power that was left had nowhere to go, and slowly sank, sank into the layer of black earth at the bottom of the lake. By then the "pot" overhead had silently broken up. Where the cloud opened, the stars were a little brighter than usual, a little nearer.

---

That day Ahe didn't go into the garden. She stood at the herb-garden gate all day with her little notebook in her hand, and didn't write a word. Elder Qu, at the mountain gate, swept back and forth all day over the three hundred steps he'd swept clean before it was light; by dark the cloud over the back mountain had silently broken up, his broom stopped in mid-air, and then he shouldered it and climbed, step by step, towards the back mountain.

When Xiaoman opened his eyes, it was already dark. He thought a stick of incense had gone by, and instead a whole day had gone.

Stranger still, his eyes, his ears, his mind had all been exchanged for new ones. He stood up and looked down the cliff, and the whole Sunken Cauldron mountain was in his eyes: every tile, every tree, even Xiaozheng stealing sweet potatoes behind the woodshed with the kitchen auntie brandishing a ladle in pursuit, all perfectly clear. Three li away, the bronze bell at the eave-corner of the Scripture Library swung, and he could hear that it swung east. Further still, the Si River rushed along at the foot of the mountain, and under the sound of the water lay another sound, very slow, very deep, rising and falling, as if something enormous were sleeping under the water, had been sleeping a great many years. He cocked his ear and listened a long while, and the sound was gone again, and there was only water.

He looked down the mountain again, and saw Ahe standing at the herb-garden gate, her face turned up towards the clifftop; saw his master with the broom on his shoulder, climbing the steps towards the back mountain, wobbling once halfway, then steadying. Looking at these two, his eyes suddenly went hot, and he didn't know whether it was the Foundation Establishment or something else.

The wind rose again on the clifftop. Only now did he notice that he saw these two differently from before. His master's cultivation he couldn't see through, like looking into a deep well, seeing only the mouth; Ahe's cultivation he couldn't see through either: that well was shallower, but he still couldn't see the bottom. Every Qi Condensation disciple on the mountain was perfectly clear in his eyes, like the marks on a steelyard, and yet these two, he couldn't see through either one.

From the wooden tablet on his chest, a very sleepy voice said: "...Mm. Foundation laid. For a mortal, not bad." After a while it went on: "Other people, once they've laid their foundation, take years to walk the Lesser Attainment step to the end. You walked it to the end in one night: Foundation Establishment, Lesser Attainment, perfected. You only used a small part of that pill. The rest has all sunk to the bottom of that lake of yours, and you'll dissolve it slowly yourself from here on." And lazily: "If the foundation isn't laid firm, then later, Core Formation, Nascent Soul, every storey you build falls down." Xiaoman said, "So is my foundation firm?" There was no sound from the tablet for a long time; after a good while he heard her yawn: "...Firmer than your family's roof."

Xiaoman said, pleased as anything, "Did you know all along that once I'd laid my foundation I'd be able to see at a glance what layer people are?" Yinyue said, "Don't get above yourself. The only ones you can see through are the ones below you." Xiaoman said, "Ahe laid her foundation three years before me. If I can't see through her, is that because she's above me?" Yinyue said, "She's walked her Lesser Attainment to the end too, just one step short of Middle Attainment; for now she's as high as you. Two people of the same height can't see through each other."

Xiaoman went on, "Yinyue, last night I said I was scared." The tablet was quiet a while, then said, "...This queen was asleep." Xiaoman said, "Then how do you know I said it last night?"

There was no more sound from the tablet. After a good while, that silver tail (the length that had gone dark in the secret realm, and still hadn't lit again) went bright, then dim, as though someone had turned over and put their face the other way.

Xiaoman broke into a grin.

---

In the middle of the night someone was sitting on the bluestone by the grass-hut door. Elder Qu was back. The broom leaned in the corner by the wall, the bamboo twigs of its head worn down a length. He sat there without lighting a lamp, and by the starlight watched Xiaoman come over from the cliff edge, watched him all the way till he stood still, and said, "Eaten it?" Xiaoman stopped. He knew which one his master meant. "Eaten it." Elder Qu said, "How many lines?"

The clifftop was very quiet. Xiaoman looked at his master, and his master looked at him. Those eyes that had watched tens of thousands of children climb the steps were there under the starlight now, and didn't blink once.

Xiaoman said, "...One." Elder Qu said nothing. He looked at his face, looked at his eyes, looked at how he stood there: legs steady, not a wobble, not one black spot on his face, cleaner than the day he came through the gate. And the lake in his dantian: a man who had only laid his foundation yesterday, and the lake was already full. A pill with one line couldn't lay a foundation like that. Of course he could see it.

But he only nodded and said, "Mm. One." Then he stood up, walked to the edge of the cliff, and with his back to Xiaoman looked down at the dark mass of the stone steps below. He looked a long time, and said:

"That step of your grandfather's: you've finished walking it for him."

His voice wasn't loud, and didn't shake; it was level, just as when he'd said "Steady legs. Careful mind." But Xiaoman saw that his master's hands, the hands that had swept the steps for fifty-six years, hanging at his sides, never stopped shaking.

Xiaoman said nothing. He walked over and stood behind his master and looked down at the steps too. The two of them stood like that until the sky in the east showed a little white.

Elder Qu turned and went into the grass hut. Of the two teacups on the table, one was right side up and one upside down, and the upside-down one had sat there over forty years, under a thick layer of dust. Elder Qu turned it over, wiped it with his sleeve, poured two cups of cold tea, and pushed one in front of Xiaoman. Neither of them said anything. They drank the tea.

When the tea was drunk, Elder Qu shouldered the broom and went down the mountain. That morning, as always, he swept from the bottom step upwards, and at the hundred and sixtieth he stopped, swept that step with the worn-down broom once, and once more, as if sweeping the place where someone had sat. When he'd finished he sat on that step for a stick of incense, then stood up and went on sweeping upwards.

---

The next day Zhou Heng saw him in the stewards' office, looked him up and down a long while, and said, "You've laid your foundation." Xiaoman said, "Yes. Last night it was done." Zhou Heng opened the ledger, found a line, ticked it with his brush, and said, "The Foundation Pill awarded to you at last year's contest has been sitting on the books all this time with the word 'unused'. Today I've ticked it off." Xiaoman said, "Tick it off, senior brother Zhou."

Zhou Heng capped his brush and went on, "By abbey rules, a Qi Condensation disciple who meets one who's laid his foundation calls him Martial Uncle. You've laid your foundation; from now on when I see you I ought to call you Martial Uncle Shen." Xiaoman said hastily, "Senior brother Zhou, just call me Xiaoman as before." Zhou Heng shook his head and said, "Rules are rules. If you still call me senior brother, that's you being polite; if I call you Martial Uncle, that's what I ought to call you." The corner of his mouth moved, and then he set his face straight again.

Zhou Heng shut the ledger and went on, "Once you've laid your foundation you ought to have a room of your own. The third one at the east end has come free. The window faces south; it's warm in winter." Xiaoman said, "What about the one who lived there before?" Zhou Heng said, "Went down the mountain. Didn't get through the gate." As he said it his eyes stayed on the ledger, and he didn't look up. "This year two tried for the gate, counting you."

When Xiaoman came out of the stewards' office, someone was waiting at the door with an osmanthus cake in his hand: Ma Xiaobao. In the past, when Ma Xiaobao met him on the path, he could turn his face away and pretend not to see him; from today, he couldn't. When a Qi Condensation disciple met one who'd laid his foundation, whoever was older, he had to stand aside to the edge of the path, bow, and say "Martial Uncle".

Ma Xiaobao stood at the door without moving, the flesh of his cheeks twitching. The osmanthus cake had gone flat in his fist, the sugar filling squeezing out between his fingers. Only one thing went round and round in his head: six years ago in Shi'ao Village, this person was the son of his family's tenant, and he'd had people pin him to the ground and beat him, and this person hadn't even dared hit back; the day they came through the gate, this person was the leaky ladle at the bottom of the whole abbey. He, Ma Xiaobao, had eaten pills for six years, eaten till his face was covered in blotches, and only got to the seventh layer. And now he was to bow his head and call this leaky ladle Martial Uncle.

In the end he did step aside to the edge of the path, bowed his head, joined his two hands in a salute, and squeezed three words through his teeth: "Martial Uncle Shen." His voice was as small as a mosquito's whine. Xiaoman stopped, looked at him a while, nodded, and said, "Nephew Ma." Having said those words, he walked past, without one word more. Ma Xiaobao's face went red from the root of his neck to the tips of his ears.

When Xiaoman had gone a long way off, Ma Xiaobao went into the stewards' office and asked Zhou Heng across the table in a low voice: "Senior brother Zhou, he's got five spirit roots, and he laid his foundation on one pill?" Zhou Heng said, "He's Elder Qu's disciple. How he trained these five years, Elder Qu doesn't come and report the accounts, and I don't have it here. All I have on the books is one entry: one Foundation Pill, awarded at last year's contest, ticked off today."

Ma Xiaobao stood there and didn't move for a long time. The one five years ago, his family had sold three-tenths of the trees on Mount Meng and not got it; last year's, a leaky ladle had taken and laid his foundation with. He didn't say a word, and went out of the stewards' office with his head down.

Ahe came last. She stood at the door of the third room at the inner sect's east end and didn't go in. Across the threshold she looked him over from head to foot, then at his face, which hadn't a single black spot on it. She looked a while, and asked, "What you ate: was it the Foundation Pill the abbey gave you?" Xiaoman said, "It was the one the abbey gave me." Ahe nodded, took out her little notebook and wrote a line, closed it, and said, "Since that's what you say, I won't ask further. I'll just write this one down. No interest on it." When she'd said it, she turned and left.

---

That night a Daoist boy knocked at the door of the third room at the east end and said the Abbot was waiting for him in the main hall.

Only one lamp was lit in the main hall. The Abbot, Xuanchenzi, sat beside the empty stone pedestal with a yellowed map spread in front of him, his finger pressed on a winding ink line in the north. Xiaoman came closer and craned to look, and beside the ink line two small characters were written: **Zhang River.**

His heart gave a *thump*. The line from New Year's Eve stood up off the page of that old ledger, word by word.

Xuanchenzi didn't look up. He said, "Foundation laid." Xiaoman said, "...Yes. Foundation laid." Xuanchenzi said "Mm," rolled up the map, and said, "Ji Province. Ye. Go there. Take Zhou Heng with you. Zhou Heng, he..." He got that far and stopped.

Xiaoman waited a long time; the Abbot only waved a hand. As usual he had said only half a sentence, and the other half, who knew whether he'd left it to Zhou Heng or to the Zhang River.

Xiaoman couldn't help saying, "Abbot, to do what?"

Xuanchenzi looked at the empty stone pedestal and said, "On the Zhang River there's something strange." A pause. "Every year, someone finds the River Lord a bride."

Xiaoman was about to ask more, but Xuanchenzi went on, "This year we're not the only ones watching that river." With that he closed his eyes.
