# Chapter Five: Three Days Pinned to the Cliff for the Bully / A Pinch of Living Soil Opens Five Doors

On the last night of the tenth month, just past the third watch, in the long shared bunk of the outer sect's boys at the Sunken Cauldron Abbey, a boy sleeping by the window sat bolt upright and shouted: "The water's gone!"

It was a sharp, frantic shout, and it woke more than half the room. Some swore, some groped for their shoes, someone planted a foot on somebody else's face. Xiaoman slept in the bunk nearest the door. He pulled his quilt round him, squeezed to the window, looked down the mountain, and froze. Under the moon, the Si River had vanished. The broad river of every other day had shrunk to one bright thread down the middle, and on either side lay mudflats further than the eye could reach, the mud glimmering white. In the bend of the river below Sunken Cauldron Mountain, something stood up out of the flats, black and square, more than ten feet tall, like a door.

"The secret realm," said the boy who had woken them, his voice shaking. "The secret realm's open." The notice that had hung on the board for a day or two said the first day of the eleventh month; nobody had thought the river would go in the middle of the night, all in one breath.

The boy's name was Lu, a fisherman's son from the banks of the Si, who had come into the sect the intake before: dark and thin, his hands soaked white and wrinkled, and everyone called him Lu the Loach. Clinging to the window frame, he told them what his grandfather used to say: there was something under the river that turned over once every ten years, and when it turned it drew a breath, and the water went down; three days later it let the breath out, the water came back, and the door was gone.

Behind the door was a ruin. The abbey's founder, long ago, had carried out of it the broken scrolls of the *Instructions on the Divine Elixirs of the Nine Cauldrons of the Yellow Emperor*{典=jiuding}, and only then built the abbey on the mountain by the river. Since then it had opened once every ten years; whoever went in went on their own wits, and whatever they picked up was theirs. "Then why don't the martial uncles and the elders go in first and pick it clean?" somebody asked. Lu the Loach said: "There's a ward on the door. It only lets in Qi Condensation. A martial uncle who's established his foundation can't get a single foot through; an elder who's formed his core is the same, stands at the door and glares." "The good stuff got picked clean by the ones before," said an older disciple. "Master Chu says so."

Nobody slept again that night. The older disciples had never been in either, and the less they'd been in, the more vivid they made it: "There's a cliff in there. Cursed. Ten years ago three went down and one came up."

Xiaoman lay back down on his bunk, felt the wooden tablet on his chest, and whispered: "Yinyue, that cliff. Do you know it?" The tablet didn't answer. At the far end of the bunk, on the best spot by the brazier, came a *creak, creak*, one at a time, slow and even: Ma Xiaobao was eating osmanthus cakes again. When he'd finished he balled up the oiled paper and tossed it over his shoulder, onto the very last bunk, and said thickly into the dark: "What's to be scared of. The ones who fall off cliffs are idiots." Nobody answered him.

The very last bunk sat under the draughty window, and in it slept a long, thin older disciple everyone called Old Wilt. He was the son of one of the Ma family's tenants, three years in the sect, second layer of Qi Condensation, and didn't say three sentences a day. He picked up the ball of oiled paper, smoothed it flat, folded it into a neat square and tucked it into his robe, to throw away for Ma Xiaobao first thing in the morning. On the night of the twelfth of the tenth month, in the woodshed, at the twentieth blow, of the five of them, the one who had pulled his hand back and said low, "That's enough," was him. Ever since, Ma Xiaobao had made him sleep under the window.

---

On the first of the eleventh month, before it was light, the usual long queue had formed outside the stewards' office. Zhou Heng sat behind the elm table with the ledger open in front of him, thick enough to use as a pillow. He called a name, handed out three spirit stones, then fished a copper name tag from an old wooden box at his feet, the person's name cut into the back. His manner today was not quite his usual one; with each he handed out he looked up and added a word: "In the secret realm, keep yourself alive first. Lose a spirit herb, it grows again next year; lose a person, it doesn't sit well in the books. Keep the tag on your body. It lets you out as well. Lose it and you don't get out." A new disciple asked him: "Senior Brother Zhou, has anyone died in the secret realm?" Zhou Heng leafed through the ledger to the last few pages. Their paper was yellower than the rest, and on them were a few names, with a red line drawn under each. He looked at them for a while, closed the ledger, and said: "Keep yourself alive first."

Behind Xiaoman in the queue was Old Wilt. He took his three spirit stones but didn't move off. He stared at the closed ledger and asked, low: "Senior Brother Zhou... the ones with the red lines. Do their families know?" Zhou Heng raised his eyes to him. "The abbey sends someone to tell them." Old Wilt nodded and tucked the three spirit stones into his robe, alongside the neatly folded oiled paper. His family rented three mu of hillside field from the Ma family; three mu, and the rent each year was six tenths of the harvest. Last year Ma San had said one thing to his father: "Your boy follows Xiaobao, your rent comes down a tenth." His father kept that tenth in mind, and so did he.

Halfway through, Zhou Heng stood up (Zhou Heng spoke standing perhaps a handful of times a year) and went over the Laba Grand Contest once more. A hum went down the line, and he was already sitting again, handing out stones. Sun Ergou, just ahead of Xiaoman, held his three spirit stones and didn't move off either. Red in the face, he asked: "Senior Brother Zhou, can z... zero-layer people go up on the stage?" Zhou Heng said: "Yes. Anyone whose name is in the register." Sun Ergou said "Oh," and asked again: "Has anyone from the outer sect... ever won?" Zhou Heng turned a page of the ledger. "In thirty years, not one." Sun Ergou walked off with his head down. After a few steps he felt the cloth bundle at his waist and said under his breath: "...Then I'll go up once. Win one bout, and write to tell my ma."

Xiaoman turned the words "Foundation Pill" over in his head. Ge Liu had said them, and Zhou Heng had said them, and at the time they'd sounded like something in the sky. Now the thing in the sky had come down to the door of the stewards' office, and there were thirty-seven days to the eighth of the twelfth month.

Behind the Common Fund box, Ma Xiaobao's hangers-on had gone from two to three today, standing like door gods. When he heard the words "Foundation Pill" he took a bite of his osmanthus cake and said at his leisure: "This year's pill is called Ma." Then he rapped the wooden box with his knuckles, *tok tok*, without lifting his eyelids: "Shen Xiaoman. Two."

Sun Ergou, ahead of him, had paid one. So Xiaoman said: "Brother Xiaobao, Senior Brother Sun paid one." Ma Xiaobao said: "He's him. You're you." Xiaoman said: "Why's that?" Only then did Ma Xiaobao lift his eyelids and grin at him: "Because you took in a beast. Beasts have to eat too." Xiaoman thought: the little zheng ate the leftover stalks from the herb garden, and those leftover stalks Zhou Heng had entered in the books and given him; they had nothing whatever to do with the Common Fund. But he didn't say it. He counted out two spirit stones, put them in the box, and said: "Brother Xiaobao's right. Beasts have to eat too." Ma Xiaobao heard nothing wrong in that and waved him on, well pleased. Xiaoman opened his hand. One spirit stone was left in his palm. Just the one.

The little zheng could not be taken into the secret realm: the realm's door knew name tags, and there were no beasts in the register. Xiaoman had to leave it at the stewards' office. It clung to his sleeve and wouldn't let go, all five tails standing straight up. Zhou Heng took a radish from his drawer, set it on the corner of the table and said: "It eats one, I write down one." The little zheng stared at the radish with a look that plainly said *you're selling me for one radish*, then let go of the sleeve, jumped onto the table and hugged the radish, gnawing it, *crunch, crunch*. One bite, and it turned to give him a look with wet, swimming eyes; another bite, another look. When it had finished, it hiccupped.

The name tags had come down from the founder: one tag, one person in. The outer sect's three hundred and more couldn't use them all, and for the hundred or so left over there was a separate page in Zhou Heng's ledger: sold to the young people of the Green Duckweed Sword School of Xiapi, the Red Pine Abbey of Pengcheng, and a few households in town, twenty spirit stones a tag, into the Common Fund. Seeing him look, Zhou Heng drew the ledger closed. "The abbey is poor. What we sell is a tag. Whatever you pick up inside is your own wits."

---

On the open ground outside the stewards' office, seven or eight tables had been set out since early morning, spread with sheets of yellowed paper: maps of the secret realm for sale. Someone who'd gone in ten years ago had drawn one, someone twenty years ago another, someone thirty years ago yet another; they copied each other, filled in each other's gaps, and every flagstone that would tip if you stepped on it was clearly marked. The sellers were a few disciples three years in, and Xiaoman knew two of them. Not their faces (he had had his arms over his head that night) but their shoes: one shoe with the toe patched in black cloth. On the night of the twelfth of the tenth month, that was the one that had broken the ribs on his left side. The other pair had kicked the right.

"One spirit stone a map! Going into the secret realm without a map is like a blind man going into the mountains!" Xiaoman stopped at the table and read the map from end to end. It showed several routes. The one drawn most thickly was a long corridor, three stone chambers, a stone bridge, and beyond the bridge a garden of spirit herbs, beside which was a line of small writing: "Herbs here thirty years ago." Below it, in another hand: "Twenty years ago, three plants left." And below that, in the freshest ink: "Ten years ago, none." Under the garden was drawn a river: "Deep water, do not go down." The older disciple selling it said: "The garden's bald, but there's another level under it. It's only on the inner sect's maps."

He held the spirit stone tight in his palm and didn't buy. Someone tapped his shoulder; he turned, and it was Ahe. She wasn't wearing the robes the Abbot gave his personal disciples. She had on the same grey robe one size too big as always, her nose red with cold, and among a crowd of grey robes nobody gave her a second look. Ahe said: "You're not buying." Xiaoman said: "One spirit stone for a piece of paper that says 'none'. Not worth it." Ahe said: "I knew you wouldn't buy." She pulled him round behind the tables to the foot of a wall where nobody was, and dropped her voice right down: "I know which opening you'll take, too."

Xiaoman blinked. Ahe counted on her fingers: "Four hundred and seventeen name tags, seven or eight openings. The inner sect goes by the inner sect's map; everyone else has a map each, and what's drawn thickest on the map is the long corridor, so the crowd will squeeze into the corridor, and the rest will follow their own people. In the end, there's one opening nobody wants. You're used to watching deer in the mountains. When a herd bolts, you stand still and wait for them to run past—" She paused. "The Deadly Cliff."

Xiaoman's mouth fell open, and it was a while before it closed.

"The Abbot's personal disciple can get into the Scripture Library," said Ahe. "The old records of the secret realm, three hundred years of them. I went through them all night. On the map the Deadly Cliff gets one line; the old records have a few more. The one who came up ten years ago said there's a wall at the bottom of the cliff, and the cracks in it draw air inwards. And the underground river beneath the garden: thirty years ago someone wrote that there's a cave halfway up the cliff of the river channel that blows warm air outwards, for three days at a stretch, as long as the realm stays open." She looked at him. "Those are the only two places in the secret realm with wind. One draws in, one blows out. It's one breath. They're connected in the middle. If you're really at the end of your road, go into the wind. At the other end of the wind there's a way out."

Xiaoman said: "...You're not going to stop me?" Ahe said: "Could I?" She turned to go, took two steps and looked back: "I'm taking the corridor. The corridor goes to the garden, and the old records say the underground river under the garden once grew purple-whisker lingzhi. I want one."

---

On the three hundred stone steps before the mountain gate, Elder Qu was sweeping as usual. Three hundred and more people poured down past him, and he didn't lift his head, only moved his broom aside a little. When Xiaoman reached him he slowed. Elder Qu spoke suddenly, not loudly, as if to the steps: "The water's gone lower than last time." Xiaoman started. Elder Qu kept sweeping. "Last time was lower than the time before." Xiaoman said: "...Is that good or bad?" Elder Qu didn't answer. He moved the broom aside and left a way clear: "Steady legs."

At the hour of the dragon the three hundred and more outer disciples were crowded onto the riverbed. The mud was black with a skin of white frost on it, and on the frost lay a few fish that hadn't followed the water in time, mouths opening and closing. Overnight the abbey's servants had laid planks over the mud, from the bank right up to the stone door, one narrow path. The stone door stood exposed on the mudflat, and white wisps were seeping out through the crack. On a winter morning, that white air was warm.

Mixed in among the grey robes were a hundred or so of other colours. Green robes with long swords, speaking with Xiapi accents: the Green Duckweed Sword School. Ochre-yellow robes with gourds at the belt: the Red Pine Abbey. And seven or eight young gentlemen in brocade, one tag each; their servants couldn't get in, so they stood on the bank holding their masters' food boxes. The steward calling the names (Ma Xiaobao's uncle, his father's cousin) stood at the head of the path with a thin register, read out an outsider's name, handed over a tag. When he read out a plump young gentleman in brocade, the yellow robe behind him smiled and stepped half a pace aside to let him go first. That yellow robe was twenty-five or twenty-six, fair-faced, and the gourd at his belt was not like his fellows': red copper, the size of a fist, its mouth stopped with a copper plug, and the white frost round his feet had melted in a ring around the gourd. The right hand that held the gourd had a scorched black patch in the palm, as if branded. On his left arm hung a small red-copper shield the size of a plate, engraved with flames, and in the grooves something red seemed to move, slowly. One of his fellows was boasting to someone: "Our Senior Brother Zou, ninth layer of Qi Condensation. That shield was the founder's gift. Nothing below Core Formation can break it." He didn't look at the Sunken Cauldron people. He looked only at the bulging purse at the plump young gentleman's waist. A fish at his feet was still gasping; he set his heel on its head and ground it, and his face went on smiling at the steward. The steward read: "Red Pine Abbey of Pengcheng, Zou Qingsong." He stepped up and took his tag.

Before the stone door stood a man in a grey robe, mild of face, hands tucked into his sleeves: the Abbot, Xuanchenzi. When he came down from the mountain, a martial uncle of Foundation Establishment arrived ahead of him on a flying sword, and the people on the riverbed parted of their own accord to make a path; as he walked through it, hundreds of people on the mudflat swallowed even their coughs, and the bright thread of water in the river's middle lay behind him still as a drawn string. The older disciples said that every time the door opened, the Abbot stood like this, from the first one in to the last one out. Asked what he was standing there for, he said he was counting people. But he never used a book.

Sun Ergou squeezed up beside Xiaoman, his snot frozen into two little icicles, and said quietly: "Senior Brother Shen, can I follow you? My spirit root's poor, and if I go in alone I'm scared..." He went red first. "...scared I won't come back." At Sun Ergou's waist was tied a blue cloth bundle with a bit of red string showing: the charm his mother had sewn. On Xiaoman's back was his grandfather's bow. A hunter going into a mountain he'd never been in doesn't put his bow down. In his quiver were three bamboo arrows he'd whittled himself in the woodshed, the heads made from old iron nails begged off the kitchen. He took a radish out of his bundle and pushed it into Sun Ergou's hand: "Follow the crowd. Walk in the middle. The blades fall on the edges first." Sun Ergou held the radish and said: "...And you, Senior Brother?" Xiaoman said: "I'll go where there's nobody. No people, no blades." Sun Ergou didn't understand, but he nodded very solemnly and tucked the radish into his robe, alongside the charm.

The steward struck the gong three times. Xuanchenzi took a name tag from his sleeve, a size bigger than everyone else's, its copper older too, and pressed it into a hollow in the middle of the stone door. Ma Xiaobao led his people in a rush to push to the front, and as they pushed past Xiaoman, the last of them fell half a step behind. It was Old Wilt. In one hand he carried Ma Xiaobao's gilded food box, and over his shoulder was slung a big coil of hemp rope. His eyes weren't on Xiaoman, only on his own toes, and his lips barely moved: "...Brother Bao's got someone watching you. The rope's for you." Having said it, he took two quick steps and caught up.

The stone door groaned and opened a crack, and across the crack was stretched something shining and wet, like a skin of water pulled tight, a faint light running over it. The chill hit your face from three paces off, and the faces of the people nearest it turned blue in its light. The first lot in were the inner sect's Qi Condensation disciples, twenty-odd of them, who didn't queue: they walked across the planks one behind another, each holding a rolled-up map of the inner sect's own. Those tables on the open ground they had never so much as glanced at. The five at the front were ninth layer. A young gentleman in brocade tried to squeeze in on their tail; the ninth-layer man in front didn't turn round, just swept his sleeve backwards, and the young gentleman flew twenty or thirty feet and planted himself in the black mud, sunk to the chest. Servants on the bank hauled him out with bamboo poles, and nobody looked twice. One after another the inner sect sank through the water skin. Only then did the four hundred-odd others crush in after them, the skin wobbling, people vanishing one by one. Xiaoman didn't crush in. Ninth layer, he thought: from now on, every time I see one, I keep out of the way. But there was one other person in the crowd who didn't crush in either: of the two map sellers, the one who'd kicked his left ribs stood at the edge of the crowd with his arms folded, eyes not on the stone door but fixed on him. Xiaoman stood on a rock and waited. When the last person had squeezed through the door, he jumped down and walked in, unhurried. Inside the door he looked back: the man with folded arms was following him in, no hurry either.

The moment he stepped through the stone door, the wooden tablet on his chest flared hot. It wasn't like the usual: it burned once and didn't stop, a second time, a third, each one closer on the last, like a heart beating under his own. Yinyue came out and crouched on his shoulder, her body a little more solid than before. She didn't yawn. Her golden eyes were wide open, looking at the stone walls, at the patterns on the stone walls, her nose twitching and twitching, as if she were smelling something very, very far away.

She spoke, and her voice had changed: it was like someone standing in the courtyard she'd lived in as a child. "...This place. This queen has been here." Xiaoman asked: "When?" Yinyue said: "I don't know." Xiaoman asked: "To do what?" Yinyue said: "I don't know." Xiaoman said: "Then what do you know?" Yinyue's nose twitched twice more before she said: "...Down."

---

Inside the door was a stone hall, with seven or eight openings in its four walls. Everyone had a map, and the routes on the maps were all different: those going by the map, those following their own people, those guessing with their eyes shut, one cluster after another scattered into the openings. The inner sect's twenty-odd went by their own map into one without looking back. The long corridor that was drawn thickest had the most people crammed into it. Opening by opening, the hall thinned out. One opening was left that nobody took: a narrow cleft in the rock, half blocked by a great fallen boulder, and on the boulder was carved a crooked character, *Deadly*, with a skull drawn under it.

Ahe followed the crowd. Before she went through the door she had seen two things: of the two map sellers, the one with folded arms, who, while everyone pushed for the door, didn't push but kept his eyes nailed on Shen Xiaoman; and on Old Wilt's shoulder, a big coil of hemp rope. You don't need that much rope to pick herbs in a secret realm.

She went by the map in her head. At the door of the second stone chamber, the third flagstone on the floor would tip when stepped on. She stood to one side and called, "Don't step on the third one." One person heard; another didn't have time to, was elbowed aside by a young gentleman in brocade behind him, and fell in. From the pit came an "Ow!" in Sun Ergou's voice. The crowd pushed her forward; she looked back and called a second time, and nobody answered.

The stone bridge was narrow, room for one person. Two people ran into each other in the dark in the middle of it and neither would give way, and a green robe's sword came out of its scabbard first; the one who fell was holding a fire-stick in his hand. Ahe crouched at the head of the bridge for as long as an incense stick takes to burn before she dared to cross.

In the herb garden beyond the bridge, the last plant had been pulled up root and all. Two of the abbey's own older disciples held a young gentleman in brocade face down in the mud, held him until he stopped moving, and only then let go. They squatted in the mud and split the plant, half each; then they went through his purse. Five spirit stones: two each, and for the one left over, the two of them played finger-guessing. The underground river below the garden ran through the belly of the mountain, and the map showed it as water. This year the Si had gone down so low that the river had dried and showed its bed, and the cracks in the rock were full of spirit herbs, clump after clump, high grade, middle and low, with a seam of copper in the wall. Everyone surged downwards. There was only one way down, a stone step, one person wide. At the top of the step stood Qi Changsong of the inner sect, eighth layer, the hem of his robe grey with yellow dust, both feet nailed to the spot. People surged up to him, pushed him, shoved him, like water against a post: they couldn't shift him, and could only split round him on either side and flow back. Below the step, the four ninth-layers pulled clump after clump, pulled one and stuffed it in a sleeve, without looking at the grade. Only when they'd had enough and came up did Qi Changsong move one step aside.

Ahe didn't go and fight for any. She was fourth layer, and her method was for nursing plants; fighting was the thing she was worst at. The four ninth-layers below the step and the eighth-layer at the top, any one of them could sweep her twenty or thirty feet with a sleeve. She went along the edge of the underground river towards the end where there was nobody, and at the very end was a patch of ground that was black, black and shining, without a blade of grass, exactly like her family's hillside field in Shi'ao Village. At the edge of the black ground, of all places, one plant had come up: three leaves, purple shot through with gold, a fine down on their backs, and in the black ground it glowed by itself with a dark gold light, like three little oil lamps burned down to the last of the oil. "Purple-whisker lingzhi: one plant is worth three spirit stones." Three spirit stones were an outer disciple's monthly allowance. She crouched, and under her fingertips the black earth loosened and showed half of something, black and hard as a pebble: grain. Grain that looked burned. She dug down further with a fingernail. Under that patch of black ground, ridge after ridge, in tidy rows: a long time ago, somebody had farmed here. The roots of the purple-whisker lingzhi were twined right into one ridge of that grain; pull the herb, and you'd turn roots and grain over together into a mess of mud. She didn't pull it. From this end of the ridge, far from the herb's roots, she dug out three grains, one at a time, and didn't hurt a single root.

---

There were some in the stone hall who went towards the Deadly Cliff too. An older disciple of the abbey went up to the cleft, stuck his head in and looked down, pulled his head back with a curse, and went after the crowd. A Green Duckweed green robe stood there a while and then left as well. Xiaoman was no braver than them, and no cleverer. The opening the ninth-layers went into, he wouldn't take; the opening Ma Xiaobao went into, he wouldn't take either; this one was left, and nobody wanted it. Besides, it was a cliff, and cliffs he was used to climbing.

Before Xiaoman reached the cleft, someone spoke beyond the corner of the hall, in a very friendly voice: "...Twisted your ankle? Let me help you." He pushed Yinyue off his shoulder and into the front of his robe and stood flat against the stone wall. Beyond the corner, the plump young gentleman in brocade sat on the ground clutching his ankle. Everyone had scattered into the openings and not one had waited for him. Crouched in front of him was the yellow robe with the scorched palm. The plump young gentleman said: "Thank you... thank you, senior brother." Zou Qingsong said: "What's in the purse?" The plump young gentleman clapped a hand over his waist: "My father gave me... a charm to save my life..." Zou Qingsong smiled a little and pulled the plug from his gourd.

A fire snake shot out of the gourd's mouth, a foot or so long, red as red, and wrapped itself once round the plump young gentleman's neck. He got out half a scream. The burnt smell drifted over, half silk, half flesh. Zou Qingsong called the fire snake back into the gourd, untied the purse and tipped it out to look: one life-saving charm, split in two; seven spirit stones. He tucked the stones into his robe, wiped the gourd's mouth on the young gentleman's brocade, and pulled the name tag off his belt and, without a glance at it, threw it off into the dark. Then he stood up and, humming a little tune, went into the crowded opening.

Xiaoman stayed flat against the wall until the tune could no longer be heard, and only then moved. He went past the plump young gentleman; the man's eyes were still open, looking up at the roof of the hall. He turned sideways and squeezed into the cleft.

Behind the cleft a flight of stone steps led down, narrow, steep, wet, thick with moss. After about as long as it takes to eat a meal, there was a sheer drop ahead, black below, and all he could hear from down there was dripping water: one drop, a long gap, another drop. Yinyue came out of his robe and said: "Down. The spirit qi here comes up from below... it's thick. Outside, it's as thin as a bowl of pot-scrubbing water; here, it's like a well sealed for a few hundred years with the lid just lifted."

He rolled up his sleeves and climbed down the cliff face. He was a hunter's child, and climbing cliffs was his trade, but this cliff grew not a blade of grass from top to bottom, and the rock was cold to the bone. His left ribs, broken that night of the twelfth of the tenth month, still hadn't knitted, and every time he strained, it felt as if someone were pushing a needle in.

Halfway down there was firelight below. He wedged himself into a crack in the cliff and lay flat. Below was a narrow stone ledge with two inner-sect disciples on it, one holding a fire-stick; the other held a sword, and he was the ninth-layer who had swept his sleeve on the riverbed. The ninth-layer went all the way round a bare stone wall on the ledge, tapping it, and when he'd tapped down to its foot he brought his sword down hard.

At the foot of the wall was a half-dry pool, and beside the pool lay a black rock the size of a millstone. Xiaoman too had taken it for a rock. At the sound of the sword, a head came out of the front of the "rock", big as a bushel, red as red, like a coal burned through. The ninth-layer swung back and struck its shell; sparks flew, and the blade skidded off, cracking a line in the shell's rim near the neck. It didn't pull in. That head shot out, faster than the sword, and with a *crack* it had the ninth-layer's shoulder and half his neck in its jaws and was dragging him into the pool. The sword left his hand and fell with a clang by the fallen rocks. The one with the fire-stick threw it down, turned and dived into a crack behind the rockfall (that was where the two of them had come from) and didn't look back once. There was some thrashing in the pool, and then there wasn't.

He didn't go down. He lay in his crack and took in everything below. The ledge hugged the foot of the cliff and was only three or four paces wide; one step further out and it was black again. That was the real Deadly Cliff, bottomless, and the dripping he'd heard came up from down there. At the outer edge of the ledge an iron peg had been driven in, rusted almost through, with half a length of hemp rope tied to it, hanging down into the dark. At one end of the ledge, against the cliff foot, was a pile of fallen rock half a man high; at this end was the stone wall, a dozen feet high, no door, no crack. In the floor at the wall's foot a huge shallow hollow had been cut, worn smooth, with a layer of dried mud in the bottom, and right above it a stone eave jutted from the cliff. Yinyue said: "Behind that wall, there's air coming through."

The black rock didn't move. Its shell was like a great iron pot turned upside down, black as iron; on the shell's rim were old marks, one after another, cut by blades, pried by iron tools, so old they'd gone black. On the bottom of the pool, besides that ninth-layer just now, lay two sets of white bones, one still clutching a rusted knife.

Yinyue watched a long time and said: "White body, red head, like a turtle... When this queen was young, I saw a nest of them on a mountain, palm-sized, and the mountain folk picked up their shells and hung them under the roof beams. They said it kept fire away. A palm-sized one grown to that, it's probably older than this queen... A [gui]{注=gui}. There's a book called the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*{典=shanhai-gui} that says of it, 'it is shaped like a turtle, with a white body and a red head,' and 'it can be used to ward off fire.'"

A pebble rolled down from the cliff and landed on its shell; it didn't move. A woodlouse crawled out of the rockfall and crawled right past under its nose; it didn't move either. The woodlouse reached the edge of the pool and touched the water, and the head came out with a *crack*. Every so often it put its own head down into the pool, reached the bottom, and pulled back. The water was only a hand deep, and on the stones round the pool were water marks, ring above ring, the highest level with the top of its shell. It was dying of thirst. The new crack in the shell's rim was oozing black fluid. Xiaoman looked at the shallow hollow again: oval, pointed at one end, a small dip on each of its four sides, the same size as that iron pot. He thought: what this wall wants is that whole weight of it.

He couldn't beat it. He had seen that sleeve-sweep on the riverbed; the man who swept the sleeve had made one mouthful for it. His father used to say: "What you can't beat, you don't fight. You hang a deadfall on it."

He waited until the head had pulled in and settled, then slid down out of his crack, crept along the cliff foot to the rockfall, and picked up the sword. Not a notch on the blade. He climbed onto the stone eave above the hollow, took off the strap of his bundle, and lashed the sword point-down under a rock as big as a bushel. He set the rock on the lip of the eave, only half on, tied one end of the strap to the rock and let the other end hang down and tied it to his bamboo water tube. He slid down, pulled the tube's stopper, and poured three days' water into the hollow, every drop, and set the tube in the bottom of the hollow. Yinyue said: "That's your water for three days." Xiaoman said: "There's air behind the wall, and there's damp in the air. Where there's damp, there's water."

At the sound of water, the head came out. It sniffed, then pushed up that iron pot on its four legs and, a step and a shuffle at a time, shuffled into the hollow. The shell settled in without a gap, and inside the wall there was a long muffled *rumble*. It stretched its neck far out to reach the tube at the bottom of the hollow. The strap went tight; the rock on the eave tipped over, sword point down, and drove straight into the crack in the shell's rim near the neck.

It didn't die. It was a thing that had lived who knew how many hundred years. Taking that blow, the whole iron pot heaved up half a foot, and the head whipped round and clamped onto the left forearm of Xiaoman, who was crouched at the edge of the hollow. The teeth went into the flesh, down to the bone, and a jerk pulled him over onto the shell's rim. The jaws didn't let go. The head was pulling back into the shell, to drag his arm in with it. Xiaoman couldn't pull his arm free, and he didn't use any cultivation either. The sword hilt was right beside its neck, less than a foot from that mouth. He braced both feet on the rim of the hollow, gripped the hilt with his right hand, and bore down with his whole body's weight. An inch, then another inch. The jaws bit tighter and tighter, and then, little by little, loosened.

The wall split down the middle, and air came pouring out of the crack into his face, warm as spring. Xiaoman prised the jaws apart, pulled his arm out, tore a strip off the front of his jacket and bound it; the blood soaked through in moments. He looked at the overturned bamboo tube at the bottom of the hollow, not a drop left in it, and said: "That was three days' water." He paused, then said: "It lived in water for hundreds of years. And I tricked it with water." It was a long while before Yinyue said: "It was the door bolt... This time it won't come out again." In other years the pool had been full and it wasn't thirsty, and in all its life it had never moved into that hollow; the people before, when they saw it, either fought it or tried to lever it; this door had never once been opened.

Just then came footsteps overhead, and gravel trickled down in spatters. "—I told you he came this way!"

---

Xiaoman looked up. A row of people stood at the top of the cliff, six of them exactly. The tubby one in the middle, whose new cultivator's robe looked brand new even in the dark, was Ma Xiaobao; the five behind him were the five from that night in the woodshed. The two map sellers were among them, and Old Wilt was among them, and the big coil of hemp rope had come off his shoulder and was in the hands of the hanger-on with folded arms. Ma Xiaobao looked down on him: "Shen Xiaoman, did you think if you hid down here I wouldn't find you?" He jerked his chin at the hanger-on behind him. "The moment you came through the door, someone ran and told me. So you know quality when you see it. That wall, it's open?" Xiaoman stood in front of the wall and said nothing. Ma Xiaobao glanced at the black mouth of the drop by Xiaoman's feet and looked back, and his voice wasn't loud any more: "In a secret realm, a person or two dying is nothing new. Ten years ago three went down and one came up. This year one fewer comes up, and nobody will say a word." With that, he led the five of them down the cliff face.

Gravel clattered, and the six of them reached the ledge. His left arm was bitten through by the gui, and the bone under his ribs still hadn't knitted. He swung his right fist and hit back just once, and caught one of them; then the shoe with the black-patched toe came kicking in, right on the bone that hadn't knitted. He went down on his face and couldn't push himself up again. Ma Xiaobao didn't lift a hand. He stood at the back and watched. Old Wilt stood at the very back and didn't raise a foot. Ma Xiaobao jerked his chin at the drop: "Push him over. If he grabs the edge, tie his hands and push him again." Two of them dragged him by the feet towards the drop, and the one with folded arms squatted to undo the coil of rope.

Only now did he really understand: this time, they meant to kill him.

Yinyue didn't yawn. She was inside his robe, and her voice was low and quick: "The stone by your left hand. Press it down." His hand clawed at the ground and found a round stone half buried in the earth. On its face were nine small shallow pits, three across and three down; eight of them had pebbles set in them, and the one in the middle was empty. He had no time to ask. With his last breath of strength he pressed down on it with his palm. The stone sank.

At once a ring of faint light lit up on the cliff, bluish-white, from the foot of the cliff right up to the top, as if someone had taken an enormous brush and drawn a circle on that cliff. As the circle lit, the six on the ledge felt their feet go light, as if a great invisible hand had scooped them up, and one after another they were slapped against the cliff face, arms and legs spread, faces to the stone. However they struggled and kicked, they couldn't move half an inch. The one nearest the ledge had his toes a foot short of the ground. The coil of rope slipped from the folded-arms hanger-on's hands, spilled loose ring after ring, and hung in mid-air, and didn't move either. Ma Xiaobao was stuck in the very middle and could only squint down sideways. He yelled: "You—what did you do!"

Xiaoman didn't answer. He lay on the ground and for a long while couldn't get up. Yinyue said in his ear: "A trapping array left by the ones before, to guard this wall. The moment it starts, it slaps everyone inside it onto the cliff, except the one who pressed the array's eye; after that, it holds whoever's on the cliff. Leave it alone and it'll hold them till the secret realm closes. In three days, an hour before the stone door shuts, it lets go by itself. The array's eye is right under your hand. Nine pits, the middle one missing. Press the middle and it opens now."

Ma Xiaobao struggled a long time, struggled till his head ran with sweat, and the fierceness in his face melted bit by bit, and his voice went soft: "Sh... Junior Brother Shen. We're from the same village. Let us down and I'll give you ten taels of silver." Xiaoman said nothing. Ma Xiaobao said: "Twenty!" Xiaoman still said nothing. Ma Xiaobao strained for a long time and said: "Then... then the Common Fund, you don't have to pay it any more!"

Xiaoman raised his head and looked at them one by one. At the one with folded arms, at the one who had kicked his right ribs, at the coil of rope hanging in mid-air, at Ma Xiaobao. Last of all he looked at the one nearest the drop. Old Wilt was stuck at the very outer edge of the cliff, half his body hanging over that black mouth. He hadn't made a sound. He only looked down at him from the corner of his eye.

He knew how to open it. The empty pit in the middle; he had only to reach out one finger. But open meant all six came down together. This array didn't know who had said "That's enough," or who had brought the rope.

Xiaoman took his hand off the stone, got up with a hand on the wall, and said: "Three days."

Having said it, he turned and walked into the wall.

Behind him, Ma Xiaobao swore first, swore at his ancestors, swore at his mother, and the swearing turned at last into begging, and the begging at last into nothing you could make out. Old Wilt didn't say a word. Xiaoman walked through the crack in the wall, one step, two, his feet unsteady, as if he were walking on cotton. In his life he'd shot hares, he'd shot river deer, and this was the first time he had left six people stuck on a cliff. One of them, a few hours before, had said in his ear: "The rope's for you."

---

Behind the wall was a stone chamber, square and even, its four walls of bluestone polished smooth enough to show your reflection. No spirit herbs, no magic tools, no bones of the people before, clean as clean, as if someone had swept it yesterday. In the north-west corner, at floor level, was a black hole half a man high, and the air outside the wall was drawn in through it, gust by gust. In the middle stood a stone table, and on it a jade box. But when Xiaoman came in, the first thing he saw was not the jade box. On the floor of the chamber, three paces from the table, were four pits.

Four square pits, one on each side, set in a square, each the width of a bowl's mouth and a foot or so deep, their bottoms worn smooth and their rims worn shining, with a ring of moss green almost to black, and under the moss a faint glimmer of green light. Up close there was a cool smell of copper. His father had taught him to read hoofprints: a deep print means the thing is heavy; a print worn shiny means the thing stood there a long, long time. Something very, very heavy, on four legs, had stood until it made pits in the stone. And then it had gone.

Xiaoman crouched, spread his hand, put his thumb to the outer edge of one pit, and measured span by span to the outer edge of the next, the way hunters always measure hoofprints. One span, two spans, three... seven spans. The other three sides, each one, seven spans. He remembered what the old steward at the second trial had said: a thousand men went down into the Si to dredge for the cauldron and didn't find it. In this chamber the spirit qi was so thick it wouldn't dissolve, while outside it was as thin as pot-scrubbing water. Standing among the four empty pits, for the first time he had a faint sense that those two things might be one thing.

On the four walls of the chamber there were carvings you could only see up close, very shallow, very fine. The east wall was water, wave curling over wave, with houses floating in it, and people floating with their arms reaching up. The south wall was a man carrying a basket on his back, taking something out of the basket and scattering it on the water, and wherever it was scattered, patches of land rose up from under the water. The man's back was bent, as if he were carrying something very, very heavy. The west wall was nine things in three rows, three to a row, every one square, with four legs below and two ears. The north wall was empty, carved all over with shallow squares with nothing in them; and right at the bottom, against the floor, was one very small carving, cut deeper and newer than all the rest: a fox, curled up, its tail wrapped round its body, exactly like the one on the wooden tablet on his chest. Yinyue jumped down from his shoulder and went up to the carved fox, lowered her head and touched it with her nose, her tail hanging, then jumped back onto his shoulder, and didn't say a word.

He went to the stone table and opened the jade box. When the lid came up, all the spirit qi in the room sank gently towards the box's mouth, like a roomful of water finding the low place. In the box there was only **a pinch of earth**. Yellow-brown, loose, just enough to cover the bottom, no different from the earth in that patch behind his family's house where nothing would grow. Xiaoman stared at it a long time and said: "...That's it?" On his shoulder Yinyue said, one word at a time: "[Living soil]{注=xirang}." Her voice was shaking, and the fur at the tip of her tail stood up hair by hair. "Earth that grows by itself. Give it a grain and it grows a pinch; give it a pinch and it grows a mountain." Xiaoman asked: "Air behind that wall: you knew all along it was in here?" It was a while before she turned her head back to him, her golden eyes bright and a little wet: "...This queen could only smell that there was something behind the wall that grows. What it was, this queen couldn't remember. This queen only knew that you're a leaky ladle." She turned her head away again, and her voice was low, as if talking to herself: "A leaky ladle, sitting on the woodpile every night till past midnight, leaking all night, then sitting again. A whole year of it... This queen got sick of watching." Xiaoman wanted to say something, and what came to mind was, again, that word "thanks," owed on the woodpile on Mount Meng and owed to this day, not yet repaid.

Her nose twitched twice more and she sneezed. When she opened her mouth, the air in the room went pouring into it. "The air in this room is as strong as a jar of old wine," she said. "This queen is going to sleep. Nobody disturb me." The words were barely out when she turned into a wisp of silver light and went back into the tablet.

---

He closed the jade box and sat cross-legged in front of the stone table to wait for her to wake. He waited as long as a meal takes, and as long again. She hadn't said what the earth was for, so he didn't touch it. The chamber was so quiet he could hear the air in the black hole in the north-west corner: a draw, a pause.

---

Meanwhile, in two places. In the black ground at the end of the underground river, Ahe dug out the third grain, and at the corner of her eye something lit up. Not firelight: spiritual power. The first thing her Master had taught her was to see spiritual power. From the garden end, a mass of spiritual power was coming along the edge of the underground river, a thick layer of it on a person, heavy enough to press you down; and in one hand it held a ball of fire, red going on purple. The yellow robe from the riverbed with the red-copper gourd. Ninth layer, far above her. Meet him head on and she was dead. She closed her hand on the three grains, took from her sleeve the first of the three talismans her Master had pressed on her (the concealing talisman), stuck it over her heart, and she was gone, shadow and all. She slid sideways into a crack a foot wide at the edge of the black ground, breathing lighter than a blade of grass.

It was him, as she'd thought. Those three little oil lamps, he saw at once. The older disciple who had won that spirit stone at finger-guessing in the garden came pouncing from the other end too, his hand on the herb first. The yellow robe pushed with his right thumb, the copper plug came out, a fire snake wrapped once round the man's neck, and the man got out half a scream. The fire snake went back in; he glanced down at the gourd's mouth, and only then pushed the plug back in. Ahe watched it all from the crack, piece by piece: the spiritual power on him was very thick, but to kill he didn't spend a shred of his own; he used only the fire in the gourd. The shield hung on his left arm, the gourd sat in his right hand. In the moment of pulling the plug and pushing it back, his eyes had to leave the other person: that was when he was slowest. He pulled the purple-whisker lingzhi up root and all, and that ridge of grain turned into a mess of mud. He went through the dead man's robe, then swept his spirit sense over the ground: the chill of it slid over the crack and didn't stop. As if remembering something, he smiled: "That coil of rope at the door..." He had seen that coil of rope on the riverbed. Nobody went to the Deadly Cliff, and places nobody went were where things were; a coil of rope going where nobody went was going to tie something up. He tucked the gourd back at his waist and set off towards the stone hall, walking fast.

Ahe waited until she couldn't hear his footsteps and then crawled out. The talisman over her heart had turned to a pinch of ash. She wrapped the three black grains in a small piece of cloth, pushed them into her empty bamboo tube and sealed it with wax. Rope, Deadly Cliff, Shen Xiaoman. Go after him, and it was a fourth layer running into a ninth: she'd die and the message wouldn't arrive. Shout, and across half the secret realm, nobody would hear. She didn't go towards the stone hall. She went down into the underground river: the cave from the old records that blew warm air outwards was halfway up the cliff of the river channel. If the person at the bottom of the Deadly Cliff came to the end of his road and went into the wind, here was where he'd come out. Ahe felt her way to below the warm air, lay down behind a rock with her sleeve over her mouth and nose, and waited patiently. Lying there, she was stiller than the rock.

---

"Earth that grows by itself?"

Xiaoman spun round. In the crack in the wall he'd come in by (the door the gui's dead body had pressed open), there stood, who knew since when, a man in an ochre-yellow robe, fair-faced, all smiles, the red-copper gourd in his right hand. When he came, his feet hadn't made a sound.

Zou Qingsong was in no hurry to come in. First he looked all round the rims of the four pits, at the ring of moss, looked at it until his eyes narrowed to slits, like a starving dog that smells a meat bone. His throat moved; he swallowed. "[Cauldron moss]{注=dingtai}. The chief herb of the Foundation Pill. Pill-masters outside never see a single strand in their lives." Only then did he raise his eyes to Xiaoman, and his smile deepened. "Little brother, you opened the door for me, you dealt with the gui for me, you stuck those six up there for me. I followed behind and didn't waste a single step." He raised a finger and pointed up the cliff. "Those gentlemen up there have been crying for their mothers half the day. Did you hear? I found it rather fun."

Zou Qingsong glanced at the front of Xiaoman's robe, and his voice went soft, soft as if coaxing a child: "Put the box down." He paused. "As for you—" He tilted his head and thought, as if working out a very, very small sum. "The fat one in the stone hall, you saw him. He went quickly. Didn't suffer. I'm a soft-hearted man."

Xiaoman tucked the jade box into his robe and took the bow off his back. This time he didn't think of hiding.

First read the wind, then the deer. For the first time he put the whole strength of the second layer of Qi Condensation into one bow. The strip of jacket round his left arm went red through all at once, and the bow bent fuller than any time his father had ever drawn it. An arrow straight for the face. Zou Qingsong raised his left arm; *clang*, the arrowhead struck the small red-copper shield on his left arm and bounced off. The flame pattern on the shield didn't show so much as a white scratch. Zou Qingsong laughed: "Second-layer trash. Don't bother struggling."

Before the second arrow was on the string, Zou Qingsong flicked his left hand and a fireball got there first. Xiaoman jerked his head aside; the fireball went past his ear and singed a lock of hair. The gourd's plug was already out, and a fire snake came lunging. What he watched wasn't the snake but the gourd's mouth: wherever the gourd's mouth tilted, the snake went. At the first lunge he rolled left; at the second he jumped right; at the third he leapt, but his left side couldn't keep up. The snake's tail lashed right across his left ribs, *crack*, exactly like that night of the twelfth of the tenth month behind the woodshed. The tongue of flame licked through his jacket and into the flesh. He flew sideways and hit the stone table, everything went white, his mouth was full of the taste of blood, and his left side felt as if it had been shoved into a stove. He rolled to the north-west corner; the half-man-high black hole was right by his hand, air drawn into it gust by gust. In his ears was Ahe's line: go into the wind; at the other end of the wind there's a way out. He dived in head first.

It was a wind tunnel, slanting downwards, steep and slippery, pitch black. He rolled down it, the jade box in his robe digging into his broken rib. He didn't know how long he rolled. Then all at once there was nothing underneath him; he shot out of a cave mouth halfway up a cliff face, fell twelve or fifteen feet in the dark, and landed, *thump*, on something soft.

It was a dead man.

---

It was the underground river, dry. The cave mouth he'd shot out of was halfway up the cliff, blowing warm air outwards in gusts. In the secret realm there was no sky to see, only a few patches of moss in the rock cracks giving off a faint green glow. Under him was one of the abbey's grey robes, long cold, one hand gripping a short knife with two notches in its edge, the other clenched in a fist with a little green showing between the fingers. Before he had his breath back, a hand reached out of the dark and covered his mouth.

The hand was cold, there was earth under its nails, and its cuff smelled of dry hay from the herb garden. He knew it. He went still. A breath came into his ear, finer than a mosquito, just two lines: "He kills only with the gourd." "Pulling the plug and putting it back, that's when he's slowest." The hand let go; there was a rustling in the dark, and then no sound at all.

Xiaoman lay where he was and didn't move. He could hear: on his left, someone panting, very low; a dozen paces ahead, two people, one grinding his teeth; behind a rock on the right, one more. Four people, round a clump of fine grass in the middle of the channel, all crouched in the dark. It was [dragon-whisker grass]{注=longxu-cao}, which grew only in this underground river. The four of them had felt their way to the same spot, and none would move first. Whoever reached out first was the first to be heard.

From the cave mouth overhead, someone dropped down and landed very lightly. No firelight: Zou Qingsong had stoppered the gourd. He was someone who crouched in the dark too, and knew whoever lit up first died first. His spirit sense swept over; cauldron qi seeped from the rock cracks of the channel and spirit herbs grew there, and wherever it swept there was light everywhere, and the crouching people were mixed into it and couldn't be told apart. He had to find them by ear.

Xiaoman got hold of the short knife. The dead man's fingers had stiffened, and he prised them open one by one. The man was coming through the dark step by step, searching. His steps Xiaoman could hear; the gourd he could smell, a whiff of sulphur, front right, less than ten paces. First read the wind, then the deer. In the dark you can't see wind, you can't see deer; there's only listening left. You can't shoot a man in the dark. But he killed only with the gourd, and when the gourd opened, there'd be light. Xiaoman took out the last two arrows from his quiver, nocked one, felt for a pebble on the ground with his left hand, and tossed it off into the dark on the left.

*Tok.*

The copper plug went *pop*, a ball of red light lit up in the gourd's mouth, and a fire snake lunged at the *tok* and hit nothing. In that flash Xiaoman saw it: ten paces off, a red-copper gourd, and by its mouth two fingers holding the plug. First read the wind, then the deer. This time the deer had lit itself up. He sent an arrow straight at the red light.

*Clang.* The arrow grazed the gourd's mouth and struck the two fingers, and the copper plug went flying and rolled off into the dark. Fire spouted from the gourd's mouth and lit Zou Qingsong up bright as day: a man in a yellow robe holding a fire-spouting gourd, standing in the very middle of the channel. The plug was gone; it couldn't go back in. He threw all his spiritual power down hard onto the gourd's mouth, and half the fire went back in, but the other half kept leaping from the mouth, as if he were holding up a torch. He was stuck, the whole of him, in that moment between pulling the plug and putting it back, lit up there. Xiaoman pinched his voice and shouted into the dark, shrill and scared: "The Red Pine one's carrying treasure—" By the time he'd finished he had already rolled a dozen paces away, rolled past the clump of grass and plucked a stalk on the way. Before his shout had died away, from the other side of the dark, a dozen paces off, another voice took it up, pitched low and thick and hoarse, like a man half mad with hunger: "Treasure in the gourd, give me a share—"

The four crouching in the dark heard "treasure," one shout at this end, one at that; and what they saw was one man, one ball of fire, lit up all by himself. The tooth-grinder sprang out first, and the other three sprang after him.

Xiaoman didn't see all of the next few moments. Blades rang on the shield, *clang, clang, clang*, sparks spattering everywhere, and Zou Qingsong was still laughing: "Below Core Formation—" Before the laugh was done, out of the dark at his feet a green vine shot up, *whsst*, its root trailing a long way back into the dark, and wound round and round his shield arm. A vine couldn't hold a ninth-layer's arm, but all his spiritual power was pressing down on the gourd's mouth, and to break free he'd have to let go at one end first. What the vine held was the space of that one breath. In that half beat, the vine hauled his shield arm outwards, a strip of body showed under the shield's rim, and the body-guarding light on his left arm dimmed with it, like a lamp flame wavering in the wind. In that waver a sword slipped in and drove into his upper left arm. Zou Qingsong didn't make a sound. The spiritual power pressing the gourd's mouth slackened, the fire poured back out of the gourd and burned all the way along that vine into the dark. Somewhere in the dark someone sucked in a breath, very short, and bit it off. After that came the hiss of fire snakes, a cry cut in half, another, then the sound of someone running into the dark and falling before he'd gone ten paces. Of the four, not one got out a second shout. In the firelight, blood dripped down Zou Qingsong's sleeve from his upper left arm, and his face was white as paper; the shield lay across his chest, gleaming as ever, not a single white scratch. He swept a fire snake across the dark where the vine had burned back, and what it swept up was only a strip of scorched earth. He didn't leave. He squatted and went through the four men's robes one by one, and found two spirit stones and a silver bangle; then, finger by finger, he prised open the fist of the dead man under the wind tunnel and dug out a pinch of moss; and that clump of grass he pulled up root and all. He put each thing away carefully before he stood up.

Xiaoman was already at the foot of the cliff. He had the short knife between his teeth and was climbing, fingers dug into the cracks. One rib broken, his left arm no use; every foot he climbed, everything went white for a moment. In one crack his fingers closed on something round, warm, beating, beating. He gripped it and pushed it into his robe. As he reached the cave mouth, the firelight below swung round and Zou Qingsong looked up: the one who had shot away his plug, with the box he wanted in his robe, was hanging halfway up the cliff. A fire snake came racing up the cliff face after him and licked his calf, and his trouser leg caught fire. He looked down into the dark below and could see nothing. The fire was after him, not her. He rolled into the wind tunnel, buried his leg in the dirt, made no sound, and crawled upwards on hands and feet.

---

He rolled back out of the black hole in the north-west corner into the stone chamber and didn't stop. In one breath he burst out of the crack in the wall and onto the ledge. The ring of bluish-white light on the cliff was still lit, and Ma Xiaobao's six were still stuck on it. Xiaoman stood with his back to the foot of the cliff. The trapping array held whoever was on the cliff: if the man came out after him, one jump up the cliff and he'd never come down.

Zou Qingsong came out of the crack in the wall, his left arm hanging. The smile was gone from his face, only white left. He looked at the cliff behind Xiaoman, looked at the six stuck on it, and gave a cold laugh: "What that array holds, I watched from behind a rock for the time of an incense stick. I know better than you." He set the shield across his chest and raised the fire-spouting gourd. "This time I'm done playing with you."

The gourd shook, the fire came out and split four ways. Four fire snakes, one from the left, one from the right, one along the ground straight at him, one curling round through the dark beyond the drop to come from behind, all lunging at once. Behind was the cliff, and on the cliff the array; in front was fire; underfoot three or four paces of ledge. Either way was death. Someone on the cliff cried out.

His hand went to his belt. In the lining was a little folded square, a sheet of yellow paper: the crooked horse the Rice Bucket had spat out. Yinyue thought it ugly. The line of rice-grain writing at the talisman's corner he had read dozens of times (*tread it underfoot and ride away at once*) and never once understood; he just couldn't bring himself to throw it away. Now this one line was all that was left to trust. He slapped it onto the sole of his left foot and, with all the strength in his body, stamped on the ground.

The sole of his foot went hot, like stepping on a coal. His whole body left the ground, three feet up, and shot sideways skimming the earth; behind him the four fire snakes slammed into one another. In mid-air he had no say in where he landed. He cleared the little pool and came down, *thud*, sitting square on top of a great iron-black pot: the dead gui, still lying in the hollow at the foot of the wall with the sword in its neck. Then he rolled off the shell and down behind it. The four fire snakes turned and came after him, and all of them slammed into that "iron pot" together. *Tsss*: like four ladles of water thrown into a stove. A puff of white steam, and they were out.

*It can be used to ward off fire.*

Zou Qingsong stood with his mouth open, looked at the dead fire, looked at the dead gui, looked at Xiaoman huddled behind the turtle shell, and his voice cracked: "Where did you get that hell-for-leather charm!?"

Xiaoman couldn't answer. He'd only just found out himself. The talisman on his sole had already turned to a pinch of yellow ash.

He took out of his bundle the wooden Grass-Mud Horse the Rice Bucket had spat out and set it on the ground. It stretched its neck, fixed on its master, and set off after *him* at a gallop on its four wooden legs. Xiaoman turned and ran, round the little pool, away from Zou Qingsong for all he was worth, swearing as he ran: "You traitor lump of wood! Not after the enemy, after me! I suppose you know he's the one with the fire, is that it..."

Zou Qingsong gave a great shout: "Where do you think you're going! You think riding a horse makes you faster than your granddad's escape art? Watch this—" And as he said it he made the hand-sign for a fire escape. Quick as the telling, in less than a breath he was within three feet. A ninth-layer's escape art was no gift for nothing. In an instant a fireball had formed at his fingertip. This close, Xiaoman had nowhere to run, and said: "So this is how I die."

Just then the wooden Grass-Mud Horse seemed to sense something; as if it couldn't bear the heat of the fireball at that fingertip, there was a huge *boom*, and it burst into powder all at once, and a mushroom cloud rose up and hid everything.

When the smoke and fire cleared: Xiaoman, who was further off, had only been knocked head over heels and rolled a dozen feet. He got up with a layer of black on his face but no new wounds on his body. Zou Qingsong wasn't so lucky. The blast had gone off right under his fingertip, and his shield was still on his left arm; he hadn't had time to raise it. The body-guarding light could stop a blade from outside; it couldn't stop the fire at his own fingertip. He staggered back three steps and sat down hard. Half his yellow robe was burned through, his eyebrows and hair singed clean off, his fair face smoked black, only his two eyes blinking; the five fingers of his right hand were charred like five sticks of charcoal, and when he opened his mouth he breathed out a puff of black smoke. The shield hung on his arm, gleaming as ever.

Zou Qingsong got up off the ground and wiped his face, and wiped off a handful of black ash. He looked at his five charcoal-stick fingers, and his eyes went red. He tucked the empty gourd back at his waist and laughed through his teeth: "Good. Very good."

Xiaoman didn't wait for him to get his breath back. He went at him with the short knife, feinting left; Zou Qingsong's shield came up to the left after it (that sword wound in his upper left arm was still bleeding, and the shield came up half a beat slow), and Xiaoman had already twisted round to his right. The shield hung on the left arm. On the right was the burned hand. The short knife went in, at his right ribs—

Zou Qingsong didn't even dodge. Half an inch before the point reached his side, a layer of pale yellow light lit up there with a hum, thin as a sheet of oiled paper. The point struck it and skidded off, *tsst*, slitting only a strip of yellow robe. Body-guarding light. A short knife, with a second layer's strength behind it, couldn't so much as touch his skin. Zou Qingsong glanced down and laughed: "Tickling me?" The burned hand closed into a fist, the whole of a ninth layer's spiritual power wrapped round it, and he drove it into Xiaoman's left ribs, right where the bone was broken. Xiaoman flew sideways and landed, *smack*, by the little pool, half his body in the water. There was sweetness in his mouth, and he spat a mouthful of blood onto the water, where it slowly spread.

He tried to crawl and couldn't get up. The water was so cold it hurt his bones. So cold his head cleared.

Zou Qingsong came over step by step and raised his right hand, his five charred fingers making a fire sign. A spark puffed up at the fingertip and went out; he made it again, it sparked again, went out again. That hand had been blasted ruined, and fire wouldn't form. He looked down at his hand, and under the layer of black ash it was turning blue, bit by bit. "Good. Good," he said again. He made the hand-sign for a fire escape, became a red streak, and in one breath was at the pool.

That was the moment Xiaoman had been waiting for. The instant the red streak landed, the hand he had in the water came up hard and threw a double handful of pool water, mud and grit and all, into that face. The fire wrapped round the fire escape went *tsss* and sent up a great cloud of white steam, and the mud and grit plastered the light in front of his face, plastered it all over. Everything went black for Zou Qingsong and he reeled back. Xiaoman shot up out of the water and swept the short knife upwards, at his throat—

The point swept across his throat; the light flashed with a squeal, like scraping a stone, and it skidded off. Eyes shut, going by the sound of the water, Zou Qingsong swept the shield's rim across, right onto the wrist of Xiaoman's knife hand, *crack*, and the knife nearly left his grip. Zou Qingsong wiped the mud from his face and came at him, set the shield's rim against Xiaoman's throat and drove him all the way back, and pinned him to the wall.

In the dark beyond the crack in the wall, someone went "Shh," soft as a breath of wind.

A handful of grain came scattering out of the dark into the cracks in the rock under Zou Qingsong's heels. The cracks were still pooled with the water Xiaoman had thrown, seeping with the secret realm's cauldron qi. The grains fell in, and there was a crackling, *pip-pip-pop*, like beans roasting in a pan, as one grain after another burst into sprout. A sprout shot up a foot, and a foot became ten; green vines came pushing out of the cracks one after another, wound round his ankles, wound up his calves, and hauled backwards.

Zou Qingsong swayed, and the shield's rim slackened half an inch. Half an inch was enough. Xiaoman dragged in a breath and choked on it, eyes streaming.

Zou Qingsong looked down at his feet, then up into the dark beyond the crack in the wall, and laughed: "That vine down in the underground river, that was you, wasn't it? Burned once and you still haven't learned." He raised the burned hand and made a sign. A spark at the fingertip, then out. The smile on his face stiffened. He couldn't burn them, so he used strength: a ninth-layer's legs heaved, and the vines snapped, *snap snap*. For every one that snapped, two more pushed out of the cracks, like chives you can never cut to the end. He gave up on Xiaoman altogether, turned round, and dragging a legful of vines went step by step towards the crack in the wall: "Come out. I can smell you. A smell of grain."

Xiaoman slid down the wall and sat on the floor, getting his breath back a gasp at a time.

Out of the crack in the wall flew a yellow talisman, *whsst*. The cinnabar on it was drawn not as fire but as points, one under another, falling downwards: an ice-spike talisman, high grade, the second of her Master's three. It lit in mid-air and burst with a cracking rattle into dozens of ice spikes a foot or so long, white and glinting, carrying the cold of a river's surface in the eleventh month, and drove at Zou Qingsong from every side.

Zou Qingsong swung the shield across. The ice spikes struck it one after another, *ting ting tang*, and shattered; but the shattered ice didn't fall. The cold crept outwards across the shield's face. A layer of white frost formed on it, and the frost crept up his left arm, up his shoulder, down to his waist, down his legs, and together with the legful of green vines and the pool of water at his feet it froze, *creak, creak*, into one lump, wrapped round the outside of his body-guarding light like a shell of ice fitted onto him, freezing the whole of him fast to the rock with only his head and neck left out. One spike had got past the shield's rim into the sword wound in his upper left arm, half an inch in, and frozen there. Zou Qingsong sucked in his breath, *sss*.

His hand couldn't form fire, so he made the sign for a fire escape again. Inside the ice shell a layer of red light rose up with a *whoosh*, and the ice melted bit by bit, melted into water that ran down the shell, *drip, drip*. But the ice had been frozen by a high-grade talisman; when one layer melted there was another under it, and it wouldn't melt through in a hurry. He gave a cold laugh: "How long can you hold me?"

Xiaoman didn't wait for him to break out. He got up from the foot of the wall and went at him with the short knife. The man was frozen there and couldn't dodge; the ice came up to his neck, so Xiaoman picked the places with no ice and stabbed. He tried several vital spots, and every stroke skidded off. The man was frozen, but that light wasn't frozen. The knife's edge lost another tooth. Zou Qingsong, frozen there, watched him stab and didn't even blink: "Stab away. When you're tired of stabbing, I'll finish you."

Layer by layer the ice shell thinned, and water spread all over the ground at his feet.

Another talisman flew out of the crack in the wall: the last of the three. The cinnabar on it showed a mountain, three strokes across and one down: a mountain-pressing talisman. It slapped onto the crown of Zou Qingsong's head and lit, and the whole of him sank, as if someone had set a mountain on his shoulders. The ice under his feet froze solid again with a *crack*, and his knees bent. Zou Qingsong set his teeth, the veins on his neck standing out one by one, and an inch at a time he straightened his back again. The light on the talisman flickered, flickered, and dimmed.

"Two," he said, laughing through his panting. "Which old fogey at the abbey could bear to give a fourth-layer something this good? Any more? Bring them out."

There weren't any more.

No more sound came from the crack in the wall. Xiaoman stood three paces in front of him gripping the notched knife, water dripping from its point. He'd cut, he'd thrown water, he'd run, he'd shouted; the horse was used, the talisman was used, the talismans Ahe kept at the bottom of her chest were used. He had nothing left on him. He remembered what his mother had said, poking the fire: if you can't beat it, run, there's no shame in running. Behind him was the cliff, on the cliff the array. There was nowhere to run.

For the first time he saw death so clearly: three paces away, a smoke-blackened face, hung all over with ice splinters, smiling. Beyond the crack in the wall, that trembling breath stopped trembling too, and held.

There was only a thin layer of ice shell left, clear as glass, the red light under it pulsing. Zou Qingsong tipped back his blackened face and laughed out loud, ice splinters pattering off him, the laugh bouncing round the foot of the cliff: "Ha... hahahaha! A second layer, a fourth layer, and a few weird little tricks! You think the likes of you—" He laughed until he couldn't breathe, his eyeballs so red they looked ready to drip blood. "Twenty years in the Red Pine Abbey, climbing layer by layer to the ninth, killed so many people I can't keep count myself; and today two brats burn my face and freeze my legs. Good, good! You first. Then into the crack in the wall to drag that little rat out, and finish her in front of you, an inch at a time."

Only a thread of light was left on the talisman. Zou Qingsong stopped laughing and heaved his shoulders up, and the thin ice under his feet split with a *crack*. He raised the shield and went to step forward, but the last frozen ring of vine round his ankle still held, and it jerked him to a halt.

Just then Yinyue came out of Xiaoman's robe and crouched on his shoulder, thin as a layer of frost. Without even lifting her eyelids, she pulled two hairs from her one tail and flicked them lightly at the shield: "Core Formation?"

The shield split down the middle with a *crack* and broke into seven or eight pieces. The two fox hairs didn't slow at all; they went through his body-guarding light like going through a paper window, nailed into Zou Qingsong's two legs, and threw the whole of him backwards. The ice vines at his feet all snapped at once, and he crashed into the fallen rocks at the foot of the cliff. His two legs bent where legs shouldn't bend. He lay sprawled there, still breathing. The red-copper gourd rolled to the edge of the drop and stopped.

Yinyue crouched on Xiaoman's shoulder, gave the tail she'd pulled the hairs from a flick, looked at him with her golden eyes, and said nothing.

---

Xiaoman got up with a hand on the wall and coughed up two mouthfuls of blood. The short knife was still in his grip.

Zou Qingsong lay sprawled under the fallen rocks. When he saw him coming he bared his teeth, frothing blood: "...I've got spirit stones in my robe. Half for you." Xiaoman didn't stop. He thought of the plump young gentleman's eyes at the corner of the stone hall, looking up at the hall's roof; of the four in the underground river who hadn't got out a second shout; of the trembling breath beyond the crack in the wall. This man had smelled her. If this man got out alive, Shi'ao Village, the Sunken Cauldron Abbey, the box in Xiaoman's robe, her beyond the crack in the wall: not one of them could stay hidden.

He crouched. When his father bled a deer, the knife went in under the root of the ear and was drawn outwards. He did as his father did.

His hand was clean about it. The shaking came after. He crouched there and his hand wouldn't stop shaking, and his ears were ringing. The feel of that cut going in, the heat running through his fingers, were exactly like the year he was twelve by the stream, when his father held his hand to bleed the deer. People and deer, it turned out, were the same. He turned his head away and retched a few times and brought nothing up.

Under Zou Qingsong's robe was tucked a small grey cloth bag, the size of a palm, with a talisman pattern round its mouth: a storage pouch, low grade. With its owner dead his mark had faded, and when Xiaoman's hand touched it, the mouth opened. Inside it was black and heavy; when he reached in, it went far deeper than the bag, like reaching into a well. The first thing he felt was a money purse, bulging: the plump young gentleman's seven stones, the two from the underground river, a silver bangle. Under the purse was another, heavier, its mouth tied with red string in three dead knots. This one was Zou Qingsong's own. Xiaoman undid the knots one by one and tipped it into his palm, and with a rush a handful of spirit stones poured out, twenty-three, every one bluish-white; and among them rolled one a size bigger than the rest, clear green right through like a spring frozen solid, with a thread of light in it turning slowly by itself, and each time it turned, the four walls of the chamber brightened with it. Xiaoman's eyes went fixed. The cool of it lay against his palm, seeping qi thread by thread into the lines of his hand, stronger than three months of his allowance put together. On his shoulder Yinyue said "Hm?" and the tip of her tail shot up: "A middle-grade spirit stone. One is worth a hundred of yours. That one, who knows whose body it was taken off." Xiaoman held the handful, and his hands began to shake. He counted the stones back one by one into the red-string purse, tied it, and put it with the other. There was more in the pouch: the clump of dragon-whisker grass and the pinch of moss from the underground river; a bottle of Spring-Return Pills, three of them; two breath-hiding talismans; a purple-whisker lingzhi with a clod of black earth round its roots. The empty gourd at the edge of the drop and the seven or eight pieces of shield on the ground were Red Pine Abbey things; carry them openly and someone on the mountain would know them, but he took them too. The gourd's plug had been shot off into the river channel; on the day they came out of the secret realm, he felt his way down the wind tunnel and fetched it back. He tied the storage pouch at his own waist.

"Scared?" said the voice on his shoulder, not loudly. Xiaoman didn't answer. Yinyue said: "Scared of what. This is how it is in this line of work. You don't kill him, he kills you; let him go today, and tomorrow it's you who dies, and the day after it's your mother. Hold the blade steady. Keep your heart a bit cold." She paused. "But you remember this for this queen: why you picked up that blade. Forget that, and you're him." Then she said: "That pinch of earth: scatter it where the cauldron stood, and sit in it. Your dantian is a patch of sand: however much rain falls, that much leaks away. Living soil is earth that grows by itself. Let it grow into your body, into a patch of deep ground. Deep ground holds water."

The words were barely out when she turned into a wisp of silver light and went back into the tablet. The tablet was icy cold. He turned it over: on its back there was a new crack, a fine one, and on the little curled fox, the one tail that was lit had dimmed by a length. He gripped the tablet and thought of Zou Qingsong's face, of his own knife going in and skidding off, stroke after stroke. What had shielded him today was two of her hairs and a crack; next time, how many hairs would she have left? He couldn't work it out. He could work out only one thing: next time, it had to be his own knife that got in.

He turned to look through the crack in the wall. Ahe was crouched in the north-west corner of the stone chamber, by the mouth of the wind tunnel, her right hand drawn up into her sleeve. The six stuck on the cliff had their faces to the stone and couldn't see her; but if she opened her mouth, they could hear. She came over bent low without a sound, made a sign with her left hand and pressed it to the ring of tooth marks on his left forearm, shut her eyes, and her lips moved: the first line of her Master's scripture. *Observe the way of Heaven; hold to the working of Heaven.* A layer of green spiritual power came through her palm, cool, like grass blades after rain against the skin. The green seeped into the tooth marks, and Xiaoman felt something nudging in the wound, itching, like a seed under the ground at the start of spring cracking its husk. The torn flesh reached thread by thread towards the middle, this side reaching that, like two rows of new shoots just through the soil; where they reached each other, they twined. The bleeding stopped. She moved to the burned patch under his ribs, and this time she pressed for longer, and the colour drained from her face bit by bit, and the green between her fingers faded bit by bit too, faded like tea left overnight. Under the burned skin a layer of new flesh nudged up, pink, so thin the light came through, not fully grown, and she took her hand away. It still hurt. She only saw to it that he wouldn't die. She drew back her hand, pointed at the wind tunnel, then at herself. He nodded. She crawled into the wind tunnel and went off towards the underground river without a sound.

A name tag hung at Zou Qingsong's waist. Xiaoman untied it and looked at the back: Red Pine Abbey of Pengcheng, Zou Qingsong. He'd heard the steward read it out on the riverbed. He dragged the man to the outer edge of the ledge, to the rusted iron peg, and pushed him over. The name tag was copper. He weighed it in his palm twice. Copper could be sold; but keep it, and it was a man's life hanging at his waist. He threw it over too. After a long, long time a sound came up from below. Very faint, like a drop of water.

He went down and searched the ninth-layer in the pool too. Inner-sect people didn't carry money into the secret realm; in his pouch there was only a bottle of Qi-Gathering Pills and a roll of the inner sect's map of the secret realm, and he took those. The empty pouch, and the name tag with it, he threw into the dark. The sword in the gui's neck he gripped by the hilt and pulled at twice, and it didn't budge.

The six stuck on the cliff, faces to the stone, didn't make a sound.

He went back inside the wall, took the jade box out of his robe, and opened it. The earth was still there, yellow-brown, loose, just covering the bottom. She'd said: sit in it. He scattered it in the middle of the four empty pits, where the cauldron had stood, ringed with cauldron moss, and sat on it cross-legged.

---

At first the earth didn't move. After he'd sat a while, it began to **grow**. It pushed up from under him, over the tops of his feet, over his knees, up to his waist, up to his chest, heaping into a warm mound with him buried inside it, like a seed planted. Out of the earth reached root after root; they went in at the base of his spine, pushed up into the dantian, and from the dantian pushed on into every corner of his body, into the meridians, into the bones, and last of all into the five doors. The five doors were his spirit roots, small and jumbled, like five holes poked any old how in a leaky ladle.

The roots pushed into the cracks of the doors and forced them outwards, and as they forced, earth grew under and around each door. The first door was metal: a *twang*, the string of his grandfather's bow. The second was wood, creaking, like the pines on Black Pine Ridge moving a step aside by themselves on a windless night. The third was water: a cold, the big wooden tub of that marrow-washing night; and once the cold was past it turned into a clear river flowing far, far away. The fourth was fire, so warm his nose stung: the fire in the stove, his mother stirring the firewood with the tongs. "If you can't beat it, run. There's no shame in running." The fifth was earth, the heaviest. Through its crack came first the rusty smell of the patch behind his family's house, fainter the wider it opened, and under it came another smell, a sweet one, like new soil turned with a hoe after the first good soaking rain of spring. Granny Wu had said it: back then, this ground smelled sweet.

Buried in the earth, he shook all over, swollen like a field after a soaking rain, and only clenched his jaw till his teeth rattled. He drew a breath. The spirit qi in the chamber moved, like a pool penned up for hundreds of years finding an outlet, and all of it surged towards him. Before, he drew a breath and half of it leaked away; underneath was sand. Now the five doors stood open, and under them a patch of deep ground had grown: a breath drawn was a breath kept. He wasn't a leaky ladle any more. He was a water vat. The rib on his left side first went hot, then numb, and when he pressed it, it didn't hurt. The burned patch under his ribs had scabbed over; the ring of tooth marks on his left forearm had closed too.

His hands were still shaking. In the wooden tablet, a very sleepy voice lazily recited a line from the *Book of Documents*{典=shangshu-hongfan}: "Water is said to soak and sink; fire is said to blaze and rise; wood is said to bend and straighten; metal is said to yield and change; earth is for sowing and reaping... Five tempers. Earth is what you can grow things in. Earth that grows nothing isn't called earth. Push them round once."

So he pushed. A force that pushed upwards rose from the dantian, turned a corner and went hot, the heat settled into warmth, the warmth hardened into the bones, then cooled and seeped back into the dantian: wood, fire, earth, metal, water, round once. He thought of the stove at home: wood burns to fire, fire burns to ash, the ash goes to feed the field, stalks grow in the field, the stalks are dried in the sun and stuffed back into the stove. What the book wrote line by line, his mother had done every day of her life. His hands stopped shaking.

The first night he perfected the second layer, and then the barrier of the third went *crack* inside him, like a split opening in ice. That night his throat burned with thirst. Past midnight, from a crack in the corner of the wall, there was a *plink*, and a thin spring seeped out. Air behind the wall, damp in the air; where there's damp, there's water. He hadn't miscalculated, only reckoned half a night too early. At noon on the second day, he broke through to the fourth layer.

---

On the second night, buried in the earth, he heard through the wall the sounds on the cliff outside.

On the first day there was still swearing on the cliff, still crying, and someone knocking his head against the rock. By the second night there was no more swearing, only panting. A secret realm in the eleventh month: the cliff was cold to the bone, and the six stuck on it had had nothing to eat or drink for a day and a night, only their tongues to lick the thread of water seeping down the cliff. Xiaoman, buried in the mound of earth inside the wall, the round of the five phases turning just evenly, could hear every breath outside.

First Ma Xiaobao's voice, low and hoarse with hunger: "...Old Wilt. Your hand moved."

Nobody answered. Ma Xiaobao said again: "Old Wilt. It's your jacket that's stuck to the rock, not you. Take the jacket off and you can get out." After a long time, Old Wilt's voice, fainter than a mosquito: "Brother Bao... the cliff's cold, my hands... I can't feel them." Ma Xiaobao said: "Climb up, shout for someone, get a rope. Three days? If he says three days it's three days? What if the array doesn't let go?" After another long time, Ma Xiaobao's voice suddenly changed its tone: flat, not hoarse any more, like his father Ma San collecting rent at the village gate. "Your pa's three mu of hillside field. Whose are they?"

Nobody on the cliff said another word.

After a while Xiaoman heard cloth tearing, very slowly, *rrrip*, inch by inch, like someone peeling off an old New Year picture stuck fast to a wall. When it was done there was a moment's quiet. Then from outside the wall came a voice, not loud but perfectly clear, calling to this wall:

"Shen Xiaoman—"

It called only that once.

Xiaoman was buried in the earth up to his chest, both hands on his knees, his nails digging into his flesh. He had only to stand up out of the earth. But break the earth and those roots would snap halfway, and the half-grown ground would be a patch of mud. The round of the five phases turned in his body, even and fast. He didn't move.

Outside the wall came the sound of climbing, one pull at a time, fingernails scraping rock, very slow: a long gap, one; a long gap, one. Xiaoman counted. At nineteen, the sound stopped.

Then a scream, long, long, falling from the cliff, falling past the ledge, falling into the dark beyond the ledge, further and further, thinner and thinner, thin as a thread, and then it broke.

After a long, long time a sound came up from below. Very faint, like a drop of water.

Not a sound on the cliff. Inside the wall, Xiaoman sat buried in the earth without moving. The round of the five phases still turned in him, but he felt cold, so cold his teeth knocked together.

Near dawn on the third day the spirit qi in the chamber was still thick. Half a day more of drawing it in, and he might touch the threshold of the fifth layer. So he drew another breath. As this breath went in, he felt something was wrong: the round in his dantian had gone slow and heavy, like a stove stuffed too full of wood so the fire chokes and only smokes and won't flame. He drew again, and the spirit qi jammed in his meridians, swelling until his chest was tight and his temples throbbed with pain.

A very sleepy voice said in his ear: "Enough." Xiaoman said: "Not enough yet. Half a day more and it's the fifth layer." The voice said: "...The name your mother gave you. What is it?" Xiaoman was taken aback. He remembered New Year's night, his mother clicking her abacus and telling him about his name: when a thing's full it spills over, and what spills is wasted; a little fed, not yet full, you still know hunger. Knowing hunger is how you know other people are hungry. Slowly, he let out the breath he hadn't finished drawing. As it went out, his chest eased, like pulling one stick out of a stuffed stove: air got in, and the flames leapt up with a *whoosh*. The round turned again, light and quick and even. The spirit qi lay thick in the chamber still; he'd only scooped off a shallow layer. The rest he left to itself. Just then, through the wall, came Ma Xiaobao's voice from the cliff, low and hoarse with hunger, but every word getting through: "...Shen Xiaoman. I can hear you breathing in there."

The mound of earth split open with a *crack*. He climbed out of it, leaving behind him a hollow where he'd sat. The earth that was left was ordinary earth now; the living soil had all grown into his body. He crawled to the corner and drank a mouthful from the thin spring, sweet to the point of cloying. On the bluestone floor, where a few crumbs of earth had fallen, a green shoot had come up. In the whole room, that was the only bit of green.

He went to the smooth stone wall, and in it was a reflection: tousled hair, a face covered with mud and blood. And under that reflection's skin there was light. Gold-white, green, black, red, yellow, five colours, streak by streak, like five little rivers flowing from the heart to the fingertips and back. *Mixed spirit root*: those words the steward had drawn out long. Yesterday he'd have grinned at this. Now there was only a bitter taste in his mouth. He took out the red-copper gourd and held it, and the belly of the gourd warmed little by little: of his five doors, one was fire. There was one radish left in his bundle. He ate it.

---

At noon on the third day Xiaoman crouched beside the four empty pits and scraped the cauldron moss off their rims bit by bit, all four pits, not one missed, even the glowing bits in the cracks, until he had a ball the size of a fist, and put it in the storage pouch. The empty jade box went in too; jade is jade, after all. Then he faced the four empty pits and cupped his fist to them in salute. He couldn't have said why, and Yinyue didn't ask him whom he was saluting.

He walked out through the wall. The wall stood open; the iron pot still lay in its hollow with the sword in its neck. The ring of bluish-white light on the cliff was still lit, and five people were still stuck on it, too hungry even to groan. At the very outer edge, right by the black mouth of the drop, a grey robe was still stuck there, both sleeves spread, the collar torn open, empty, nobody inside it, held to the stone by the ring of light, as if a man had just this moment walked out of it. By the rusted iron peg at the outer edge of the ledge, on the lip of the drop, there were a few fresh white scratches, made by fingernails, each shorter than the last.

Xiaoman stood by those white scratches for a while. He didn't look into the dark.

He didn't leave. He sat down by the iron peg, his back to the cliff, laid the notched short knife across his knees, and waited. A hunter waiting a whole day for a deer: nothing unusual about that.

An hour before the stone door shut, the ring of bluish-white light on the cliff went out. Five people dropped onto the ledge, thump, thump, lay there a long while, and got up one by one. In the dark, Ma Xiaobao made out who was sitting by the iron peg, and his eyes slowly brightened: "...A second layer." His voice was hoarse with hunger, but the grin was the same grin. "Three days without food, but it's enough for you." He lunged.

Xiaoman stood up and met him with a fist to the bridge of the nose.

"One."

Ma Xiaobao reeled back, his mouth full of blood, his eyes gone round: "You... you're fourth layer?" Xiaoman said: "Fourth layer." The second fist arrived. "Two." Xiaoman counted them out one at a time, not fast, not slow, like his father skinning a hare, in proper order: bridge of the nose, left ribs, right ribs, belly. Of the fourth layer's spiritual power he used only three tenths; use all of it and the man would be gone. The four hangers-on stood in a row against the foot of the cliff, and not one dared move, and he didn't touch one of them. At "Twenty-three," the last blow was neither hard nor light, a tap on Ma Xiaobao's shoulder, as if putting a seal on the matter.

Ma Xiaobao lay curled on the ground with his arms round his head, both eyes swollen to slits. Xiaoman crouched: "Twelfth of the tenth month, the woodshed, twenty-three blows. Paid back. Not one more, not one less." He stood up to go, and a sentence came up from the ground, pitched very low: "Shen Xiaoman."

Xiaoman stopped. With his face against the stone, Ma Xiaobao said, one word at a time: "Inside the wall. What did you hear?" Xiaoman said: "I heard." It was a good while before Ma Xiaobao said: "The business with Old Wilt. You don't tell, I don't tell." He took a breath. "In the twelfth month my pa's sending me a mirror. On the contest stage, I'll shine it on you."

Xiaoman didn't answer. He climbed out through the cleft, and all the way, what he was thinking was: that one finger, he hadn't pressed it.

---

In the stone hall, only a few dozen paces from the stone door, the people leaving were walking one behind another into the water skin. Xiaoman squeezed in at the back of the crowd and stood on tiptoe to look round: among those going out, there was no Sun Ergou. The day they came into the secret realm, Sun Ergou had wanted to follow him, and it was he who'd told Sun Ergou to follow the crowd; and the crowded long corridor's first obstacle was a tipping flagstone. He remembered the pit under the tipping flagstone on the map, with a line of small writing beside it: "Old water channel at bottom of pit, leads to stone hall." Whoever fell in either crawled back through the channel or didn't come back. He turned round and went back, into the long corridor, up to the third flagstone, and stepped right on it. The flagstone tipped and he dropped into the pit. The bottom was dark and smelled of dead people. He'd only just found his feet, his bow bumping the wall, when somebody in the corner said quietly: "...Senior Brother Shen? You're the only one in the outer sect who carries a bow."

It was Sun Ergou. He'd fallen in here three days ago. His grey robe was torn from the collar to the waist, and several of his ten fingernails were split: the pit walls were slippery and he couldn't climb up; the water channel was pitch black with two dead men lying across it, and he didn't dare crawl through; he'd shouted, and nobody answered.

Xiaoman was just bending to crawl into the water channel when the light above dimmed and a sword scabbard reached down. Crouched at the mouth of the pit was a senior sister in pale blue, a head taller than him, with sharp, clean features, her cuffs tied tight, her scabbard worn white: "Grab hold. I'll pull you up." Sun Ergou grabbed first and was hauled up in one pull. The scabbard came down again. Xiaoman took hold, touched the pit wall twice with his toes, and was up; from start to finish the scabbard never took half his weight. At the mouth of the pit the senior sister looked down at the hand that had held the scabbard and said: "You didn't hang on it." She broke off half a piece of dry ration, pushed it at Sun Ergou, and got up and left. Someone nearby called her Senior Sister Qin. Xiaoman kept his head down and walked out at the back of the crowd.

---

Three days on, the tide was coming back. Lu the Loach stuck a reed in the mud, watched it a long time, and said: "Half an hour earlier than last time." The Abbot, Xuanchenzi, still stood before the stone door with his hands tucked in his sleeves, as if in those three days he hadn't moved a step. A small wooden table had appeared beside him; Zhou Heng sat behind it, and for each person who came out he took a name tag and tossed it into the wooden box at his feet. Xuanchenzi didn't look at faces. He looked at the name tags. The first lot out was the inner sect's party: twenty-odd had gone in, one fewer came out, their robes hardly torn; the inner sect collected its own tags. The four ninth-layers left all had bulging sleeves. The green robes came out early too, every one with a bulging robe-front, and two of them had black cuffs: dried blood. One green robe held in his palm a bead the size of a fingernail, a wisp of white air turning slowly inside it, like a breath drawn in and not yet let out: "[Breath pearl]{注=xizhu}. Ten years to one breath, and that's all it makes, a handful of these." The green robe looked back at the stone door and said to his junior: "A sect that sells name tags has gone poor. Next time, we don't buy." Zhou Heng's hand, taking tags, paused.

At first they came out one by one, then three or five at a time, then one only every long while. Zhou Heng's outstretched hand stopped in mid-air.

Ahe was crouched by a rock on the riverbed. When she saw him she stood up: "What did you find in there?" Xiaoman said: "I only found a pinch of earth." Ahe said: "...Just a pinch of earth?" Xiaoman said: "Just a pinch of earth, nothing else. And you, what did you find?" Ahe opened a little piece of cloth with her left hand; in it were three black things. "I found three grains. Very, very old grains. Somebody farmed in the secret realm." Her right hand stayed drawn up into her sleeve all the while, and under the cuff a bit of palm showed, all blisters, glistening. Xiaoman looked at that cuff for a while and asked nothing. Ahe was looking at him too: at his burned-through jacket, at the scorched black trouser leg at his calf. She looked a while and asked just one thing: "The wind. Did you use it?" Xiaoman said: "...I used it." Something stirred in Xiaoman's mind: on the south wall of the stone chamber, the man with the basket scattering earth on the water, and wherever it was scattered, land grew.

Ahe went two steps, then turned back and looked him up and down and said: "Have you... got taller?" Xiaoman's back tightened, and he shrank himself an inch: "It was cold in the secret realm. I was hunched up for three days, and when I came out I stretched." Ahe looked him over from head to foot and said: "People who are cold stay hunched." Having said it she turned and left, and didn't take out her notebook.

It was only as the stone door was about to close that Ma Xiaobao's lot came out. The four hangers-on were holding each other up, their eyes hollow with hunger, their lips all split and bloody from licking rock; Ma Xiaobao's brand-new cultivator's robe was plastered with mud, his face swollen like a risen steamed bun, and he walked at the front and glared at anyone who looked at him twice. Five came out.

Zhou Heng took the fifth name tag, raised his head, and looked into the stone door. "Where's Meng Youtian?"

Only then did Xiaoman learn that Old Wilt's name was Meng Youtian. He had known the man more than a month, and this was the first time he'd heard his name.

Ma Xiaobao's lips moved twice. He said: "He climbed by himself." One of the hangers-on put in quickly: "We took a wrong turn, ended up on that Deadly Cliff, set off an array the people before left, and got stuck three days. Meng Youtian wanted to climb up by himself and lost his grip." Not one of them mentioned Shen Xiaoman. Mention Shen Xiaoman, and they'd first have to say what they'd been doing climbing down that cliff with a coil of hemp rope. Who had beaten that face, they didn't say a word about either. Zhou Heng looked at Ma Xiaobao for a long time. Ma Xiaobao turned his face away.

The tide came in, over the mudflats, over the lintel of the stone door. Zhou Heng tipped the name tags out of the wooden box onto the table and counted them: four hundred and seventeen went in, one hundred and eighty-three came back. More had died in there than had come out alive. He spread the register open and checked the returned tags against it one by one; the ones that didn't match were the ones who hadn't come out. Xiaoman stood in the crowd and watched him check, and by dark he still hadn't finished.

The man with his hands tucked in, standing before the stone door, had flickered an eyelid when he heard the words "Deadly Cliff." Xuanchenzi went along the hundred and eighty-odd mud-covered people on the riverbed, looking at each one. When he came to Xiaoman, he paused. He beckoned Xiaoman over and asked mildly: "Which way did you go?" Xiaoman said: "The way with the most people." Xuanchenzi looked down at his straw sandals. On the sides of the sandals were a few flecks of moss, wet, green almost to black: the moss that grew on the stone steps of the Deadly Cliff. Every other route was dry. Xuanchenzi looked a while, smiled a little, and said: "The way with the most people." Having said that, he didn't look at him again. Xiaoman went back into the crowd, head down, and counted the mud spots on his sandals; by the seventeenth, the sweat on his back had gone.

---

Xiaoman went to the stewards' office to fetch the little zheng. It was lying on the ledger, asleep with all four feet in the air, its belly round as a small watermelon. Zhou Heng opened the night-watch book and pointed at the page for the tenth month: "Ten plants lost in the first ten days. Since the zheng was taken in, not one lost." He closed the book. "To be settled separately." Xiaoman asked: "Settled how?" Zhou Heng said: "Accounts close at Laba."

Xiaoman picked up the little zheng and went to the door, then stopped and turned round: "Senior Brother Zhou. Meng... Senior Brother Meng's family. Who's going to tell them?" Zhou Heng didn't look up, and the beads of the abacus stopped too. After a while he said: "I'll go and tell them."

The next morning the kitchen's big pot boiled half as much porridge, and a whole stretch of places stood empty in the dining hall. On the first day people still looked at those empty places; by the third, nobody looked. Zhou Heng went down the hundred and thirty-one names left in the register one by one, getting ready the spirit stones for the first of the twelfth month. The bunk under the window was empty. On it was folded a thin quilt, and on the quilt lay a ball of oiled paper folded neat and square. Nobody ever touched it.

That night Xiaoman tucked the little zheng into his robe and went to the woodshed. Passing behind the main hall, he stopped: a lantern was lit in the hall. The Abbot, Xuanchenzi, was kneeling before the empty stone pedestal with one hand spread, his thumb against the darker square mark in the middle of the stone, measuring it span by span. When he had finished he knelt there a long time without moving, and said softly: "...It isn't there any more." As he got up he glanced towards where Xiaoman was standing, said nothing, and went out by the front door with the lantern. Xiaoman went into the hall, crouched by the pedestal, and spread his hand: one span, two spans... seven. The other side, seven too. The same size as the four empty pits in the stone chamber. The founder had kept a stone pedestal for it, kept it three hundred years, and it had never stood on that pedestal for a single day.

He took from his robe that one spirit stone, the one left from the first of the eleventh month, held it in his palm and drew one breath. Just one, and the stone went cold and dimmed by a layer, as if dust had settled on it. He let go quickly. When he was a leaky ladle, a spirit stone took him the best part of half a month to draw; now he was a water vat, and one more breath would make it a handful of powder. He put it back in his robe and felt the storage pouch at his waist. That pouchful of spirit stones, at this rate, would last twenty or thirty days. There were thirty-four days to the eighth of the twelfth month.

He said quietly: "Yinyue." The tablet didn't answer. He waited a long time, and then the voice in the tablet spoke: "That story in the stone chamber. Do you still want to hear it?" He said he did. So she told it, fast and hard, as if afraid that if she stopped she couldn't go on: "Long ago there was a great flood that drowned the nine provinces. There was a man called Gun who stole that earth from the Emperor of Heaven and scattered it into the flood. The water rose a foot, the earth grew ten. The Emperor of Heaven had the fire god kill him on Mount Yu. His son was called Yu. Yu didn't dam the water, he let it out, and he spread that earth flat a handful at a time, settled the nine provinces, and cast nine cauldrons, one for each province. In the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*{典=shanhai-xirang} the whole thing is a few lines." She paused. "...This queen remembers more than the book does." Xiaoman said: "And the nine cauldrons, what happened to them?" After another long time, a very, very soft voice: "...After that, they sank." Outside the woodshed, far off, the Si had risen full again and was lapping the bank, again and again, slow and even, as if someone under the river were slowly breathing.

Two nights later he dug the Rice Bucket out from the very bottom of the woodpile and laid out on the chopping block what he'd brought out of the secret realm: a pinch of cauldron moss, a stalk of dragon-whisker grass, a breath pearl. Of the fist-sized ball of moss he pinched off only this much; the rest he wrapped and put back in the storage pouch, to eat himself on the day he established his foundation; even eaten raw it would add a little to his chances. He held the breath pearl in his palm like holding a heart. These three things he'd traded his life for. Fed into the Rice Bucket, they might come out as a Foundation Pill.

He tipped in the cauldron moss, the dragon-whisker grass, the breath pearl, and with them the whole pouchful of spirit stones, the middle-grade one, and the one in his robe that had dimmed a layer, all in one clattering rush. If you want an immortal-grade thing out, you have to feed it immortal-grade things. He pressed both hands to the copper belly and fed it power until his eyes swam. Inside it went *crunch, crunch*, chewing loud enough to shake the mountain; when it had finished chewing it gave a full belch, and went quiet. He waited the length of an incense stick and lifted the lid. It was empty inside, not even a torn talisman.

Xiaoman crouched there, looking at that empty belly. He thought of the thread of dripping water at the foot of the Deadly Cliff, of the shield against his throat and everything going black a little at a time, of that long scream on the cliff. That was what he'd traded for these; and the whole pouchful of spirit stones too, every last one, thrown in. That belch was still in his ears. He felt in his bundle, and what came out was a radish, begged from the kitchen the day before, kept to chew at night. He gripped the radish, and the more he looked at it the angrier he got, and he threw it into the Rice Bucket: "Eat! Go on, eat!"

*Click.*

The lid opened, and a cool fragrance came out, like a pine wood after rain; the little zheng's nose moved first. In the belly lay a pill, clear as a drop of dew set solid, with nine lines on it, gleaming gold. Beyond the lines it was smooth, not a word on it: not like the bits and pieces the Rice Bucket had spat out before, which all announced themselves, on the jar, on the corner of the paper, on the underside. Xiaoman craned his neck and looked, and his eyes went round as two copper coins and his jaw dropped with a *click*, and his mouth hung open a long while. He rubbed his eyes and counted again: nine lines. Rubbed again, counted again: still nine.

He wrapped the pill in three layers of oiled paper and put it in the storage pouch. If half a word about this pill got out, never mind his own little life, Yinyue and the Rice Bucket would be scooped up in one pot along with him.

Only the next morning did Yinyue come out. He unwrapped the oiled-paper packet to show her. She looked at the pill, then at the flat storage pouch at his waist. Xiaoman said: "I fed it everything. It just belched." "Then this one, where did it come from?" "...A radish." For a long while Yinyue said nothing. She leaned in and sniffed it, sniffed it again, and her voice changed altogether: "A Nine-Turn... Foundation Pill." Xiaoman said: "A Foundation Pill? The kind the contest champion only gets one of a year?" "That kind is one-turn," she said. "This is nine-turn." Her golden eyes got rounder and rounder, and the fur on her tail exploded with a *whumph* into a feather duster. She twisted round and glared hard at the Rice Bucket at the foot of the woodpile, the corner of her mouth twitching, twitching. Her own Nine-Turn Soul-Return Pill had got back a Qi-Gathering Pill.

That night he begged another radish from the kitchen, fed it in, and waited rubbing his hands. With a *pfff* the mouth of the furnace shot out a puff of black smoke right into his face, and the lid snapped shut, *click*; he pressed, and it wouldn't open. Xiaoman sat there with a black face, only the whites of his eyes blinking, and when he opened his mouth he breathed out a little puff of black smoke. The little zheng watched him, snorted, pitched headfirst into the woodpile, and rolled about with its feet in the air. He said: "...Is this bucket sulking?" Three nights running he crouched in front of it with an apologetic smile and said a basketful of nice things, and the lid wouldn't open for him.

---

On the nights after that, he trained neither for the books nor to hide. He drew the bow behind the woodshed: draw full, release, draw full again. When he'd done that, he took the short knife with three teeth gone and practised, cut after cut on a wooden post, that one stroke of his father's for bleeding a deer. When he'd done that, he sat down to breathe out and in, a breath at a time, filling the water vat towards full, and when it was half full, he stopped of his own accord.

One day, carrying water past the mountain gate, he found Elder Qu sweeping the steps. Xiaoman stopped and watched the broom. Without lifting his head, Elder Qu just moved the broom aside a little: "Your step's gone light."
