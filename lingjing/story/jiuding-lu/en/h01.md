# Chapter One: A Broken Bowl Bids the Sunset Farewell / Half a Flatbread Buys a Fox Queen

In the old days — when people went out on their own two legs, lit an oil lamp at night, and paid their rent in deer hides and grain — a story went round at the foot of Mount Meng. It was about a boy. How bad was his life, exactly? When the storyteller in town told it, he never got halfway before he started crying himself, and cried so hard he couldn't stop, and in the end he had to hand his listeners back their tea money, one coin at a time. After that the storyteller would never tell it again. Not because he couldn't bear to. He simply couldn't afford to.

[The boy]{注=xiaoman} was called Shen Xiaoman. He was twelve that year, thin as a rake, with a pair of eyes black and bright as wet stones, and he lived in Shi'ao Village at the foot of Mount Meng. Ask what he did, and he was the only son of a hunter's family. Ask what he was good at, and there was just the one thing: being hungry. There wasn't a day in the year his stomach didn't growl.

That autumn, the bow on his family's wall was about to change its name to Ma.

---

That morning Xiaoman was woken by basins. It had rained in the night, not hard, but with great patience, right through to the fifth watch. His mother was already emptying the basins one by one. She was a small, thin woman, her hair pinned up without a strand out of place, her clothes patch upon patch, every patch sewn dead square. She emptied one, she counted one: "One, two, three…" The one under the stove was "nine"; the one at the head of his father's bed was "thirteen"; the one just inside the doorway of the room where they hung the hides was "seventeen". Seventeen basins, not one missing. His mother straightened her back, looked at the half-vat of rainwater in the corner, and said, "Half a vat. Enough for three washes, and two trips to the well saved. That rain wasn't for nothing."

Shi'ao Village had seventeen households in all. Sixteen farmed and one hunted, and the one that hunted was the Shens. When the villagers talked about the Shens they always put up a thumb and said, "The hunter's family — that's a craft handed down from father to son." Then they slowly took the thumb back and added: "Along with the poverty." The Shens had two rooms, one for people and one for hides, and on rainy days both of them were for water. Xiaoman's [mother]{注=mama} said their roof was the most learned roof on Mount Meng: it knew where the rain came from, and it knew where the rain was going. Xiaoman's [father]{注=baba} said this was what they called **Heaven and Man as One**. His mother said, "Try being one with it once more." His father said nothing. The man knew better than anyone when to say nothing. It was the greatest skill he had in all his life, and the only one — oh, no, there was one more: his left knee.

His father was tall, dark and lean, and a little stooped; standing, he looked like a bow that had been drawn too long and couldn't spring back. When he was young a wild boar had gored that left knee, and ever since it ached on cloudy days, ached when it rained, ached when the wind blew, and ached before the Ma family came for the rent — more reliable than the brass gong at the village gate. That morning it wouldn't stop aching. His father rubbed it, frowned, rubbed it again. His mother gave him a look and asked, "What's it saying today?" His father thought it over very seriously and said, honestly, "It says… we might have company today."

His mother said nothing more. Mount Meng belonged to the Ma family — or so the Ma family said. It turned out old Master Ma had a distant nephew who was a clerk at the county yamen, and so overnight every tree on the mountain was named Ma, every rabbit on the mountain was named Ma, and even every spring on the mountain was named Ma too. If a villager cut a load of firewood, one bundle went to the Mas; of whatever game the Shens took, half went to the Mas: two rabbits, hand over one; one river deer, hand over half a side. One year Xiaoman's father drank two ounces of wine, screwed up his courage, and asked in a small voice, "And the wind on the mountain — is that named Ma as well?" The next day the Ma family started collecting "mountain wind money". He never drank again. All year round the Shens had one regular guest, and this guest never came empty-handed — he always carried that short club — and never left empty-handed either.

After a bowl of wild-greens gruel so thin you could see your face in it, his mother handed Xiaoman the woodcutter's knife and said, "Go up East Slope and cut a load of firewood. Go early, come back early." As Xiaoman went out, [Ahe]{注=ahe} from next door was squatting in her vegetable plot, guarding a cabbage. She wore two little yellow-haired plaits, and her small face was wrapped tighter than the cabbage. It was the biggest cabbage in her family's plot, green shading into white, packed firm and tight, a good size bigger than Ahe's own head. A speckled hen had just poked its head through a gap in the fence; a flick of her bamboo pole and the hen pulled back again, so neatly done you could tell at a glance she'd been guarding it all autumn.

Xiaoman said across the fence, "Ahe, guarding the cabbage?" Ahe didn't look up. "I've been growing this cabbage for eighty-one days. I've poured a hundred and sixty-two ladles of water on it, picked off thirty-one grubs, and chased off the hen forty times — this time doesn't count, I'm not done chasing. Our hill field brought in two-tenths less again this year. Pa says the soil's thinner every year. This one is for the winter." Xiaoman said, "I'm going up East Slope for firewood. Coming?" Ahe said, "Ma won't let me past the village gate." Xiaoman said, "East Slope isn't outside the village." Ahe said, "To get up East Slope you pass the old locust tree, and the old locust tree is the village gate." Xiaoman thought about it and couldn't find a way round that.

He was just about to go when Ahe called after him: "One more thing. You owe me half an egg." Xiaoman stopped. "Since when?" Ahe said, "The fifteenth of last month. You helped chase our hens and you chased the speckled one up onto the roof. She didn't lay that day." Xiaoman said, "She doesn't lay every day anyway." Ahe said, "That's why it's only half." Xiaoman said, "…You don't even have a book. How do you remember a debt like that?" Ahe tapped her forehead with one finger. "It's written here." Xiaoman said, "And if one day you forget?" Ahe said, without any expression at all, "If I forget, you still pay."

The firewood on East Slope had long since been cut thin by the villagers. Halfway up, Xiaoman looked across: on the far side of a valley, halfway up the mountain, stood a spread of white walls and black tiles, a big house three courtyards deep. That was the Ma place. Even from across a valley you could see the Ma roof didn't leak. It had no need to know where the rain came from, Xiaoman thought; it was probably the least learned roof on Mount Meng. Something round was moving at the Ma gate. It was too far off to make out — whether it was one of the young Ma masters or one of the Ma millstones, he couldn't tell. He cut a load of firewood, carried it home on his shoulder pole, and sat in the yard splitting it: split one, look at the sky. It stayed grey all day, until towards evening the clouds in the west cracked open and let out a thread of sunset. Indoors, his father's left knee gave a *click*.

---

The rent collector was [Ma San]{注=masan}. Ma San was a distant nephew of old Master Ma, and he was built like a door — though to say he was like a door was flattering the door. He was more of a door than the Shens' own door, which at least had a few cracks in it. To come into a house he had to turn sideways, and turned sideways he had to duck, and ducking he first had to take a deep breath. In his belt he carried a short club bound in iron, and in Shi'ao Village that club was more famous than he was. Wherever it went, the dogs stopped barking; wherever it went, people suddenly remembered they hadn't fed the chickens yet.

That evening the clouds at the edge of the sky suddenly darkened, a gust of wind came swirling into the village full of dead leaves, the dogs at the village gate all shut their mouths together, and the sparrows in the trees lined up and flew off one after another. Xiaoman put down the knife and sighed. He knew: **he** was here.

There was a great *bang*. The Shens' brushwood door flew out, turned three circles in mid-air — quite gracefully, in fact — and came down, neither left nor right of the mark, in Ahe's vegetable plot next door, flattening a cabbage. Not any cabbage: the very one Ahe had been guarding that morning, the one her family had grown for a whole autumn, eighty-one days, a hundred and sixty-two ladles of water. On the other side of the fence Ahe's bamboo pole fell to the ground with a clatter. Her eyes were as round as two copper coins, and her mouth hung open and stayed open.

In the doorway stood a man who blocked out the sunset completely: Ma San. From behind him came a round figure, and at every step the ground shook — mostly it was him shaking. This was [Ma Xiaobao]{注=maxiaobao}, Ma San's only son, two years older than Xiaoman and twice his weight, dressed all in silk, waddling like a rice dumpling that had come to life, with a piece of osmanthus cake in his hand. Only then did Xiaoman understand that the round thing he'd seen from East Slope had not been a millstone.

Ma San tapped the doorframe with his club, tap, tap, with some sense of rhythm, and drew out his words: "Mountain — rent —" Before he could finish, Xiaoman's father had dropped to his knees with a thump. "Third Master!" Ma San said, "I'm not done." His father said, "I'll kneel first. You take your time." The second half of "rent" stuck in Ma San's throat, the club stayed up in the air and forgot to come down, and for a good while he just stood there, eyes round, mouth open. All these years of collecting rent, and plainly this was the first time he'd met anyone so cooperative.

Only then did he go on: "…You're still short one deer hide and twenty catties of meat." As he said it, his eyes went round the room — past the basins in the corner catching drips, past the bowls on the stove — and came to rest on the old bow on the wall, the one wrapped in ox sinew. "Noon on the first day of winter, I'll come for it. If I don't get it — that bow, I'm taking."

Xiaoman's father said in a panic, "Third Master! My father handed that down to me!" Ma San reached out, patted him on the shoulder, and said, with great earnestness, "What your father owed the Ma family, you pay back. That's called a son paying his father's debts." He said it harshly, but in his heart Ma San knew perfectly well: if he really took the bow, what would old Shen hunt with? Where would the deer hides and river-deer meat come from after that? He didn't really want the bow. What he wanted was for the Shens to be afraid.

Just then Ma Xiaobao swayed his way round his father, came up to Xiaoman, and weighed the osmanthus cake in his palm twice, as if he were weighing silver, before holding it up under Xiaoman's nose. In that moment, time actually slowed down. Xiaoman saw every grain of osmanthus on that cake shining, golden, like a dusting of gold flakes; the smell crept in at his nostrils, slid down his throat, slid all the way down into a stomach that had been empty all day, took a turn round it, waved, and left. He even heard the osmanthus cake speak to him. What it said was: **As if you deserve me.**

Ma Xiaobao drawled, "Shen — Xiao — man, want some? Do a dog bark and you can have a lick." Xiaoman drawled back, word for word: "Shen — Xiao — man, want some? Do a dog bark and you can have a lick." Ma Xiaobao froze; every bit of flesh on his face froze with him. "…What?" Xiaoman froze too. "What?" Ma Xiaobao said, "Are you going to do it or not?" Xiaoman said, "I'm doing it now."

The room went quiet at once. At some point half the village had gathered at the door. Ahe was holding the flattened cabbage, her shoulders jerking. Ma San's face went red first, then purple, then black, and in the end it turned a colour that Xiaoman's mother later said was called "**done for**". Ma San stamped his club on the ground and roared, "**You make fun of my son!**" Only then did Ma Xiaobao come to his senses. Shaking with rage in every bit of flesh, he rushed forward and gave Xiaoman a shove.

Xiaoman went over backwards. In the instant of falling a great many things flashed through his head: his twelve short, poor years of life, his father's knee, his mother's hands, the old locust tree at the village gate he'd climbed a hundred times, and the four bowls on the stove. A thousand threads, and when they reached his mouth only one was left. He didn't dare say it out loud, but in his heart he swore it as loud as he could: **Damn your grandpa.**

His back hit something with a jolt of pain — the stove. There was a *crack*, and the bowl on the edge of the stove was shaken off and went tumbling through the air, turning two somersaults. Just then the setting sun was slanting in through the doorway, and the rim of the bowl flashed white, as if it were saying goodbye to him. Xiaoman flung out a hand to catch it. His fingertips just reached the bottom of the bowl, and yet in the end he was short by an inch. By the stove, his mother had reached out a hand too, and that hand stopped in the air, stopped just above the inch the bowl fell through.

*Crash.* The bowl broke into seven or eight pieces on the floor.

Xiaoman lay on his back on the floor, looking up at the hole in the roof, and for a while he forgot to get up. Just then a drop of last night's rain, saved up in the thatch, leaked through the hole and landed right in the middle of his forehead, cold. He sighed at the roof and said, "That was the only one of our four bowls without a chip in it."

---

Ma San waved a hand and left, taking Ma Xiaobao with him. On his way out Ma Xiaobao looked back, winked at Xiaoman, and, right in front of him, stuffed the whole osmanthus cake into his mouth, his cheeks puffed out like two steamed buns. The Shens' door was still lying in Ahe's vegetable plot.

Xiaoman's mother crouched down and picked up the pieces of the bowl one by one. His father stood by, rubbing his hands. "Wife… a broken bowl, we can buy another…" His mother said, "Buy it with what?" His father said, "…With… with my knee?" His mother said, "And what's your knee worth?" His father thought it over very seriously and said, "On cloudy days it's pretty accurate. Anyone who wants to air their quilts ought to come and ask it first."

His mother didn't laugh. She just crouched there with a piece of bowl in her fingers and didn't move for a long time. His father stopped talking too. He slowly crouched down beside her and picked up pieces with her. The two of them picked them up one by one, very, very slowly, as if, picked up too quickly, the bowl would really be gone. His mother wrapped the seven or eight pieces in an old cloth, tied the four corners, didn't throw it away, and put it in the wooden chest at the head of the bed. What a broken bowl could be kept for, nobody asked that day.

Supper that night was a pot of cornmeal mush. His mother went to the cupboard for the bowls, put her hand in, paused, and took out three. Each of the three had its own temper. The first had a thumb-sized chip out of it, and when you drank from it you had to keep your bearings. The second had a crack running right down to the bottom, so anything hot seeped out slowly. The third had a piece knocked off its base, so it wouldn't stand on the table; set it down and it spun round and round. His mother pushed the chipped one to Xiaoman and reached for the cracked one herself, but his father got there first and gathered it up. "This one's mine. I drink fast; it won't have time to leak." So what was left in his mother's hands was the one that wouldn't stand.

Xiaoman looked at this one, then at that one, and did a sum in his head. A coarse china bowl in town, three coppers. In the Shen purse, none. For three coppers his father would first have to shoot a rabbit; to shoot a rabbit, there'd first have to be rabbits on the near hills — and by this point there were no rabbits left on the near hills, so the sum couldn't go any further. His mother said, "Eat." So Xiaoman ate. Chip turned outward; he kept his bearings and didn't spill a drop.

Xiaoman asked, "Ma, how did you ever come to marry Pa? You can read, and Pa can't." His father went red and got in first. "That year I took a wild rabbit to your grandfather's school, and there was a notice on the door. I couldn't read it. I was afraid it said 'No Entry', so I stood outside the door for two hours." His mother looked down at the bowl in her hands. "It said 'No School Today'."

That was all she said, and she said no more. Xiaoman wondered: a man who'd stood outside a door for two hours and hadn't dared go in — how did he ever get in after that? After supper his mother took the three bowls to the stove to wash. She washed one, put one in the cupboard, counting under her breath: "One, two, three." At three her hand stopped in the cupboard for a moment, as if waiting for a fourth. She waited a little while, and then she shut the cupboard door, gently.

That night Xiaoman, facing the hole in the roof, said very, very quietly, "One day I'm going to make it so my mother never has to crouch on the floor picking things up again." The roof of course didn't answer. A single drop of the rain saved up in the thatch leaked down and fell into the seventeenth basin in the corner. *Ting.*

---

Through a cloth curtain, his father and mother were talking in low voices. His father said, "…When's the first day of winter?" His mother said, "Three days from now." A pause, then: "Three days left." His father said, "The near hills are empty. There's only Black Pine Ridge." On the other side of the curtain it was quiet for a good while. His mother said, "Your father died on Black Pine Ridge." His father said, "I know." His mother said no more. After a while she cried for a little, and then she didn't.

Xiaoman lay on the bed doing his sums, item by item. His mother had taught him that; she said when something happens, do the sums first. While he did them he murmured the verse she'd taught him: "Nine on the head, one at the feet, three on the left, seven on the right…" It was the Luo Writing verse, handed down from his grandfather's school: nine numbers set out in a square. When he was little, his mother had laid out nine chestnuts, each marked, in three rows under the oil lamp to show him: "When your grandfather taught me sums, the first thing he taught wasn't adding and taking away. It was this. Say it, and your heart won't get muddled." Ma Xiaobao was a head taller than him and twice his weight; in a fight, he'd lose. Even if he won, Ma San was standing in the doorway; in that fight, his father would get the beating. Even if he beat Ma San as well… Xiaoman pictured it: twelve-year-old Shen Xiaoman, knocking a door flat with one punch. That "even if", he felt, was a bit of a stretch. So today he hadn't hit back. He'd only talked back. Talking back isn't hitting back — that was a rule he'd made for himself.

He lay with his eyes open in the dark and set it all down in his memory, one thing at a time: the shove Ma Xiaobao gave him, the look in Ma San's eyes when he looked at the bow, the bowl flying through the air, and his mother's hand stopped in mid-air. Ma San, Ma Xiaobao, old Master Ma, and old Master Ma's distant nephew the clerk — he swore at each of them once more in his heart: **Damn your grandpa.** When he'd finished swearing, he said four more words to himself: **Can't beat them now.** And when he'd said it once, he said the last word again on its own: **Now.**

In the middle of the night there was a rustling on the other side of the curtain. His father went out in the dark and came back with something heavy, which he set down on the threshold with a *thunk*. Then came a *shk, shk, shk*, one stroke after another, slow and even: iron on stone. The Shens' whetstone had split in two the year before last; this one must have been borrowed from somewhere. Xiaoman counted the strokes, got to three hundred and something, lost count, and stopped counting. He knew what his father was sharpening. The near hills were empty, and there were three days to bring down a deer. His father was sharpening arrows for the deep mountains.

---

Just before dawn there were three taps on the paper window. *Tok, tok, tok.* Xiaoman pushed it open, and there outside stood Ahe from next door. She was a year younger than Xiaoman, her nose red with cold, exactly like a little radish just pulled from the ground; she still had mud on her, and you could tell at a glance she'd been up early, crouching in the plot to lay out the body of the cabbage the door had flattened. She pushed something into Xiaoman's hand. It was warm. A boiled egg.

Ahe said, "Ma says your pa came to our house in the middle of the night to borrow the whetstone. The near hills are empty again, so he must be going to Black Pine Ridge." Xiaoman said, "Mm. He's going." Ahe said, "Don't go dying in there." Xiaoman said, "I won't." Ahe pulled her hands back into her sleeves and said, very properly, "Then it goes on the books. One egg. Pay me back later."

Xiaoman asked, "Pay you back what?" Ahe thought about it. "Pay me back two." Xiaoman said, "…That's steep interest." Ahe said, "Black Pine Ridge. High risk, so of course the interest is high." Xiaoman said, "And your cabbage…" Ahe said, without any expression at all, "That's a separate account. I picked up the leaves one by one and pickled them. That's enough for half the winter. The other half of the winter, the interest is higher."

With that she turned and ran. Halfway, she turned round and added: "Remember, it's two!" Xiaoman stood at the window holding the egg, and held it for a long time. It was warmer than the last bit of fire in the Shens' stove.

---

He shut the window, turned round, and the lamp in the kitchen was already lit. His mother was crouched at the stove making flatbread — and white-flour flatbread at that. The little cloth bag that held the family's white flour, Xiaoman knew, had been flat for a month. His mother had shaken out every last bit of flour, shaken it three times, even patted the seams of the bag clean, and made two flatbreads, one thick, one thin.

His father sat on the edge of the bed wrapping his left knee round and round with a strip of old sacking. When he was done he stood up and tried a couple of steps. The muscles in his face twitched once, and then he sat back down as if nothing had happened, and grinned at Xiaoman. "It's fine. Old trouble. It's a bit talkative today."

His mother didn't turn round. "Husband, are you really going to Black Pine Ridge?" His father said, "The Mas have combed the near hills bare. There's nothing for it." His mother said, "Your leg—" His father said, "My leg's no good any more." He paused, then said, "There's the boy's legs as well."

The spatula in his mother's hand stopped for a moment. Then she lifted out the thick flatbread, wrapped it in a clean cloth, came over and tucked it inside Xiaoman's jacket. The thin one she gave to his father. She had none herself.

She crouched down and looked Xiaoman in the eye, her voice pressed so low only the two of them could hear: "Your father's an honest man. All his life he's never schemed against anyone, and never guarded against anyone either. You're different. You take after me." She reached out and pinched off a loose thread on his collar. "Things in the mountains are fiercer than Ma San. When something happens, think first, and move once you've thought it through. Once you've worked it out: if you can beat it, fight; if you can't, run. There's no shame in running."

Xiaoman said, "Yes, Ma. I'll remember." His mother said, "One more thing. Yesterday — I saw it." Xiaoman looked up. His mother said, "You didn't hit back. That was right." The corner of her mouth moved, very, very slightly. "And what you said back to him — that was right too." She brushed the hair back from his forehead. "But don't forget it, either."

Behind them his father coughed twice and shouldered the bow. His mother stood in the doorway — there wasn't actually a door in the doorway; the door was still in Ahe's vegetable plot — and watched the two backs, one big, one small, walk off into the thin grey line at the edge of the sky. She didn't call out, and she didn't wave. She just stood there, and stood there, until Xiaoman looked back and couldn't see her any more.

---

The way out of the village went past its east end. By the well platform squatted a half-grown boy, Beansprout Liu, the eldest of old Liu's family — a thin neck holding up a big head, exactly like a beansprout — taking in his sparrow net. He saw father and son heading for the mountains with a bow on their backs, and his eyes flicked this way and that. [Granny Wu]{注=wupo} sat on her doorstep as usual, her clouded white eyes turned towards the mountain, as if she'd been waiting for them specially. Granny Wu was the oldest person in Shi'ao Village. Her eyes couldn't see, but her ears were sharper than all the village dogs put together. If someone stole someone's melons in the night, she knew first thing next morning — knew not just who'd done it but how many, and even which ones weren't ripe. When Xiaoman was little, the Mas had held a birthday banquet at the village gate, with gongs and drums going all day; she'd listened from her doorstep all day, and at the end of it said just one thing: "That Ma lot. They eat and they're still hungry."

She asked, "Going to Black Pine Ridge?" Xiaoman's father didn't make a sound. Granny Wu didn't want an answer anyway. She went on to herself: "Once upon a time there lived an old man on top of Mount Meng who rode a white crane. He ate one jujube a meal and lived to eight hundred." His father said, "Granny, we've got a way to go." Granny Wu said, "Mm. So did he." His father didn't answer that. He bent his head to Xiaoman and said, "Made up."

Granny Wu fumbled something out of the front of her jacket and pressed it into Xiaoman's hand. It was a [little wooden tablet]{注=fox-token}, rubbed shiny, carved with a curled-up fox. The wood was black with a touch of silver in it — what wood, he didn't know — heavier in the hand than it looked, and cool. Held under his nose it had a very faint scent, like a pine wood after rain, and like something else, though Xiaoman couldn't say what. She said, "The year your grandpa went, he passed my door too." Xiaoman opened his mouth and not a word came out. Granny Wu said, "When you get to the stone, look at the back of it." Xiaoman asked, "Look at what?" Granny Wu smiled, showing her last three teeth. "Look, and you'll know. Don't look, and you won't."

Xiaoman felt that this was the same as saying nothing at all, but he tucked the tablet inside his jacket anyway. His mother said that of every ten things old people say, nine are nonsense, and the one left over can save your life.

---

Xiaoman's father often said there were three things a hunting family couldn't do without: a good bow, a good pair of legs, and a good pair of ears. The bow had come down from Xiaoman's grandpa. Its back had cracked twice and was wrapped in ox sinew, and from a distance it looked like a pair of patched trousers. Once, when he was small, Xiaoman had asked: if Grandpa never came back, how did the bow come back? His father said only, "Your grandpa didn't come back. The bow did." Ask again, and it was "Eat your food." The ears were his father's; he could hear a river deer snap a dry twig three miles off. As for the legs — his father's knee was no good now, and these last two years the legs had been Xiaoman's.

That day father and son went into the mountains before it was light. The near hills had nothing in them. The ground was all dog prints and hoof prints. The Mas had held three hunts since autumn began, with twenty hounds and dozens of men, and combed the near hills over and over, finer than his mother combed her hair. Xiaoman climbed the tallest pine and looked all round for a long time, then slid down and said only: "Pa. The near hills are empty."

His father said, "How empty?" Xiaoman said, "Even the rats have moved out. They left a notice on the door." His father said, "…Saying what?" Xiaoman said, "'The Mas Are Here. No Rat Can Live.'"

With the near hills empty, his father led him further into the mountains. After a while his father suddenly stopped, took the bow off his back, weighed it in his hand, and said, "Today I'll teach you something." Xiaoman perked up at once. His father had used that bow for decades. Xiaoman had touched its back and counted how many turns of ox sinew were wrapped on it, but he had never once drawn it. His father wouldn't let him.

His father said, "This came down from your grandpa. It's called *Three Arrows That Bring Down the Goose*." Xiaoman said, "Bring down the goose? Pa, have you ever shot down a wild goose?" His father said, "No." Xiaoman said, "Then did Grandpa?" His father thought about it. "No, he didn't either. That year your grandpa went to town to sell hides and heard the storyteller going on about 'one arrow brings down the goose', and when he came back he gave our bit of skill a name. He said once it had a name it counted as martial arts." He paused, then said, "That storyteller also told how Li Guang shot a rock and the arrow went in. Your grandpa came home and shot a rock too. The arrow broke. One arrow takes a whole evening to whittle." From then on, the Shens only shot living things.

Xiaoman said, "So is it martial arts or not?" His father said, "It's three sayings. One arrow, one saying. Your grandpa taught me one arrow a year, and I'll teach you one arrow a year. This year it's the first arrow." He planted the bow on the ground and said, one word at a time: "**First read the wind, then read the deer.**"

Xiaoman waited a while, and when nothing more came, asked, "…That's it?" His father said, "That's it." Xiaoman said, "Just those few words?" His father said, "When your grandpa taught me, it was those few words too." Xiaoman thought: a few words a year — teach at that rate, and by the time he'd learned the lot, his father's knee probably wouldn't be up to chatting any more.

His father couldn't read. He hadn't owned a single book in his life; his books were all in the mountains. Seeing his son wasn't convinced, he crouched down — very slowly, his left leg stuck straight out — pulled a blade of grass and held it up in front of his eyes. The tip of the grass leaned a little to the south-east. His father said, "What's it telling you?" Xiaoman stared at it a long while. "…The wind's from the north-west." His father said, "And which end are you standing at?" Xiaoman looked down at his feet, then at the tip of the grass, and went red. "Upwind. The deer would smell me before it ever saw me." His father nodded, put the blade of grass into his hand, and said no more. Xiaoman said, "And the deer?" His father said, "Read the wind right and the deer can't get away. Read it wrong and the deer's somebody else's."

Just then his father suddenly put a hand on his shoulder and jerked his chin forward. Forty-odd paces off, on a slope of tangled grass, a pheasant had its head down, scratching for food among the roots, its tail bobbing up and down, with no idea at all that there were two such people as them in the world. His father didn't hurry to draw. First he read the grass. On the slope the grass would lean this way for a moment, then stand up straight again. He put a finger in his mouth, held it up in the air for a moment, and then, without a sound, circled round to the left. His left leg was awkward, and with every step he tested the ground first with his toes: one step, two steps — a good twenty-odd steps he circled, until he was where the wind blew straight along the path of his arrow, and there he set his back against an old chestnut tree. That knee of his wouldn't let him kneel; he could only shoot standing. Then he nocked an arrow, drew the bow only halfway, and simply held it there. Xiaoman held his breath, waiting for him to draw, and he wouldn't draw. A gust swept down the slope and the grass tips all lay flat together; he didn't move. The gust passed, the grass tips wavered twice and slowly rose — and in the instant when they were nearly upright but not quite, that patched bow in his hands came to full draw in one breath, bent like a crescent moon, and the moment it was full he loosed. There was a *whoosh*, and before the pheasant could so much as flap, it had turned over in the grass.

One arrow, one pheasant. Xiaoman ran over and picked it up. The arrow had gone clean through its neck, dead on the mark. His father took the arrow back and, before he even looked at the pheasant, wiped the head clean on his sleeve, held the shaft up to the light to see if it was still straight, and only then put it back in the quiver. Xiaoman said, "Pa, you already had the arrow on the string. What were you waiting for?" His father said, "For the wind to catch its breath." Xiaoman said, "The wind catches its breath?" His father said, "Everything has to catch its breath. The wind does, deer do, people do." He paused, then said, "Ma San does too." Xiaoman didn't understand that at the time, but he felt it was the most learned thing his father had ever said in his life, and he fixed it firmly in his memory.

There was one more thing he fixed in his memory, but didn't say out loud. Yesterday evening, kneeling in front of Ma San, his father's two hands pressed on the ground had been shaking. Today, drawing this bow, his father's two hands had been steady as rock, and the bowstring hadn't so much as quivered. So his father's hands hadn't been born shaking after all.

Xiaoman said, "Pa, what about the second arrow?" His father said, "Next year." Which was the same as saying nothing, and on that point, Xiaoman felt, his father was a little like Granny Wu.

Xiaoman wouldn't give up. He thought: if he couldn't draw the bow, he could at least read the wind. Before they'd gone far he copied his father, plucked a hair from his head, held it up by one end, and waited to see which way it would float. Float it did — not east, and not west. A gust came, and it floated straight into his father's mouth. His father went *ptoo, ptoo, ptoo*, spat seven or eight times in a row, scraped his tongue over and over with a finger, and only then turned round, his dark face pulled very long, and glared at him. Xiaoman said, "Pa, the wind's blowing your way." His father said, "…So I gather."

By noon the near hills hadn't shown them a second living thing. That pheasant was the whole of the morning's take for father and son — skin and bone, like carrying a mop in your arms. One pheasant couldn't stand in for a deer hide. His father sat on a rock rubbing his knee and didn't say anything for a long time.

Xiaoman couldn't keep still. He climbed another pine to look. The near hills were bare, nothing there; further in, two ridges away, lay a pine forest, black and dense. That was Black Pine Ridge. He was about to slide down when something moved at the edge of the forest. First read the wind — the treetops leaned behind him; the wind was blowing from the forest towards him. He held his breath and looked again: under the black pines a reddish-brown shape that had had its head down slowly raised it, and on top of its head stood up a cluster of something, like bare winter branches. In the blink of an eye the shape turned and was gone into the forest.

"Pa, there's a deer."

His father was on his feet at once, knee or no knee. One deer was one deer hide and twenty catties of meat; it was the bow on the wall. Father and son fixed the direction and felt their way over, crossed two ridges, and came to the edge of the forest. Sure enough there was a line of fresh hoof prints in the mud, big and deep, heading into the trees. They followed the prints through a thicket of thorn scrub, and in front of them stood a broken stone.

---

The children of Shi'ao Village heard the story of Black Pine Ridge before they could walk. At the mouth of the ridge stood a broken stone tablet, half buried in the earth. It was a dark blue-black stone, and after hundreds of years of wind and rain, every other stone around it was thick with moss, yet not a scrap grew on it. It was clean, as if someone wiped it every day. Most of the writing on it could no longer be made out. Only the last two words were still perfectly clear: **DO NOT ENTER.** The old people of the village said that of ten who went in, one came back, and the one who came back wouldn't say what was in there. Xiaoman's grandpa had been one of the nine who didn't come back.

The odd thing was, the Mas had combed the near hills over and over, twenty hounds and dozens of men, and yet when they reached this broken stone not one of them would take a single step further in. The villagers said that when the Ma dogs chased a rabbit as far as the stone, they turned tail and ran back.

Father and son stood in front of the broken stone. The pine forest behind it was black and heavy. There was no wind and no birdsong. It was so quiet it was as if someone had put the sound of the whole mountain into a sack and carried it off. Xiaoman remembered what Granny Wu had said. He went round behind the stone and parted the dead grass, which stood half as high as a man. The back of the stone had no writing on it. Only at the very bottom was a small fox carved, curled up, its tails wrapped round its body. He took the wooden tablet out of his jacket and held it up beside the carving. They were exactly the same.

Xiaoman's heart gave a jolt. He looked at the tablet, then at the stone, then at the tablet again, and made up his mind: put the tablet away and say nothing. Some things, said out loud, would mean his father wouldn't go in today; if his father didn't go in, Ma San would come for the bow; if Ma San came for the bow, his mother would be crouching on the floor picking things up again. That sum he'd worked out to the last copper.

His father stood in front of the stone for a good while. He reached out and touched those two words, and his finger rested a moment on "NOT". His father's father had gone in from right here. He looked down at the line of hoof prints at the foot of the stone, looked up at the sky, and said, "You wait here for me." Xiaoman didn't say "I won't." He thought about it and said, "Pa. With your leg, you can't catch a deer." His father was stopped dead. Xiaoman held up three fingers. "I'm coming in with you. But we set three rules. One: every hundred steps, I cut a mark on a tree. Two: once the sun's past that ridge, caught it or not, we turn round and go. Three—" he looked his father in the eye, "if anything at all seems wrong, whatever it is, we don't discuss it. We run."

His father looked at him for a long moment. "All right. We'll do it your way." Then: "…Your mother taught you that?" Xiaoman said, "Mm. Ma taught me." His father let out a long, long sigh. Half of it was relief, and the other half was a little bit of jealousy. He said, "You take after her. Good that you take after her."

Xiaoman tied the pheasant to the quiver strap on his father's back. The two of them stepped over the broken stone. Xiaoman cut the first mark on the tree beside him. His hand shook a little and it came out crooked, so he cut another one next to it. This time it wasn't crooked. He decided on the spot that the first one didn't count.

---

The trees on Black Pine Ridge were twice as tall as the ones outside. Their bark was black, and cold to the touch, like touching a slab of iron. The pine needles underfoot were as thick as a rug, and when you trod on them they made no sound at all. His father went in front, and for the first time in all these years he walked more lightly than his son.

Past noon, the hoof prints led them to the edge of a mountain stream, where they were pressed deep and clear into the soft mud of the bank. It was a big one; the prints were wider than Xiaoman's palm. The near hills hadn't seen a deer that size in ten years. Xiaoman read the wind first: the tips of the reeds along the stream all leaned their way; the wind was blowing from the deer towards them, and it couldn't smell them. His father glanced at the reed tips, glanced at his son, and said nothing, and yet Xiaoman felt he'd been praised. His father crouched on the ground, touching a hoof print, his hand trembling, and said in a low voice, "One deer hide… that covers the Ma rent. The meat that's left, we trade for a new padded jacket for your mother. A leg for Granny Wu. And for Ahe's family… a cabbage to make up for theirs." He looked up and smiled at Xiaoman. Since Ma San left, it was the first time Xiaoman had seen him smile. One deer, and he'd cut it into four shares, and not one of them was for himself.

---

The hoof prints went deeper into Black Pine Ridge. The two of them followed for another two hours. Counting from the broken stone, Xiaoman had cut forty-one marks along the way, until his fingers went numb.

Deep in the forest, from somewhere overhead, came a long bird call, trailing off at the end, thin and far away. In the ear it sounded like someone a long, long way off, calling over and over: "…Come back…" His father stopped and tilted his head to listen. The call came three times and then stopped. His father said, "Hill partridge. Sounds like a person when it calls. If something in the mountains calls your name, whatever you do, don't answer." With that he bent his head and went back to the hoof prints.

Xiaoman worked it out in his head: the bird was a hill partridge. It had an explanation, so it didn't count as "something wrong", and the third rule wasn't needed. But he gripped his knife tighter.

In the end it was Xiaoman who felt it first. The birds had stopped — not the silence that had always been on Black Pine Ridge, but a few calls a moment ago, and then all at once nothing, as if someone had clapped a hand over the mouth of the whole mountain. A smell drifted on the wind, like rust, or like the earth that turns up before a thunderstorm. First read the wind — he looked up. The treetops weren't moving at all, but the clouds over the ridge at the edge of the sky were pressing down, low and black, like a huge iron wok turned upside down.

He grabbed his father. "Pa, the third rule." His father had seen it too. He looked at the sky, then ahead. The hoof prints were still fresh. The deer was just in front; perhaps another half mile and they'd see it. One deer hide, twenty catties of meat, the bow on the wall, two days left — Xiaoman saw his father clench his jaw, then turn round. "Come on. We're going back."

The two of them ran back the way they'd come. Xiaoman counted the marks back, one by one: forty-one, forty, thirty-nine… And then the sky fell in. One moment there was still a thread of light leaking through the trees, and the next the forest was pitch black, as if someone had pulled a black cloth bigger than the sky over the whole of Mount Meng in one go. Wind poured down off the peak, every black pine on the mountain bent at the waist together and moaned, as if the whole mountain were crying. Then the rain came smashing down — not falling, smashing. Drops as big as soybeans, stinging on the face, crackling on the leaves like a thousand people letting off firecrackers at once. In the blink of an eye the mountain path became a river, muddy water carrying broken stones, rushing straight downhill. Xiaoman couldn't see more than three steps ahead. All he could see was his father's limping back, swaying in and out of the curtain of rain.

At the twenty-seventh mark the path became a narrow slope hugging a cliff. Here his father's left knee gave way, and his whole body tipped towards the drop.

Xiaoman didn't even think — no, that's wrong. He thought, and he thought fast, as fast as his mother working the beads of an abacus: Pa two steps away, the drop past the slope. Pull him, and maybe they'd both go over. Don't pull him, and he'd certainly go over. It wasn't a hard sum. Quicker than it takes to tell, Xiaoman threw himself forward, grabbed his father's belt and hauled inward with everything he had, hauled so hard that his father slammed into the cliff face with an "Oof!" — and the force of it swung Xiaoman himself out and away. His father spun round and grabbed for him. His fingers brushed Xiaoman's wrist, and in the end they didn't catch it.

---

Xiaoman fell. Mud, stones, broken branches and rain came at him from every side; sky and earth turned more somersaults in front of his eyes than he could count, until he couldn't tell which way was up. He covered his head. His back slammed into something hard, so painful everything went black. He grabbed blindly and actually caught hold of a vine. The vine went taut and, for one moment, it jerked his fall to a stop; then it snapped.

At the last he landed in something soft. It was moss, a layer of it, very thick and very soft, like a huge quilt that had soaked up all the water it could hold. He sank half a foot into it, like an ant fallen into a giant steamed bun.

He didn't get up straight away. His father had said: when you fall, don't rush to get up; check yourself, one thing at a time. First he moved his fingers; the fingers moved. Then his toes; the toes moved too. His knees were skinned, a layer of skin was scraped off his palms, his back was burning, and his left wrist had swollen up high — broken or not, he couldn't feel, but at least it still moved. Only then did he sit up and look up. The cliff was too high to see the top; it vanished into the rain and mist.

He didn't shout. He just did the sum in his head: Pa had been hauled into the cliff face by him and hadn't fallen. With his bad knee and rain this heavy, he couldn't get down, and shouldn't try. He'd wait for the rain to stop, follow the marks back to the village, and fetch help. Forty-one marks, cut by his own hand, not one of them crooked — except the first. So what he had to do was stay alive until then.

Twelve years old, alone, in a valley he'd never been to, and night coming on. His father had said that when a hunter gets lost in the mountains, the first thing is to find somewhere out of the rain, the second is to make a fire, and only the third is to be afraid. Xiaoman decided the third thing could stay owing for now.

---

The rain eased a little. He felt about on the ground for a sharp stone and went on along the foot of the cliff, one hand on the rock, and every so often he scratched a line on the cliff face so he'd remember the way back.

He'd gone less than half a mile when, in the moss at the foot of the cliff, the sharp stone struck something hard with a *clink*. He scraped the moss away. It was a fire steel, rusted red; its sheath had long since rotted away, and beside it was a flint wrapped in oilcloth, the oilcloth half rotted too. On the wooden handle of the fire steel a character had been cut with a knife, crooked and wobbly, and he knew it. It was the first character his mother had taught him to write: **Shen**. He crouched there looking at it for a long time. In Shi'ao Village there was only one household named Shen, and of the Shens only one had gone into Black Pine Ridge and not come back. He remembered Granny Wu saying "The year your grandpa went, he passed my door too", and his palms went cold. Grandpa had been here. Grandpa had died in this valley. He tucked the fire steel and the flint inside his jacket. He didn't tell anyone about it, ever.

This valley was very strange. The mountains outside grew pine and cypress and wild chestnut, but here he didn't know a single tree. Their leaves were silver-white, so thin the light came through them, and rain that landed on them didn't run off but rolled into round beads that hung from the tips. He couldn't help himself; he reached out and touched one. There was a faint *ting*, like a very, very tiny bell, and the bead turned into a wisp of white vapour and was gone without a trace. The grass on the ground was different too. With every step, the blades gave off a faint light, and a pale ring of it spread out from under his foot and slowly faded again, as if he were walking on water. He walked very, very lightly. He didn't know why, but he felt that everything here was asleep, and he shouldn't wake it.

And then he saw the light. In the deepest part of the valley, in a shallow hollow the rain had battered out of the ground, a point of silver light was glowing and dimming, glowing and dimming, as if something were breathing very, very slowly.

---

He didn't go over. First he crouched behind a silver-leafed tree and watched for a long time. There were no footprints around the hollow, no marks of anything bleeding being dragged, and nothing else stirring anywhere. He picked up a dead branch and threw it to the edge of the hollow. The light went on glowing and dimming and paid it no attention. Only then did he edge over, a little at a time.

In the hollow lay a [fox]{注=yinyue-fox}. A very small one, a size smaller than Granny Wu's cat — Granny Wu's cat was as fat as a pillow; this fox was maybe half a pillow. It was curled in the muddy water at the bottom of the hollow, and its fur was silver. Not grey: true silver, like moonlight frozen into a layer of frost and laid over it. Rain that landed on it neither clung nor soaked in; it just slid off. But it was badly hurt. One hind leg was twisted, there were several gashes on its body, and blood had stained the silver fur dark red. Behind it there was only one tail, and at the root of that tail were several stumps, cut clean and even, as if something very fast and very sharp had cut them off, one stroke after another. Xiaoman counted. Eight in all.

He froze. He took Granny Wu's tablet out of his jacket and, by that little bit of silver light, counted the tails of the little curled-up fox on it, one by one. He counted them again and again. Nine.

Just then the fox in the hollow opened its eyes. They were golden eyes, with slit pupils, cold as well water in winter. It looked at him and bared its teeth, and a small, fierce whimper came from its throat. But it couldn't even lift its head.

His father had said a wounded animal is the fiercest kind; don't so much as touch it. Xiaoman looked at it for a long time and thought: if it dies here, it won't be anything at all. If it lives — who knows what it'll be. But he did know that something whose tails had been cut off one stroke at a time, and a child who'd been shoved against a stove, weren't so very different.

He took off his jacket. His mother had made it last winter out of his father's old padded coat; there were patches on the cuffs, and patches on the patches. He lifted it on a dead branch, laid it gently over the fox, and then picked it up, wrapped in the jacket. It bit him anyway, through two layers of cloth, in the web of his thumb. Not deep, but it hurt. He hissed, but he didn't let go. "Don't be scared. I fell down here too. We'll keep each other company."

It stopped biting. It shivered once inside his jacket, and then it buried its face in a patch.

---

At the foot of the cliff he found a shallow cave. First he threw a stone in and listened: empty. He threw another and listened again: still empty. He was about to pick up a third, then thought better of it and went in. Using the way his father had taught him, he struck the flint with the fire steel, lit some dry moss and pine resin, and got a little fire going. With a fire across the mouth of the cave, animals wouldn't dare come in.

Then he took two things out of his jacket: the boiled egg Ahe had given him, and the thick flatbread from his mother — the last of the Shens' white flour. He peeled the egg, broke off half, and put it by the fox's mouth. It sniffed it, looked at him, and only then slowly ate it. He thought about it, then put the other half by its mouth too. It sniffed, looked at him again, and ate that as well. Xiaoman groaned to himself: Ahe, I'm sorry. The interest on this egg is going to be three, at least.

Then came the flatbread. He broke it in two, gave half to the fox, and kept half himself. He'd been hungry all day, and the half flatbread in his hand was as heavy as a lump of gold. The fox finished its half and looked at the half in his hand, then looked at him. He looked at it; it looked at him; the fire crackled. He broke a big piece off his own half and put it by its mouth, wrapped up the small corner that was left, and stuffed it into the very bottom of his bundle, to save for tomorrow's breakfast. The fox finished, licked its lips, turned its head away and shut its eyes, without so much as a thank-you.

He tore a strip off the hem of his shirt, wet it with rainwater, and bit by bit cleaned the mud out of its wounds. It didn't bite him any more. Only, when he touched a sore place, its ears would flatten back and it would give a small whimper. In the firelight he saw its wounds closing, slowly, slowly.

The wounds closed; but not long after full dark the fox ran a fever. Through the old jacket, heat came off it in waves, hot enough to make his palms sweat. He lifted a corner of the cloth and saw it curled up tighter than ever, the skin on its nose dry and flaking, its breath short and fast, each breath reaching its throat and turning back, like a torn pair of bellows. His father had said that when a creature in the mountains is badly hurt, it always runs a fever the first night: get through it and you live, don't and you don't. He remembered that very clearly, because when his father had said it he'd added: "Same with people."

He had no medicine, only rainwater. He tore the strip of hem into two pieces. One he soaked and laid on the fox's forehead; the other he held out at the mouth of the cave to catch the rain, and when the first one warmed up he swapped it for the second. Whoever keeps the fire can't sleep; fall asleep, and the fire goes out. He thought and thought, and there was only one way to keep himself awake, and that was to talk. There was nobody else in the cave, only a fox, so he talked to the fox. He was soaked through himself, and his jacket was wrapped round the fox, so he was so cold his teeth chattered, and he kept shifting closer and closer to the fire.

He'd meant to start by telling it his name, then thought: a fox — even if it remembered, it would have nowhere to write it down. So he skipped that. He told it about his mother's seventeen basins; about his father's knee that could chat; that the first day of winter was the day after tomorrow, and Ma San was coming to take his grandpa's bow, and that this was called a son paying his father's debts. When he got to Ma Xiaobao he said, "He's always got a piece of osmanthus cake in his hand. I still don't know what osmanthus cake tastes like. It smells nice. Have you ever had any? — Oh, you're a fox. You eat chickens." At the words "osmanthus cake" its ear twitched. He thought it was just the fever bothering it. A fox — how could it understand people talk?

He told it about the bowl, too: how it turned two somersaults in the air, and he grabbed for it, and was an inch short. "That's how everyone in my family is," he said. "When somebody hurts, nobody says it hurts. Pa's knee hurts, and he says it's chatting. Ma crouches on the floor picking up the bowl and doesn't say anything. I'm all smashed up like this, and I'm not saying anything either. In our family, the rule is: if it hurts, you don't say so, and nobody asks. If they ask, you have to answer; if you answer, you'll cry; if you cry, the food goes cold." When he'd said that, he got up and changed the cloth again. The fox was still hot.

Around midnight the fire burned low. He ducked out of the cave, felt his way to the foot of the cliff and snapped off a few dead branches. Every branch he snapped set the whole tree's silver leaves *tinging* all together, as if they were telling him off. He could only say, quietly, "Sorry. Just borrowing a couple. I'll give them back tomorrow." With the wood in his arms he pricked up his ears towards the top of the cliff and listened. All he could hear was the rain; nothing else. His father's ears could hear a river deer three miles off. His own ears were only ordinary.

He ducked back into the cave with the wood, stirred the fire up again, turned round — and saw it lying there, not moving at all.

His head went *hummm*. He threw himself down and scooped it up. It was still hot, but the rise and fall of its chest — he looked and looked, and couldn't see it. He put his ear against it and listened, and couldn't hear a thing, only his own heart hammering away. Holding it, he didn't know what to do with his hands or feet. On an ordinary day he had an answer for everything, and now, round and round, the only thing he could get out was: "Hey. Hey. Don't you—" And then, somehow, he started doing sums again, and as he did them he shouted at it: "You ate my family's last white flour! You ate Ahe's egg! You bit me! If you die, who am I supposed to collect those debts from?" When he'd finished shouting, the cave was dead quiet. The fire crackled once. After a good while — maybe only as long as one breath, though to him it felt longer than a whole night — its chest, gently, swelled once. And swelled again. Holding it, he sat down hard on the ground and couldn't speak for a long time. His back was soaked in cold sweat.

After that he didn't dare stop talking, as if, if he stopped, its breath would stop with him. He went over his whole twelve years again and again, and by the end he didn't know what he was saying himself; only his mouth didn't stop and his hands didn't stop: change the cloth, add wood, change the cloth again, add wood again. Towards dawn the rain stopped. He reached out and felt it. It wasn't hot any more, and its breathing had evened out, one breath after another, long and steady, exactly like that point of silver light in the hollow, glowing and dimming.

He was worn out. Holding it, he leaned back against the rock and at some point fell asleep. Just before he fell asleep, he thought he heard someone say something by his ear in a very, very small voice — not a fox's cry, but people talk, a girl's voice, rather grudging: "…Mortal. This queen will remember you."

---

Xiaoman was woken by the sound of someone smacking their lips. The fire was out; grey daylight came in at the mouth of the cave; the rain had stopped. His arms were empty. The jacket lay folded neatly to one side, and the fox was gone.

Someone was sitting at the mouth of the cave: a girl, who looked only a few years older than him, with silver hair falling straight past her waist, bare feet, and wrapped in clothes such as he'd never seen, white as snow and bright as water. She had her back to him and was eating something — the corner of flatbread he'd hidden at the very bottom of his bundle for breakfast. As she chewed she said, "Disgusting." And took another big bite.

Xiaoman looked at that corner of flatbread and felt a twist of pain in his heart. Once there was a corner of flatbread at the bottom of his bundle; he did not eat it, he saved it for breakfast; only when somebody else had eaten it did he regret it, too late. Of all the sorrows of this world, none is greater than this. Quietly he slid his hand towards a stone beside him. Without looking round she said, "Put it down." Then, in a ringing voice: "This queen is Lady of Qingqiu, Demon Queen of the immortal realm. Ten thousand demons answer to me; the eight wildernesses obey my word. When I rage, mountains fall; when I smile, flowers bloom—" Xiaoman cut in: "Then why are you eating my flatbread?" She said, "—I was hungry."

For three breaths the cave was silent. A crow flew past the mouth of the cave going *caw, caw*, and it sounded very much like laughing.

She turned her head. Golden eyes, slit pupils. She glanced at the stone in his hand and the corner of her mouth went up. "Not bad. Knows to grab a stone first instead of calling for his mother." She stood up, came over, and looked him up and down like someone at the market picking over a cabbage that isn't very fresh. "Twelve years old. Bones and sinews — so-so. The eyes — also so-so. Spirit root—" she clicked her tongue, "this queen can tell at a glance: the kind that will never become an immortal in this life. Tsk. Rotten through."

Xiaoman said, "…Are you that fox?" She snapped, "How dare you! This queen's name is—" And there she stopped short. Her brows drew tighter and tighter. "Is…" Xiaoman waited. A squirrel poked its head in at the mouth of the cave, looked at them, and waited too.

She said, "…You say your name first." Xiaoman said, "You've forgotten?" Her face flushed bright red at once. "This queen has not forgotten! This queen is—" She coughed. "Testing you!" Xiaoman said, "Shen Xiaoman." She said, "Good. Correct." Xiaoman said, "…You still haven't said your name." She said, "This queen…"

The squirrel couldn't wait any longer, and left. She strained for a long time, strained till her face was red, and finally squeezed out two syllables: "…[Yinyue]{注=yinyue}." Then: "Yes. Yinyue. This queen is called Yinyue." She let out a long breath of relief, and at once she was all righteous confidence again. Up went her chin. "Mortal! You saved this queen. This queen grants you one wish. Speak. What will it be? Eternal life? Riches beyond counting? A fairy for a wife?"

Xiaoman thought about it. He thought about it very seriously, for a long time, and said, "…Give me back my flatbread."

Her mouth hung open, "a fairy for a wife" still dangling on her lips unretracted, her golden eyes round as coins. After a long moment she looked down at the flatbread in her hand, of which only a small corner remained, then looked up at him, and then, very, very solemnly, holding the corner in both hands as if it were the imperial seal of the realm — put it into her own mouth, chewed twice, swallowed, and said: "Agreed. This queen owes you one flatbread."

Xiaoman gaped at her. In the morning light the silver-white tail behind her swished gently. Just the one, and at its root eight stumps, cut clean and even, perfectly clear in the light. All at once he didn't want the flatbread any more. He asked, "…Does it hurt?"

She froze and looked at him, looked at him for a long time — so long that three drops of dew fell from the trees, and each one went *ting*. In those golden eyes, cold as well water, something wavered. Then she turned her face away. "…This queen is a demon queen. A demon queen doesn't know what hurting is."

She said it very fast, so fast it was as if she was afraid that if she said it slowly, it would come out as something else.

She lowered her head, and at that moment saw the wooden tablet he'd put down on the ground. Her eyes went wide all at once, and her voice changed: "This. Where did you get it?" Xiaoman said, "Granny Wu in our village gave it to me. She said… when my grandpa went into the mountains, he passed her door too." Yinyue stared at the tablet, stared for a long time. Then she picked it up, handed it back to him, and said nothing. What a demon queen owed him — and she'd eaten the receipt.

She brushed some dust that wasn't there off her clothes and was back to being a demon queen again. Up went her chin. "This queen has had enough of this valley." She stamped her two bare feet lightly on the ground, and a puff of milky cloud rose up under her and bore her up, floating gently, one foot, two feet, three feet… Xiaoman craned his neck to watch, his mouth open wide enough to fit an egg. At about five feet up the cloud suddenly wobbled, like the steam off a pot with the lid lifted, and with a *pff* it was gone. She went "Ah!", flailed her arms and legs in mid-air like a cat thrown in the water, dropped straight down, and landed with a *thump* on her bottom in the grass, both bare feet in the air. The grass lit up ring after ring around her, very prettily, as if the whole valley's grass were laughing at her. The squirrel that had got tired of waiting and left had come back at some point, and sat on a stone at the mouth of the cave, watching very closely.

Xiaoman quickly looked down at his toes, clenching his jaw so hard his eyes watered. She sat on the ground without getting up, her face flaming, even the tips of her ears gone red. "…No laughing." Xiaoman said, "I'm not laughing." She said, "Your shoulders are shaking." Xiaoman said, "That's because I'm cold." She glared at him for a long time before slowly getting to her feet, and said, very properly, "This queen was testing how hard the ground is." Xiaoman said, "Is it soft?" She said, "…Not bad." After a pause she began muttering again, under her breath, like a child at school reciting a lesson to herself: "[A man's life is the gathering of qi; gathered, it is life; scattered, it is death]{典=zhuangzi-zhibeiyou}." When she'd finished reciting she yanked up a blade of grass and, pointing both nostrils at him, said, "This queen scattered from hunger." Then: "…When your father's knee walked off the job, you didn't laugh at him either." Xiaoman stopped. His father's knee walking off the job was something he'd said last night, to a fox with its eyes shut. He thought of the other things he'd said to it last night: osmanthus cake, the rule about not saying it hurts, and that shout of "who am I supposed to collect those debts from" — and his face went hot. He had no idea how much of it she'd actually heard.

But she'd already turned her face away and was looking around. The valley was cliffs on every side, vines hanging down them, silver-leafed trees one after another, every one of them exactly the same. So Xiaoman asked, "Do you know the way?" She said, "This queen is a demon queen." Xiaoman said, "Do demon queens know the way?" She said, "Demon queens fly." Xiaoman said, "Do flying ones know the way?" She didn't answer. Xiaoman thought this sum wasn't hard either: she could fly, but right now she couldn't get off the ground; he couldn't fly, but all the way in he'd scratched a line on the cliff every so often, and every one was still there. A demon queen who couldn't get off the ground and a hunter's boy who scratched lines on cliffs: right now the second was worth more. She seemed to have worked it out too. She coughed. "Come along, mortal. You may lead this queen out first."

She turned an ear towards the top of the cliff, listened a moment, and said, "Your father is on that slope where you hauled him in. He called for you all night. He's stopped calling now." Xiaoman's heart eased, then tightened again. "…Stopped calling?"

"He's undoing his belt," she said. "Undone his belt, now his leg wrappings, tying them together piece by piece, one end lashed to a tree root on the slope." She cast him a glance. "If you don't go now, he's going to climb down the cliff."
