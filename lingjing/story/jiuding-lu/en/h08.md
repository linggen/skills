# Chapter Six: The Five Phases Take First Place on the Stage / A Mother’s Voice in the River-and-Luo Sword Art

On the seventh of the twelfth month, in the outer sect of the Sunken Cauldron Abbey, under the old pagoda tree outside the kitchen door, a wooden board went up.

At the top of the board, in big charcoal letters: **SECT GRAND CONTEST · BET ON THE CHAMPION**. Below, line after line of names, each followed by a number. The first few lines were all inner sect: first Qi Changsong, followed by "evens"; second Qin Yan, two to one; below them several inner-sect senior brothers, three to one, four to one; only further down did the outer sect come in, Ma Xiaobao at eight to one, a few older disciples at ten-something to one; further down still the names got smaller and the numbers bigger, and at the very bottom, squeezed into the corner of the board, one line of writing thinner than a mosquito's leg: **Shen Xiaoman · a hundred to one.** The crowd round it dissolved into laughter. One man laughed loudest, going "Hahaha" and slapping his thigh till his thigh was red, laughing till he cried: "A hundred! Shopkeeper Qian's got a bodhisattva's heart, he's giving money away!"

Beside the board sat a fat man in a silk jacket washed pale, an abacus in his left hand and a steelyard in his right; from the steelyard hung a little cloth bag that jingled and clinked, all copper coins and chips of spirit stone. A small wooden plaque was nailed to the top of the board, with eight characters on it: **PRICES MARKED PLAIN · NO CHEATING YOUNG OR OLD.** Ahe squeezed in beside Xiaoman, jerked her chin at the fat man, and said low: "Shopkeeper Qian. Runs the betting at the market. Nine bets in ten, he pays out." Outsiders couldn't come up the mountain, but Shopkeeper Qian didn't count as an outsider: every year at the contest, the abbey took ten spirit stones from him as stall fee and let him bring his stall up for two days, and Zhou Heng's ledger entered a line of income for the common fund.

Xiaoman squeezed up to the board: "Shopkeeper, a hundred to one, how's that worked out?" Shopkeeper Qian didn't look up. He clicked the abacus three times, *tak tak tak*: "A mixed spirit root on the stage: not one in ten wins. Three months in the sect: not one in ten wins. First time on the stage: not one in ten wins either. All three at once: one in ten, picked three times over, that's one in a thousand. It ought to say a thousand to one. I write a hundred; the other nine hundred is mine." Xiaoman's eyeballs went round three times following the abacus beads and came out dizzy. An older disciple beside him sniggered: "Shopkeeper Qian lose money? The sun'll come up in the west." Shopkeeper Qian didn't take offence. He tapped the eight characters at the top with his steelyard.

As he drew the steelyard back, his eyes lifted and came to rest on the bow on Xiaoman's back, and the abacus beads stopped. "Let me heft it." He took the bow, weighed it in his hand, flicked the back of it with a fingernail and cocked his ear to listen; then held the bow up to his eye and looked along the grain a good while, looking very slowly, not at all the way he looked at the names on the board. When he had finished he handed it back and said: "Seventeen layers of ox sinew, worth three qian of silver. The string's new, worth fifty coppers. This wood—" He paused. "I can't put a name to it. If you ever find out, let me know. A name, I'll buy at its price, and not a copper short."

The crowd suddenly parted. Ma Xiaobao had arrived. He had changed into yet another cultivator's robe today, and at his waist hung a copper mirror the size of a palm, face outwards, polished snow-bright. His face Xiaoman almost didn't recognise: blotch after blotch, red and purple, spreading from his chin right up to his temples, like a stove top nobody had wiped clean. Yesterday Ma San had come by boat to the foot of the mountain. The outer sect didn't let outsiders in, so he had moored his boat on the Si and had someone send up the mountain a gilded food box, three medicine bottles, and this mirror. The Ma family's pills had force-fed him up to the third layer in two months; these three medicine bottles were all empty in a single night.

Ma Xiaobao took a cloth bag out of his robe and tossed it onto the steelyard pan with a clatter: a bag of spirit stones. "On Ma Xiaobao." Shopkeeper Qian said: "Young Master Ma bets on himself? Eight to one." Ma Xiaobao said: "I'm not after your payout." He turned his face and smiled at Xiaoman. "What I'm betting isn't money. It's a prize." Having said it, he flicked the copper mirror with a finger: *tang*. When he'd gone, someone in the crowd said that Master Ma, for this one Foundation Pill, had sold three tenths of the trees on Mount Meng. Xiaoman squeezed out of the crowd and looked out beyond the mountain gate. At the foot of the mountain, on the Si, a boat was moored with a banner planted at its bow, embroidered with a line of gold characters you could read from a long way off: **MA XIAOBAO · BOUNDLESS IMMORTAL ROAD**. At the bow sat a man as wide as a door, shelling peanuts.

Behind Xiaoman, Ahe asked: "Who are you betting on?" Xiaoman said: "I'm betting on myself." Ahe looked him up and down: "With what?" Xiaoman felt in his robe and came out with only one spirit stone, the one he'd drawn on the first of the twelfth month; the pouchful from the secret realm had long since gone into the Rice Bucket. He gripped it once and tucked it back: "This one has a use." He opened his hand to show Ahe: "I'm betting nothing." Ahe said nothing. When Xiaoman turned back to the board he caught sight of her squeezing to the steelyard pan, taking something very small from her sleeve and putting it on; Shopkeeper Qian looked at it and wrote her a slip of paper, and she folded it small and tucked it into her little notebook. Xiaoman asked her who she'd bet on. She said: "Not telling you."

---

That evening everyone who had signed up had to go to the lecture hall to have their cultivation checked, so it could be written into the register: whatever layer you were was your layer, and the drawing of lots and the seating went by it. The one checking was Master Chu. He sat behind the lectern, and the entrants went up one by one and laid their wrists on a pulse pillow padded with blue cloth. He laid two fingers on, eyes closed, held them a while, called out a number, and the steward beside him wrote a number in the register.

Ahead of Xiaoman was Ma Xiaobao. As Master Chu felt his wrist, his brows knitted a little at a time. He glanced at the blotches on the boy's face, and in the end called out: "Fifth layer." He didn't let go, and said: "Two layers grown in one night. Those blotches on your face are pill poison working its way out. The spiritual power is hollow. On the stage tomorrow, don't use your full strength." Ma Xiaobao pulled his wrist back and smiled: "Don't worry, Master. My family's pills come from the best shop in the county town." Master Chu said: "What the best shop makes is still one-turn." He wiped his two fingers on the blue cloth and said slowly: "The *Daodejing*{典=laozi-qizhe} says: 'One on tiptoe does not stand; one who straddles does not walk.' Up on your toes, you can't stand; taking two steps in one, you don't get far. You straddled two layers in a night. Whether you can stand tomorrow, weigh that for yourself." Ma Xiaobao wasn't listening. He turned round with a grin and lifted his chin at Xiaoman.

It was Xiaoman's turn. He laid his wrist on the pulse pillow. Master Chu's two fingers were dry and cold, and as soon as they settled, Xiaoman felt them probing inwards, like two bamboo skewers poking an inch at a time into a stove.

"Fourth layer." Master Chu opened his eyes, looked at him, felt a while longer, and only then drew back his fingers and waved him on. The steward wrote it down. Someone below muttered: "Picked it up in the secret realm."

That month, in the lecture hall, Master Chu had taught five of the plainest spells: fireball, ice spike, metal blade, wood vine, earth wall. On the first day he taught no spells. He taught hands first, for a whole day.

"A spell is a road for spiritual power to go out by." He tapped his own arm with the ferule, from the shoulder down to the fingertips. "Spiritual power is stored in the dantian, like water stored in a vat. To send it out to water the fields, you need a ditch. An elder who has formed his core has his ditches built inside his body: a stir of the heart and the water's at the field's edge. Your ditches aren't dug yet; the water gets to your shoulder and spills everywhere. This road inside the meridians, you can't see, and your spirit sense can't carry it either. So you lead it with your hand. Where the hand goes, the qi goes. This is called pinching a sign."

Someone asked: "Master, why the hand? Couldn't you lead it with your foot?" The whole hall laughed. Master Chu didn't: "That's not a stupid question." He held up a hand, five fingers spread. "There are twelve main meridians in the body, and six run to the hands. The ends of all six are in the fingertips: the lung meridian to the thumb, the large-intestine meridian to the forefinger, the pericardium meridian to the middle finger, the triple-burner meridian to the ring finger, and the little finger has two, the heart meridian and the small-intestine meridian. When the qi gets here, there's no more road: it either turns back or goes out. The fingertips are six doors." He bent his thumb over and touched it to each of the four fingers in turn. "Of the five fingers, only this one can reach the other four. It's the door bolt. Wherever it pinches, that door opens. Your toes: bend one for me, let's see."

He opened his left hand with the palm towards them and touched the tip of the ferule to it joint by joint. "The *Jade Register of Great Purity*{典=yuce-qiajue} says: 'Count from the root of the fourth finger, joint by joint: twelve eyes in all, to match the twelve hours.' The fourth finger is the ring finger." The ferule touched the base of the ring finger. "This is the zi mark." It moved over to the base of the middle finger. "This is the chou mark." Then to the base of the forefinger: "This is the yin mark." Climbing the forefinger joint by joint, the mao mark, the chen mark, and at the fingertip the si mark; the tip of the middle finger, wu; the tip of the ring finger, wei; the tip of the little finger, shen; then down the little finger joint by joint, you, xu, and at the base of the little finger, hai. "Once round, twelve of them, set out the same as the twelve hours of the day. Each one is called a mark. Whichever mark the thumb pinches, the qi goes out by that mark." Dozens of left hands below opened all at once, and dozens of thumbs counted along joint by joint. Sun Ergou got to the base of his little finger and counted thirteen, and started again from the beginning.

"Each of the twelve marks governs a direction." He held up his middle finger. "Fire is in the south, and south is wu. For a fireball, pinch the wu mark. Water is in the north: the ice spike pinches the zi mark. Wood is in the east: the wood vine pinches the mao mark. Metal is in the west: the metal blade pinches the you mark." Someone asked: "Master, then where do you pinch for earth?" Master Chu said: "Earth is in the middle." His thumb came down on the middle joint of the middle finger. "This joint has another name in the books: the Jade Purity seal, which is said to govern all things in the three realms. You'll use it to push up an earth wall chest high, and that's quite enough."

"A mark is a point. Set several fingers together and it's a form." He held forefinger and middle finger straight together, thumb pressing down the nails of the ring finger and little finger. "The sword sign. Swordsmen use it to lead sword qi." Then his thumb pinched the zi mark at the base of the ring finger and his four fingers closed, wrapping the thumb inside, into a fist. "This, in the books, is called the thunder lock." He held the fist out for the front row to see. "Look familiar? Every newborn baby clenches its fist like this, thumb inside. The *Laozi*{典=laozi-wogu} says, 'its bones are weak and its sinews soft, yet its grip is firm.' Every one of you could do it when you were born, and grew up and forgot. This fist, you'll be using it again the day after tomorrow."

"And pinching signs isn't only for fighting," he said. "The pill-masters in the pill house feeding fire into the furnace, the herb-garden people feeding the seedlings, the stewards drawing talismans: which of them doesn't pinch? A Taoist down the mountain treating a sick person takes a jujube, pinches the wu mark, faces south and draws a breath, breathes it onto the jujube and has the sick person eat it. That's pinching a sign too. They've no spiritual power in their hands, so it works one time in ten. You have." He paused and rapped the back of his own hand with the ferule. "So remember one thing: when you fight, guard your hands first. For a Qi Condensation cultivator, break one finger and you've lost a road. Ruin both hands, and all your spiritual power just stays bottled up in the vat."

At the end he said: "Within the twelve marks you can also pick out the eight trigrams, the seven stars, the nine palaces, each with its uses. That's for later. You won't need them." Xiaoman sat in the back row looking down at his left hand. Forefinger, middle finger, ring finger, three joints each, three by three, nine squares. His thumb walked half round those nine squares and stopped, and he didn't think any further.

Master Chu pushed up his sleeve and said under his breath: "Bing and Ding well up, gather the qi into a ball. Swiftly, swiftly, as the statutes command." His right hand went forward, his thumb pinched the tip of his middle finger, and at the fingertip a fireball blossomed with a *pop*, fist-sized, glowing red, hanging in mid-air and turning three times; his fingers closed, and it went out. From start to finish, only that one hand had moved. "You lot: you'll swing both arms through a whole routine, leading the qi from the shoulder all the way down to the hand, and only that last bit is the pinch; not one word of the incantation may be dropped, and a dozen breaths makes one." Someone below said quietly: "How come Master only moves one hand?" Master Chu's goatee twitched up: "Practice makes perfect."

On the second day he taught incantations. "The hand is the ditch, the incantation is the man who drives the water," said Master Chu. "Your qi is lazy. Unless it's driven, it won't move. One sound out of your mouth and the qi goes forward a stretch; one line, one stretch of road, and eight lines take it exactly from the shoulder to the fingertips." He said a line and moved his arm one step: "Heaven and earth, as they are— Bing and Ding well up— fire lads, fire lasses— come down from heaven— true qi of Li in the south— run along the arm— gather in the palm— one point, one ball— swiftly, swiftly, as the statutes command!" Eight lines, one step each, and at "one point, one ball" the thumb pinched the wu mark; the last line was shouted, and the paper in the windows hummed. "Loudly," said Master Chu. "If no sound comes out of your mouth, the qi won't go."

Someone asked: "Master, the last line, 'swiftly, swiftly, as the statutes command': who's it said to?" Master Chu said: "To the qi. It was the line at the end of a government document: by the statutes, see to it at once. You're the official, the qi is the runner. If you don't sound like an official, the runner won't budge." Ma Xiaobao laughed in the front row: "Then can I shout 'Get going'?" Master Chu said: "Then shout it and try." Ma Xiaobao swung through a whole routine and shouted "Get going," and there was nothing at his fingertip; instead he let out a burp, which smelled of osmanthus cake. Master Chu said: "The runner doesn't know you."

"Whether an incantation works isn't about how long it is," he said again. "The *Baopuzi*{典=baopu-liujia} records one for going into the mountains, called the Secret Invocation of the Six Jia, nine characters in all: 'Approach, soldiers, fighters: all in ranks, go forward.' Ge Hong says, 'the essential way is not wordy': what matters doesn't ramble." Eyes lit up below: "Master, then let's say the nine-character one!" Master Chu's goatee twitched: "For someone who knows the road well, nine characters are enough; know it better still and a twitch of the lips is enough. Your road isn't even trodden yet. You can't spare a single line. Eight lines, not one word dropped. Drop a word and that stretch of road has nobody driving, and the qi stops halfway." And sure enough, someone dropped the line "run along the arm," and a spark came out of his elbow and burned a hole in his sleeve.

That afternoon every arm in the lecture hall was swinging, like a room full of people swatting flies; a roomful of people all shouting "Swiftly, swiftly, as the statutes command" together, shouting until dust fell from the roof beams. Xiaoman, in the back row, swung for the best part of an hour and said the incantation thirty-odd times, until his voice was hoarse. At the seventh step, "gather in the palm," a spark showed at his fingertip and went out, *pft*. He swung from the start again, and it went out again. Only when both arms ached and the stretch of spiritual power in his dantian was nearly swung dry did a fireball roll out of his palm, the size of a chestnut, wobble two feet through the air, and land on the toe of Sun Ergou's shoe. Sun Ergou jumped up slapping at his shoe, and the whole hall roared.

On the third day Master Chu taught holding. "When you fight, nobody's going to stand there waiting while you swing for a dozen breaths." His thumb pinched the wu mark and stayed, and his four fingers closed, wrapping the thumb, into half a fist: the baby's fist from the first day. A little red showed through the hollow of the fist, not bright, not going out. "Pinch it first, hold it in your hand, and when you need it, let go." He held the fist up for them all to see. "The hand that's holding can't do anything else: it can't carry a bowl, can't block a blade. And the qi still leaks out thread by thread between the fingers; hold too long and it scatters by itself, and you've pinched for nothing."

Someone asked how long you could hold. Master Chu said: "Depends how tight your hand is. Fourth layer, one incense stick; sixth layer, the best part of an hour. Fingers that close tight hold a bit longer; sweaty palms, a bit shorter." Of all the new disciples in the hall, only seven or eight could pinch a fireball by now. He had those seven or eight hold one each, lit an incense stick, and walked up and down the aisle with his hands behind his back. When the stick was half burned, three had gone out, *pft, pft*. When it was mostly burned, one sneezed, his hand opened, and a fireball rolled onto his own trouser leg, and he hopped about slapping at it for ages. When the last ash fell, only two fists were still glowing red: one in the front row, its owner's face purple with the strain; and in the back row, one more.

"Holding a sign has three rules." Master Chu held up three fingers. "One: one hand holds one thing; two hands, two things at most. Two: once you've let one go, to pinch it again you swing from the beginning, so a fight on the stage comes down to who runs out first. Three—" he put the holding fist behind his back— "don't let anyone see what you're holding. Red through the fist is fire, white is metal, green is wood, a touch of ink-black is water, yellow is earth. Your opponent takes one look between your fingers and knows what to have ready for you." He brought the fist out again; this time the five fingers were pressed together dead tight, and not a glimmer leaked. "Grip it close. Grip it close, and it leaks slower too."

In the back row Xiaoman looked at his two hands and did a sum in his head: people have two hands. What other people held in two hands was the one or two things in their own spirit roots, and an opponent could guess them on his fingers. Xiaoman's five doors were all open: his two hands could be any two of the five.

In the days after, the spells were taught one by one. "Five of them, five tempers," said Master Chu. The fireball went far, still hot three yards off, but it went straight, and a turn of the head dodged it. The ice spike was fastest, there the moment it left the hand, but it was thin; miss a vital spot and it was as good as no hit. The metal blade ran along the ground and went for the ankles; it could shear a corner off a bluestone flag. The wood vine didn't hurt you, it tied you up, and once you were tied, the others got their turn. The earth wall didn't hit, it blocked, and it crowded you too: the stage was only so big, and wherever a wall stood, there was that much less room. "Fire is a spear, ice a needle, metal a sickle, wood a rope, earth a wall," he said. "Most people use one of them well in a lifetime. Use one well and that's enough. The purer the spirit root, the stronger that one is; a heavenly spirit root has only one door, and that one door outweighs another man's five. But the other four, you have to know: if you don't use them, the person facing you will."

"How do you know these four?" He rapped the desk with the ferule. "There's a line in the *Suwen*{典=suwen-wusheng}, said by Qibo: 'Wood meets metal and is felled; fire meets water and is put out; earth meets wood and is pierced; metal meets fire and is notched; water meets earth and is cut off.' Five lines. Learn them by heart." He took them apart line by line: your opponent puts up an earth wall, you burn it with fire, you blacken a patch and the wall's still a wall; send a wood vine to bore into it, the roots go down into the earth, and the wall cracks by itself. Your opponent sends a wood vine: one metal blade, and it's cut. Your opponent uses a metal blade: meet it with a fireball and the edge curls. Your opponent throws a fireball: one ice spike, and both turn to white steam together. Your opponent's ice spike is coming: put up an earth wall, and it all sticks in the earth. "Each one eats another. Round the circle, nobody's top dog."

Someone asked: "Master, then a heavenly spirit root, with only one door, if it meets the one that eats it, isn't it just giving itself away?" Master Chu said: "A heavenly spirit root comes along once in three hundred years. It's not your turn to worry about him." The whole hall laughed. He waited for the laughter to settle and said: "Still, not a stupid question. The *Mencius*{典=mengzi-beishui} says, 'a cup of water to save a cartload of burning firewood': the fire doesn't go out, and so they say water can't beat fire. That's too little water. The *Mozi*{典=mozi-wuxing} says it more bluntly: 'none of the five phases always wins.' Fire melts metal when there's more fire; metal smothers a charcoal fire when there's more metal. Whether one eats the other depends on how much." He pointed the ferule vaguely at the peak behind the main hall. "Someone with a heavenly spirit root: one root, as thick as all five of yours; a breath drawn in, not a thread leaked, all of it down that one road. You're going to throw a ladle of water at his mountain of fire?" The questioner didn't ask any more.

Someone raised a hand: "Master, the thickest in my spirit root is metal, and the person facing me just happens to use fire. What do I do?" Master Chu said: "Run." The whole hall laughed. He didn't: "Most of you have two or three roots, and you train the thickest one or two. When you meet the one that eats yours, you either have to be faster than him, or deeper than him, or concede. Someone who can use all five—" his eyes swept the back row once and came away— "is thin at all of them, and not much use either."

Xiaoman lowered his head. Thin was true; he was thin. But he went over the five lines in his head, and the second time through, his palms sweated a little. Other people who learned those five lines knew what they had to fear. For him, learning them meant that whatever his opponent brought out, he had in his hand the thing that ate it.

Xiaoman learned all five, and learned all five only skin-deep; one throw and a stretch of his dantian was empty. But his five doors were all open, and he could throw any of them. Master Chu watched him at the back of the lecture hall trying them one by one, stroked his goatee, and said nothing.

---

On the night before the contest, nobody in the outer sect's long bunk could sleep. Ma Xiaobao sat cross-legged on the best spot by the brazier with the gilded food box in front of him, stuffing in osmanthus cakes one after another, telling the four hangers-on round him as he chewed how he'd fight tomorrow, whom he'd fight, how many moves. When the box was finished he patted his belly and said: "I'm hungry." Xiaoman lay in the bunk by the door with the quilt over his head and one hand under the pillow, gripping the short knife with three teeth gone, and remembered how Granny Wu once sat on the doorstep listening to the gongs and drums of the Ma family's birthday banquet and said, out of nowhere: "That Ma lot. They eat, and they're still hungry."

The chewing over there stopped. Xiaoman peeped out from the corner of the quilt and saw Ma Xiaobao take off the copper mirror, hold it to the brazier and rub it with his sleeve, again and again. It was the back he was rubbing. In the firelight a pattern was cast on the back of the mirror: not a dragon, not a bird, but a circle with one line across it.

Under the quilt, Xiaoman all at once forgot how to breathe. He had seen that mark. On the night of the fifth of the first month, under the woodpile at home, that was the mirror the Old Well voice had been holding.

Ma Xiaobao turned the mirror over, its face towards the long bunk, and slowly shone it from one end to the other, bunk by bunk, muttering: "Show the demon, show the demon..." With each bunk the hangers-on laughed. The firelight on the mirror's face swung along towards the door, swung onto Xiaoman's bunk, and the wooden tablet on his chest flared hot.

Xiaoman remembered the top of the woodpile, his father sitting beside him while the torches swept closer an inch at a time, and what his father had said low: "What it hears isn't your breathing. It's your heart panicking." He didn't turn over, didn't shrink into the quilt. He only let that breath out, long and slow, all the way to the bottom, then slowly drew it back. The tablet went cool, cold as a piece of ice. The firelight on the mirror's face wavered once and swung past.

Over at the far end Ma Xiaobao hung the mirror back at his waist and patted it: "My pa says this mirror was left by the Immortal in White. Show up something with it and there's a reward." He was grinning, very pleased. "Tomorrow, it'll show something."

---

That night, when the snores in the long bunk had joined into one, Xiaoman got up in the dark, tucked the little Zheng into his robe, and slipped into the woodshed.

The Rice Bucket was under the woodpile, its lid shut tight. Ever since the radish it had been sulking; nine nights in ten it wouldn't open, and the odd time it did, it spat out a pinch of ash and the lid snapped shut, *pak*, and it ignored him again. Xiaoman crouched in front of it and wiped the green rust off with his sleeve until it shone; then he went through the titles the storyteller in town used, "Heaven-Quelling Divine Furnace," "Nine-Turn Golden Elixir Furnace," louder and louder, and the little beast on the lid turned its backside to him. He sighed: "Rice Bucket, oh, Rice Bucket. Tomorrow someone's going to shine a mirror on me." He pressed once; it didn't open. Twice, three times; it didn't open. At the fourth press, *click*, the lid loosened a crack.

It had opened, but he had hardly anything on him worth feeding it. The pouchful from the secret realm had long since gone into its belly; of spirit stones there was only the one drawn on the first of the twelfth month, gripped in his palm, and he couldn't bear to throw that in first. Feed it ordinary things and mostly ordinary things came out. He fed it ordinary things first.

The first was a pinch of red fur the little Zheng had shed. *Click*: out floated a sheet of yellow paper with a nose drawn in cinnabar, nostrils to the sky, and two lines of red writing at the talisman's foot: *Sneezing Talisman; a breath of clear air, clears the passages.*

The second was one of the little Zheng's whiskers. When he pulled it, it butted him with its single horn. Out came three needles, fine as ox hairs, stuck in a little slip of red paper: *Tickle Needles; one prick, one laugh.*

The third was the cracked hand mirror the kitchen woman had thrown out by the slops bucket. Out came a copper mirror the size of a palm, with a corner missing, polished bright, and whoever looked in it saw Xiaoman's face. He tilted it at the candle, and the candlelight bounced back, *pak*, right onto the wooden tablet on his chest. Yinyue came out and squinted into it: "...Where's this queen's face?" Xiaoman turned the mirror over; on the back was cut: *Selfie Mirror; before you show others, show yourself.* He read it once: "...Selfie?"

The fourth was the pair of straw sandals on his feet, worn through at the soles. The Rice Bucket went "*hic*" and spat out nothing.

Last was the spirit stone. He pressed both hands to the copper belly, and in his heart what he said was: give me something that can beat the inner sect. *Click*: a sheet of yellow paper, with a fat pig drawn in cinnabar, two pairs of dragonfly wings stuck on its back, and two lines of red writing under its belly: *Pigcopter; rises on the wind, roosts where it finds wood.* What he wanted was something that could beat the inner sect; what came out was this.

After five things, the Rice Bucket had drawn five breaths from him; his dantian was bare to the bottom, and he couldn't get up without a hand on the woodpile. Yinyue crouched on the chopping block, looked at the row of things on the ground, and said lazily: "...Rag-and-bone man."

Still tucked under the woodpile was the little clay jar, the one that had come out in the tenth month, with crooked writing on it: *Beef-Tallow Nectar; numbing, hot, savoury, fragrant.* Yinyue had been on about hot pot for two months, and the pot had never once been set up. He put the jar in the storage pouch too and felt his way back to the long bunk in his two holey socks. It was less than four hours to dawn.

---

On the eighth of the twelfth month, before it was light, Xiaoman came out of the long bunk shuffling in a pair of borrowed worn-out shoes, his legs still weak. Freezing rain had fallen in the night, and the open ground outside the kitchen door had frozen into a mirror; the people queuing for porridge walked along the wall one by one, holding on. He held on to the wall too, swaying at every step.

Shopkeeper Qian, wrapped in a cotton quilt, was minding the betting board under the old pagoda tree, taking early bets. He narrowed his eyes at the one swaying along the wall, looked at his legs, looked at his face, white as paper, and rubbed out the "a hundred to one" in the bottom line with his charcoal and wrote "a hundred and fifty to one." Someone asked: "Why's it gone up again?" Shopkeeper Qian clicked his abacus once: "Can't even stand up straight. That's another tenth off."

The Laba porridge had been on in the kitchen for hours. The rice was old rice, the beans were mixed beans, and of the jujubes you broke open, one in two was half empty; but a whole great pot of it, steaming hot, smelled so good half the mountain could smell it. Xiaoman stood at the end of the queue, his feet still slipping, holding the one bowl without a chip that the kitchen woman kept for him. Zhou Heng was queuing too, the ledger in his hand as always. He looked back at the pot of porridge and said: "Year's end. The accounts have to close." He patted the ledger and went off with his porridge.

Ahe squeezed in beside him with her bowl. Xiaoman set a purple-whisker lingzhi by the edge of her bowl; in the clod of black earth round its roots were twined a few scorched grains, just like the three she kept wrapped in cloth. Ahe glanced at it, asked nothing, put it away in her sleeve, and slurped her porridge. Xiaoman said: "Are you going up on the stage?" Ahe said: "No. Personal disciples don't enter the contest. Master's rule." Xiaoman said: "That Foundation Pill—" Ahe said: "It isn't for me. Master says, mine, he'll give me himself when the time comes." She said it quite flatly, as if saying the porridge was thin today. Xiaoman said "Oh" and bent to his porridge; there was half an empty jujube in his bowl, and he chewed it for a long time. Ahe didn't look at him either. She said: "Yours, you'll have to go up on the stage and grab for yourself."

She took her little notebook out of her robe, tore out a page, folded it to the size of a fingernail and tucked it under his bowl: "I'm not going on the stage. My eyes have nothing to do." This month she had gone with the Abbot into the inner sect to sit at morning lessons a few times; the records of every year's contest in the scripture library (who used what, how they won, how they lost: the stewards wrote it all down) were hers to leaf through too. On that page there was one name to a line, the writing as small as ants in a queue, ten or so of them, each with how they won and how they lost. He read Lu Fang's line twice, and Qin Yan's line twice too. When he reached the last line, Qi Changsong, there were only four words, and he raised his head. Ahe was slurping porridge, her face blank: "Burn it when you've read it. If anyone sees a personal disciple copying out what's what for the outer sect, Master's face has nowhere to go, and mine has nowhere to go either." Her voice was pitched lower than the porridge. Xiaoman held the paper to the mouth of the stove and watched it curl up and go black. The words on the paper he had already taken in, one by one.

The contest stage was set up on the outer sect's training ground: five bluestone slabs fitted into a circle, grass growing in the cracks. It had a big name, the Five Phases Stage, though there were no five phases on it, only five stones. At the four corners of the training ground stood four old cypresses. The sect's Grand Contest was fought by inner and outer sect together, and always finished in a single day: twenty-odd inner-sect Qi Condensation disciples, a hundred and thirty-odd from the outer sect, the two registers put together for the drawing of lots. Most of the outer sect took one look at the inner sect's twenty-odd names and drew a circle round their own, withdrawing. In the end thirty-two were left, to fight in pairs, out after one loss. On the first page of the register three rules were written: whoever falls off the stage loses; whoever concedes loses; whoever the steward judges can't fight on loses. Each bout got half an incense stick; if it was still undecided when the stick burned down, the steward judged who had the upper hand. Below that was one more rule, written in the biggest letters: no taking life on the stage; kill someone and even if you win, it counts as a loss. Those who reached the last four went into the inner sect's register; the first place got that one Foundation Pill.

At the hour of the dragon the Abbot arrived. He came down step by step from the direction of the main hall, and of the two hundred-odd people on the training ground, talking, stamping their feet to keep warm, all at once there wasn't a sound; even the smoke of the incense stick at the stage's corner stood straight up without a waver. He sat down in a bamboo chair on the north side of the stage, his hands tucked in, mild of face, as if he'd come to watch a village opera.

For the drawing of lots, Zhou Heng sat beside the bamboo tube holding the contest register. The register was an old ledger with a blue cloth cover, its corners thumbed to rags. The first few dozen pages recorded the contests of past years, one year to a page, and the top line of each page was that year's champion: the last thirty years, line after line, all inner-sect names. When Xiaoman reached Zhou Heng he saw the register lying open near the front, the ink going from new to old, and old in the end to yellow, like autumn leaves. On one page, the name in the first-place line had a stroke drawn through it with a brush, very straight, and the ink of the stroke was newer than the ink of the name; the name under it had been blurred by a water stain, and you couldn't read what it said.

Xiaoman asked: "Senior Brother Zhou, why's this one crossed out?" Zhou Heng didn't look up: "The crossed-out ones are the ones who aren't in the abbey any more." Xiaoman said: "Dead?" Zhou Heng said: "Not in the abbey any more. The books only record whether someone's here, not why. Why isn't my business." With that he held out his hand to him. "Draw." Xiaoman put his hand into the bamboo tube. The steward read out: "Shen Xiaoman, against He Qi of the outer sect." When it was Sun Ergou's turn, the steward read out: "Sun Ergou, against Lu Fang of the inner sect." Somebody below laughed out loud.

Xiaoman's first bout was the quickest and the ugliest. He Qi was an older disciple three years in, third layer. He came up on the stage, cupped his fist at him in salute, and threw a punch straight at his chest. Last night the Rice Bucket had drawn four breaths from Xiaoman, and there was only the bottom left in his dantian, and his legs were still weak. He couldn't take it, and could only step aside. In the instant of stepping aside, he held the Sneezing Talisman between two fingers and sent a thread of spirit qi into it. The cinnabar nose on the talisman wrinkled; the spell sealed in the paper woke, and with a *tss* a wisp of black smoke came out, thin as a thread, and went straight into He Qi's nostrils and mouth. He Qi's punch hit nothing. He turned round and his face screwed up: "Ah—" It screwed up again: "Ah—*choo*!" One sneeze threw him back, the second pitched him forward, and halfway through the third Xiaoman gave him a light push in the back. Still going "*Choo! Choo!*", He Qi took himself off the stage, and was still sneezing when he hit the ground, sneezing till the tears and snot came together. The yellow paper between Xiaoman's fingers had already turned to a pinch of ash and fallen on the frozen earth. Few below had seen it clearly; even the steward was taken aback a moment before he said: "Shen Xiaoman wins." In the crowd, Sun Ergou craned his neck and told the person next to him in all seriousness: "Senior Brother Shen's move there. It's called Sneezing."

Under the old pagoda tree, Shopkeeper Qian rubbed out "a hundred and fifty to one" and wrote "a hundred to one." Sun Ergou squeezed over, red in the face: "Shopkeeper, the copper I bet at daybreak was at a hundred and fifty!" Shopkeeper Qian didn't look up: "What's been bet goes at what it was bet at."

When Sun Ergou went up on the stage, his legs were shaking. He gripped the blue cloth bundle at his waist, its four corners sewn neat and square, the stitches so close you couldn't get a hair between them. Xiaoman's mother was good with a needle too, and he could judge it at a glance: a piece that size was three evenings' work under an oil lamp, one corner an evening, and the fourth corner sewn through till dawn. Sun Ergou lifted his head and grinned at Xiaoman below: meaning, I might win one.

Facing him was Lu Fang of the inner sect, sixth layer, a little over thirty, with a long face and eyelids that always drooped. The older disciples said he had come up from the outer sect, slogged nine years, and the year he finally slogged his way into the inner sect, half his hair had gone white; and since then, the one thing he couldn't stand was outer-sect people climbing onto the stage. Once on the stage he couldn't be bothered even to take a stance, used no spells, just put his hands behind his back and said: "Come on, then." Sun Ergou charged with his eyes shut. Lu Fang turned sideways, hooked a foot out, and Sun Ergou went sprawling on the bluestone and split his chin.

He got up and charged again, and sprawled again. Lu Fang was in no hurry to push him off the stage. He made a point of not pushing him off: he knocked him down, waited for him to get up, knocked him down again. The steward sat at the stage's corner with his eyes only on the incense stick: the stick hadn't burned down, the boy hadn't left the stage, hadn't conceded, and by the rules that wasn't a stop. At first the crowd laughed, shouting "Ergou, give up" and "Your ma's charm's not working!" By the fifth time the laughter had thinned; the sixth time, Lu Fang waited till he was halfway up and set a foot on the back of his hand and ground it, and someone below sucked in a breath, *sss*. Ma Xiaobao clapped below the stage: "Good! Nicely stamped! Serves him right for being friends with Shen Xiaoman!"

Xiaoman stood below the stage and didn't say a word. His hands were clenched in his sleeves, clenched until the nails dug into the flesh. The ring of light behind his five doors leapt up at that stamp, leapt to his throat, so hot it stung his eyes. He had only to take one step onto the stage. One step, and that man would turn three somersaults in mid-air. But anyone from below who stepped onto the stage and spoiled a bout, right or wrong, had his name struck from the register. Take that step, and Sun Ergou's six falls were for nothing, and the pill he could forget about. The steward at the stage's corner saw him shift half a step forward and nailed him with his eyes.

He didn't take the step. The seventh time, Sun Ergou got up with his hands on his knees, his face covered in blood, the right hand that had been ground hanging by his side, the five fingers so swollen they wouldn't close. One string of the blue cloth bundle at his waist had snapped, and it dangled swinging at his hip. He scooped it up with his left hand, tucked it back into his belt, and went towards Lu Fang again. Not charging: walking, one step at a time, his legs shaking like a sieve, every step put down solid. The half incense stick burned to its end and the steward struck the copper gong: "Stop. Sun Ergou, can you still fight?" Sun Ergou spat out a froth of blood: "...I can still fight." The steward said: "I don't think you can." Sun Ergou thought about it and nodded: "...Then I can't." He bowed to the steward, bowed to Lu Fang too, and walked off the stage by himself.

He didn't let anyone help him. He walked all the way to the few stone steps at the edge of the training ground and sat down hard, and once down he couldn't get up again. Xiaoman went over, stood in front of him a while, and held out his hand: "Get up. The ground's cold." Sun Ergou took his hand, and he heaved and pulled him up, then took out a Spring-Return Pill and pushed it into his mouth. Sun Ergou was half a head taller than him and nothing but bones; pulling him up, he was light, like pulling up a bamboo pole. On the ninth of the ninth month, on the hundred-and-fortieth of the stone steps, a boy from Yan Province had sat unable to get up, and Xiaoman had walked past him and not stopped. He had thought then: when I'm standing steady enough, I'll pull. Today he wasn't standing all that steady either, but at least he'd pulled one up.

Sun Ergou wiped his face with his good hand, smearing tears and blood together, looked down at the snapped string at his waist, looked at it a long time, and didn't say a word.

That morning three rounds were fought on the Five Phases Stage. At the head of the inner sect was Qi Changsong, ninth layer. In the secret realm he had still been eighth, and had guarded the stone step: while the senior brothers of the ninth layer went down to choose spirit herbs, he held that step alone, three days and three nights, and didn't let a single person past. Those three days had pressed him solid; less than a month after coming out, he had broken through to the ninth. The secret realm opened once in ten years; he couldn't wait for the next. This pill was the first thing within his reach in twenty years of standing in the inner sect. In the inner sect they called him "Qi Who Doesn't Step Back." His spirit root was earth; when he pushed out a palm, what weighed on that palm was the whole stretch of ground under his feet. He didn't chase, didn't circle, didn't jump; each move went one step forward, step by step, pressing his opponent to the edge of the stage and pressing him off, or pressing him till he conceded. In twenty years, nobody had made him take one step back. Xiaoman watched two of his bouts from below, and what he watched wasn't his face but his feet: in two bouts those two feet had only gone forward and never moved an inch back.

Second in the inner sect was Qin Yan, eighth layer. She had a true spirit root, used the sword, six years in the sect; in the last two contests she had made the last four both times, and both times lost to Qi Changsong. The senior sister in pale blue who reached her scabbard into the pit in the secret realm was her. When outer-sect people talked about the inner sect, mostly they had nothing good to say; but about Senior Sister Qin, they'd all grant one thing: "Senior Sister Qin never stamps on anyone."

In the second round, Xiaoman faced Lu Fang.

When Lu Fang came up on the stage his eyelids still drooped. He looked at Xiaoman, then at Sun Ergou sitting on the steps below, and said: "Outer sect. Each one knows less than the last how high the sky is." He didn't put his hands behind his back. This time, he struck first.

His right hand swept up, and a streak of white light sheared out of his palm: a metal blade, skimming the bluestone towards Xiaoman's ankles. Xiaoman jumped aside; the metal blade sheared into the stage's edge and took off a corner of stone. Lu Fang drew a breath. It was exactly as Ahe's paper said, word for word: after a metal blade, he needs a breath. Only then did his left hand press down, and in front of Xiaoman an earth wall heaved up with a *whump*, chest high, crowding him towards the edge. In the space of that breath, Xiaoman's two hands had long since been holding signs; he hadn't let go since he came up. His right hand put a fireball into the earth wall, and the wall only blackened in one patch; his left hand flicked straight after, and a wood vine bored out from the foot of the earth wall, and the wall cracked with a *crack*. The fire was thrown for Lu Fang to look at; the wood was what ate earth. Someone below said "Huh?": "When Senior Brother Lu changes from one to another, he needs a breath. He... he threw two in one breath?" Lu Fang's eyelids, for the first time, came up.

He came through the crack with a palm strike. A sixth layer's palm, more than twice as fast as He Qi's fist. Xiaoman watched the feet and could see where he was going, but his body couldn't keep up: the first palm grazed his shoulder, the second he dodged, the third landed on his left ribs, *crack*, and the bone broken in the secret realm split again. He flew sideways, rolled three times, rolled to the stage's edge, half his body already hanging over, both hands clawed onto the edge of the bluestone, all ten fingers dug in for dear life; below was the frozen earth of the training ground. Cries of alarm below, and then someone laughing: "That's it, that's it, off you go!"

Lu Fang walked over and looked down at the two hands clawed onto the stone edge. He raised his right foot, set it on the back of Xiaoman's left hand, and ground it. Exactly as he had stamped on Sun Ergou just now.

That grinding turned everything black in front of Xiaoman. The pain wasn't the worst of it. Hanging there, looking at the foot on the back of his own hand, what rose in him was fear. Not fear of pain: fear of going off. If he went off, Sun Ergou's seven falls were for nothing.

His right hand let go of the stone edge and scrabbled in the storage pouch at his waist. His left hand, nailed to the stone by that foot, had become the only thing on him still hanging on. Whatever he found, he found; he had no time to look. He pulled it out in one grab, smashed it on the stone edge, and his right hand clawed back on.

With a *smack* a little clay jar broke on the bluestone, red oil splashing out over a patch of it, Sichuan peppercorns rolling everywhere. Beef-Tallow Nectar. The front row below all sniffed at once.

Lu Fang glanced down and sneered: "What's this?" His weight was all on his left heel, and his right foot came up off the back of Xiaoman's hand, higher than the first time: he was going to stamp. Xiaoman didn't watch the raised foot. He watched where it would come down: his own left hand, and the slick of red oil beside his left hand. His father had said by the mountain stream on Black Pine Ridge: every deer under heaven moves its feet first. The wind came pouring through the pass from the north and struck Lu Fang's back, billowing his robe forwards. First read the wind, then the deer.

Lu Fang's right foot stamped down. Xiaoman whipped his left hand aside.

The stamp missed the hand and landed in the beef tallow. A sixth layer's stamp, full force, couldn't be checked; the sole went skidding on the oil, *tsss*, over the edge of the stage, and only his left heel was still on the stone. The left hand Xiaoman had snatched back wouldn't obey him with its five fingers, so he hooked that ankle with his wrist, the wind at Lu Fang's back gave one more push, and Lu Fang went right over Xiaoman's head and landed face down, *thud*, on the frozen ground below the stage.

Xiaoman hung by one hand from the stone edge, swung twice, climbed up by himself, and knelt at the stage's edge, gulping for breath. The whole stage smelled of Sichuan pepper.

The training ground went quiet. Quiet for a full breath before a voice said: "...Outer sect?" Another voice: "Fourth layer? Threw an inner-sect sixth layer... off?" The crowd went up with a roar. On the steps, Sun Ergou shot to his feet, forgot about his own hand, raised the swollen, cloth-wrapped hand to the sky and yelled at the top of his lungs: "Senior Brother Shen—!" His voice cracked.

The steward opened his mouth twice before he said: "...Shen Xiaoman wins."

Below the stage, Lu Fang lay on the frozen ground and didn't get up for a long time. He pushed himself half up and looked down at his own right foot (the foot that had slogged nine years to get into the inner sect, a layer of red oil on its sole), looked at it a while, and drove a fist into the frozen ground. Xiaoman knelt at the stage's edge watching him. He'd thought he would feel good. When that foot slid off, he had felt good; now he only felt his ribs twinge and twinge, a blue-purple shoe print on the back of his left hand, and a piece of his heart gone empty, like a vat with the water all poured out and only the bottom left. He got up, walked off the stage, and didn't look back.

He went to the steps, sat down beside Sun Ergou, turned his back, and took the little porcelain bottle out of the storage pouch at his waist. Three Spring-Return Pills: one had just gone into Sun Ergou's belly. He tipped out the second and swallowed it. Warmth crept up from his belly. The split bone first went hot, then numb; the shoe print on the back of his left hand faded from blue-purple to yellow. A pill of this one ring could knit a bone, but not knit it whole. He pressed his side: it still hurt, but it hurt so he could breathe now. He clenched his five fingers: they closed.

Under the old pagoda tree, Shopkeeper Qian rubbed out "a hundred" and wrote "twenty." Someone stamped his foot: "I bet at a hundred last bout!" Shopkeeper Qian clicked his abacus: "What's been bet goes at what it was bet at."

In the third round, Ma Xiaobao faced Lu the Loach. Before going up, Lu the Loach squeezed past Xiaoman and said under his breath: "Yesterday I went down the mountain to see my pa on his boat. That mirror came up from Xiapi on my pa's boat; Master Ma San hired my pa's boat. It sat in my pa's cabin the whole way. Under a red cloth, in the middle of the night, it lit up by itself once. Cold, like the light at the bottom of the Si. My pa says it's not for showing demons. It's for recognising things." Having said it, he rubbed his hands and went up. Lu the Loach was a fisherman's son, a good swimmer, slippery-bodied, and with his first punch he'd ducked in under Ma Xiaobao's armpit. Only then did Ma Xiaobao take off the copper mirror.

Xiaoman stood below the stage and saw it plainly: that mirror didn't light the moment it was raised. Ma Xiaobao held it in both hands, and the blotches on his face flushed red all at once and swelled up patch by patch, as if something were being drawn out of his body, drawn out and poured into the mirror. Only then did the mirror's face light, and a beam of yellow light struck Lu the Loach in the face. Lu the Loach cried out and backed away with his hands over his eyes, yelling: "It's cold! The light's cold, it goes into your heart!" Each step he backed, Ma Xiaobao shone it again, and the blotches flushed again, and he panted till both his shoulders heaved. At the third shining, Lu the Loach put a foot on nothing and fell off the stage. Ma Xiaobao stood there holding the mirror, panting a good while before he got his breath even. He lifted his chin at Xiaoman below.

Xiaoman's opponent was Qin Yan.

By the water vat at the stage's corner, the two of them met. Qin Yan scooped half a ladle of water, didn't drink it, and handed it to him. Xiaoman took it and drank, and said: "Senior Sister Qin. That time in the secret realm..." Qin Yan said: "You didn't hang on it. I remember." She scooped another ladle and drank it herself, watching the incense stick on the stage. "Do you know why I have to have first place?" Xiaoman shook his head. Qin Yan said: "The first-place Foundation Pill. I've been stuck at eighth layer two years. The year before last a bear bit my pa's leg, and he's been lying in bed ever since; ordinary medicine can't mend it. At the market there's medicine that can: a Spring-Return Pill of three rings, two hundred spirit stones each, and my monthly allowance is ten. Once I've established my foundation, none of that counts. When a martial uncle of Foundation Establishment asks, the pill house will give credit; when a Qi Condensation disciple asks, nobody listens." She paused. "Get your realm up, and everything else is easy. Don't, and you can't do anything." She set the ladle back on the vat's rim and looked at him. "I won't give way to you. Don't you give way to me either."

Xiaoman held the ladle and for a long while couldn't say anything. He put the ladle down. Qin Yan nodded and went up on the stage first.

Xiaoman followed her up. [Qin Yan]{注=neimen-shijie} was half a head taller than him, her two eyebrows long and straight, like two swords out of their scabbards, never once pencilled; not a trace of powder on her face, clean as clean, with the brisk spirit of a mountain girl about her. On ordinary days she didn't smile much, and her face was cool. The moment she came on the stage, half of the inner sect below went quiet first. But what Xiaoman looked at first was her feet: she stood truly steady, as if her soles were nailed down. Qin Yan said: "Junior Brother Shen, I hear you're only fourth layer." Xiaoman said: "Yes, only fourth layer." Qin Yan said: "I told you. I won't give way." Xiaoman said: "If I can't win, I'll run." Qin Yan was taken aback, then laughed, a little pointed eyetooth showing at the corner of her mouth that didn't match that cool face at all, and was prettier than when she didn't smile.

This bout was nothing like the one before. Qin Yan's sword was a real sword and her moves were real moves. It was a magic tool of the lower grade, and in her hand, when the point went out, a streak of green qi on the blade went on another three feet; you could be ten feet away and the sword qi was already at you. Thrust after thrust came in, quick and steady, and below the stage people held their breath. For the first ten moves she used only half her strength: a fourth layer had thrown Lu Fang off the stage, and she wanted to see how. Xiaoman didn't watch her sword. He watched her feet: wherever the sword went, the feet moved there first; wherever the feet moved, the shoulder sank there first. But her feet were far cleaner than Lu Fang's, moving small and fast; he could see them, but his body was always half a step slow. At the seventh move the point slit his sleeve; at the fifteenth it slit his shoulder; at the twentieth a cut ran across his right cheek, hot, trickling down into his collar. His two hands were holding signs, so he couldn't block, only dodge, jumping this way, rolling that, dodging very uglily; twice it was little short of scrambling on all fours.

But he wasn't dodging at random. A jump this way, a roll that, and in twenty moves he had worked seven steps towards the north-west corner of the stage. On the stage's edge at that corner was a slick of red oil, Sichuan peppercorns still stuck in the cracks.

At the twenty-second move, Qin Yan freed two fingers of the left hand that held her scabbard and pinched, and three ice spikes shot at him together. Xiaoman slapped a palm onto the bluestone and a patch of earth wall heaved up in front of him; the ice spikes went *thup thup thup* into the earth. In his other hand a ball of fire had already come up, and he threw it at her feet; in the instant she jumped clear, he won back half a step. Someone below cried out: "Two! Two again!" Qin Yan landed and gave him one look, and in that look was surprise.

One thrust came too fast for him to watch the feet, and he could only lean back; the point passed along his nose and sheared off a lock of hair from his forehead. The lock of hair floated and floated in mid-air, floated down off the stage, and landed right on Ahe's little notebook. Ahe glanced down, tucked it into the notebook, her face blank. On the hand that held the pen, the thumbnail had been digging into the palm of her other hand all along.

But dodging and dodging, Xiaoman felt something he had never had since he could remember: he was smiling. Time after time the point went past under his nose, past his ear; he was cut all over, the bone in his side hurt with every breath, but the corners of his mouth turned up. This was the first time someone stronger than him stood facing him, every move real, nothing hidden, nothing given way, wanting nothing from him. He turned with that sword like following his father through the woods after the finest deer, following till his chest burned. At first the people below held their breath; later someone shouted "Good!", and later still it came in shout after shout.

At the thirtieth move he was forced at last to the stage's edge. The stretch of edge he had chosen himself. Under his heels there was half an inch of bluestone left; further back was frozen earth; a foot from his left foot was that slick of oil. The point of Qin Yan's sword rested against his throat, cool. Below, everything went quiet.

Up the right sleeve of Qin Yan's robe, all this time, she had been holding a yellow paper thunder talisman; in the cinnabar lines of it a thin thread of blue light wandered to and fro. She had held it from the first move to now and never brought it out. Xiaoman was only a sword's length from her and could see its corner at her cuff. She looked down off the stage: crouched in the front row were a few small children just come into the sect, watching her open-mouthed, squeezed in under the stage's edge, less than an arm's length from those bluestone slabs. She didn't bring that talisman out. Xiaoman's right hand hung in his sleeve, and his fingers touched the three Tickle Needles, touched them once, and let them go.

She didn't need to bring it out. With the point against his throat, she had only to push one inch forward, and he could only step back; and one step back, he was off. That one inch was what Ahe's paper said: in two contests, four bouts won, every one of them like this. He had known at the stove that morning this inch was coming; what to trade for it, he had decided himself.

Xiaoman didn't step back. In the instant the point pushed forward, he went forward to meet it: body turned sideways to let it past his throat, he drove in along the flat of the blade. The green qi on the sword ploughed along the outside of his left arm, from the crook of the elbow right up to the shoulder, ploughed open a gash, and where it was deepest the white showed; the pain turned everything in front of him white. Half an inch further over and that arm would have stayed on the stage. But he was already right in front of Qin Yan. Three feet of sword qi, inside a foot, has no room to work. With his bleeding left hand, he seized the length of scabbard in her left hand.

Qin Yan started. The sword was out there and couldn't come back. She let go of the scabbard and turned her left palm up, and a layer of green light lit in it. An eighth layer's palm, pressed home flat against a fourth layer's chest, and the boy would never cultivate again. The contest forbade taking life. The green light flared once, and she held back three tenths.

That holding back was what Xiaoman had been waiting for. When someone holds back their strength, their body settles back onto the rear foot; and beside her rear foot was that slick of red oil. His foot hooked out (the move a hunter uses to hook a hare's leg into a snare), and what it hooked wasn't the foot she stood solid on but the other one, drawing her back half a step. The palm did land in the end, with only three tenths left, pressed onto his chest, and struck something hard there that was hot. He sat down backwards on the stage, and for a long moment couldn't get his breath.

Qin Yan's heel came down on the oil. An eighth layer could stand on ice; but in this step she was just holding back her strength, her body loose, and her sole went skidding, *tsss*, off the edge of the stage. In mid-air she twisted at the waist and touched her toe to the edge of the stage. What it touched was oil again. It didn't hold.

She landed on the frozen ground below the stage, landed standing, not one stagger, sword still in hand. Xiaoman sat at the stage's edge with her length of scabbard in his left hand, blood running down it. Of the incense stick there was only a speck of red left.

The steward swallowed and struck the gong: "...Qin Yan is off the stage. Shen Xiaoman wins."

First a hush below, then a great roar, louder than the one when Lu Fang went off, wave on wave rolling from one end of the training ground to the other.

Qin Yan stood below the stage, looked down at her own hands, then looked up at Xiaoman for a good while. Xiaoman got up and handed the length of scabbard down to her with both hands, his voice low: "Senior Sister." Qin Yan took the scabbard, lifted her foot and looked at the layer of red oil on her sole, then at the slick on the stage's edge, and said: "You led me onto that oil." Xiaoman said: "...Yes, Senior Sister." Qin Yan said: "From which move did you start leading me there?" Xiaoman said: "From the first." Ma Xiaobao · four to one.** The inner-sect seventh layer, three to one. On Shen Xiaoman's line he rubbed out "twenty" and wrote a "five." Somebody pushed up shouting: "Shopkeeper, I bet at twenty at noon!" Shopkeeper Qian didn't lift an eyelid: "What's been bet goes at what it was bet at."

Slowly, Xiaoman worked out a reckoning in his head. Last night, when Ma Xiaobao shone the mirror over the bunks, he hadn't poured spiritual power into it; there was only firelight on the mirror, and even so the tablet had burned like that. Today he poured spiritual power in; shine it on Xiaoman's chest, and it wouldn't be a matter of a little burn. The light couldn't be dodged: the moment the mirror's face lit, the light was there. But the mirror only shone where it pointed, and Ma Xiaobao held it in both hands, in front of him; and for the mirror to light, it first had to drink a mouthful of Ma Xiaobao's spiritual power, and the blotches on his face had to flush first. That morning, shining on Lu the Loach, it had already drunk three mouthfuls. The two layers swallowed in a night were hollow and couldn't stand many more. He thought: then don't watch the mirror. Watch his face. When the blotches flush, move; move where the mirror isn't pointing; make him turn, make him chase, make him drink it a mouthful at a time, till there's only the last mouthful left. And that last mouthful, catch it with a mirror.

Catching it with a mirror meant catching it with the Selfie Mirror the Rice Bucket had spat out last night. Candlelight that hit it, it threw straight back. Ma Xiaobao's mirror drank people; whatever light it spat out, whether this one could throw that back, he didn't know. He only knew that if he didn't catch it, that light would shine into the wooden tablet.

---

The semi-finals were at the hour of the monkey. In the other half, Qi Changsong against the inner-sect seventh layer; in this half, him against Ma Xiaobao.

As Xiaoman was going up to the stage, Ahe squeezed over, caught hold of his left arm, and took off the soaked strip of cloth round it. Qin Yan's sword had ploughed from the crook of the elbow to the shoulder: one gash, the flesh turned back, the white showing where it was deepest. With her back to the crowd, her right hand drawn up in her cuff pinching a sign, she brought both hands together along the gash, and her lips moved: the same line again. *Observe the way of Heaven.* Xiaoman felt the place go cool, then warm, like the first spring rain falling into dry ground. Something was nudging in the gash, itching till he wanted to scratch: the turned-back flesh curling inwards, reaching across thread by thread, like two rows of seedlings growing towards each other over a ditch, meeting in the middle and twining together. The blood stopped seeping. Under her cuff showed a touch of green, like a new bud on a willow tip: each time it lit it faded a little, faded until it couldn't be seen, and she let go. Two hundred-odd pairs of eyes below were all on the stage. A layer of skin had grown over the gash, so thin you could see through it, the kind that breaks at a touch: not fully grown. She put on a clean strip of cloth, wound it round and round very tight, tight enough to make him grimace: "You can make a fist. Don't put strength into it." There wasn't a bit of colour in her face. She pushed half a flatbread at him, hard as a roof tile: "Something in your stomach. Getting beaten on an empty stomach hurts more." He tucked the bread into his robe, against the tablet.

Ma Xiaobao stood on the stage, the copper mirror at his waist catching the sun, dazzlingly bright. He cupped his fists round at the crowd and shouted at the top of his voice: "Everyone! This mirror of mine was left to my father by the Immortal in White himself, come down from the Taiyi Palace on Mount Taihua. It shows up demons, it beats off enemies, and it can show what's in people's hearts. Today, in front of the whole abbey, I'm going to shine it on—" he pointed the mirror at Xiaoman— "a five-phase mixed spirit root, fourth layer, who threw the inner sect's Senior Brother Lu off the stage and knocked Senior Sister Qin off too. Is he a person or a demon?" The crowd below went up in a roar, laughing, shouting, egging on, all churned together.

Ma Xiaobao went on: "Shen Xiaoman. Kneel down and call me Granddad three times, and I won't shine it. I'll let you walk off by yourself." Someone below started up: "Kneel! Kneel!" One shout after another, more and more of them joining, until in the end it even had a beat. And in the middle of all that "Kneel!" a voice suddenly came out of the crowd, shrill and hoarse, cracked like a split gong: "Don't kneel—!" Everyone turned to look. It was Sun Ergou. A plaster stuck on his chin, his right hand wrapped into a white cloth bundle, he stood in the crowd half a head taller than the rest, his face flushed red. For a moment everyone round him was taken aback, then they burst out laughing: "Ergou! You lost! What are you shouting for!" Sun Ergou went red to the roots of his ears, but he didn't sit down and he didn't shrink back. He just stood there, bolt upright.

Xiaoman stood on the stage looking at Ma Xiaobao. He remembered the year Ma Xiaobao stood at his family's door with an osmanthus cake in his hand and told him to bark like a dog and he'd let him have a lick. He didn't say it. Ma Xiaobao came a step closer, his voice pitched so only the two of them on the stage could hear: "On the Deadly Cliff, you heard. You breathing in there behind the wall, I heard too." The fist clenched in his left hand opened and flung out a fireball, big and lopsided, with a burnt smell of pills about it. Xiaoman's hand opened and an ice spike he'd been holding met it, *tsss*, the fireball went out halfway, and the ice spike turned into a puff of white smoke too. Ma Xiaobao set his teeth and tore the mirror off his belt.

He held the mirror up in both hands. Xiaoman didn't watch the mirror. He watched his face. The blotches on it flushed red all at once, and Xiaoman had already moved two steps left, round to Ma Xiaobao's right-hand side. Only then did the mirror's face light, and a beam of yellow light struck the bluestone slab where he'd been standing, and the grass in the cracks went white in a patch. Ma Xiaobao set his teeth and turned right with the mirror. He'd swallowed a night's worth of pills and his body was heavy; he turned slowly. When he came round, the blotches flushed, and Xiaoman moved again, round behind him. Xiaoman landed and spoke: "Tenth of the ninth month, common fund, one." Ma Xiaobao was thrown, turned again, shone again, shone at nothing: "First of the tenth month, one." Turned again, shone again: "First of the eleventh month, two. Reason: beasts have to eat too." Someone below snorted with laughter. Ma Xiaobao shone again through his teeth, and Xiaoman moved again: "First of the twelfth month, two." Item by item, dates and amounts, straight off, not one wrong.

Suddenly someone in the crowd joined in with a shout, shrill and hoarse: "Tenth of the ninth month, me too, one!" It was Sun Ergou. Then a second: "Me, two!" A third: "Me too, two! He said I snore at night!" Below the stage a whole stretch of people collapsed laughing. A hundred and more in the outer sect had been paying month after month into that gilded box; who didn't have an account of it in his head? The lot who'd been shouting "Kneel!" just now, one after another, were now all calling out numbers, and in the end it even had a beat. On the north side of the stage, in the bamboo chair, the Abbot sat with his hands tucked in and didn't move; Zhou Heng, standing behind him, opened the ledger, took up his brush, and wrote a line. Ma Xiaobao's uncle coughed at the stage's corner, coughed again, and nobody paid him any attention. Every time Ma Xiaobao shone the mirror, the blotches on his face swelled again, flushed again; the hands holding the mirror shook worse each time, and each breath came heavier. He stood still and said through his teeth: "Dodging? Is dodging all you can do?" Xiaoman didn't answer.

Panting, Ma Xiaobao glanced down the mountain towards the Si. The man as wide as a door, at the bow of the boat, had stood up and was looking this way. Ma Xiaobao's face went a shade whiter, but the blotches flushed redder than ever. He stopped shining. Holding the mirror, he pressed forward step by step so Xiaoman couldn't get round behind him again. Xiaoman went left, he went left; Xiaoman went right, he went right; step by step he pressed Xiaoman to the stage's edge. Under Xiaoman's heels, again, there was only half an inch. On the left, Ma Xiaobao's mirror; on the right, Ma Xiaobao's mirror; behind, the frozen earth. On Ma Xiaobao's face the blotches lay patch against patch, red going on purple, and he poured the last mouthful left in his body into the mirror.

In that instant the wooden tablet on Xiaoman's chest burned like a red-hot iron. He had dodged five beams, and this one was what he'd been waiting for. He had nowhere to move. He laid his left hand flat on his chest and pressed down on the tablet, and with his right hand took the Selfie Mirror from his sleeve, held it on his palm with its face outwards, and raised it to meet the beam: a piece of copper the size of a palm, one corner missing. Someone below snorted with laughter: "He's got a mirror too?"

The beam of yellow light struck the Selfie Mirror. It didn't break up as it had on the bluestone; instead, like an ice spike, it bored into the mirror, boring as if looking for something: looking for the tablet, for the wisp of silver inside the tablet. The Selfie Mirror didn't know it. All its life this broken bit of copper could do only one thing: whatever light hit it, throw it straight back. With a *hum* the yellow beam turned back on itself, dead straight, right into Ma Xiaobao's face behind the mirror he held.

Ma Xiaobao screamed. The light was cold: on the bluestone, it had turned the grass in the cracks white in a patch; on a face, the blotches all over it sent up white smoke, *tsss, tsss*, like a ladle of ice water thrown onto the bottom of a red-hot pot. His eyes were still open, and that light filled them right up. The mirror in his hands was still drawing out of him, drawing it out and shining it, and the Selfie Mirror threw it straight back, beam after beam, all into his own face; between the two mirrors it drew out into one column of light too bright to open your eyes on. The copper in Xiaoman's palm was too hot to hold. He didn't let go. His five doors all opened at once, opened up, for the first time, in front of people; the round of the five phases in his body turned into a wheel, spinning fast, all of it pushed into that one palm, holding the mirror up. Under the hand pressed to his chest, the wooden tablet glowed once through his clothes, and a thread of silver light leaked out between his fingers, just for an instant, and went out. The mirror in Ma Xiaobao's hands first hummed, then gave a *crack*: a split opened across its face from the middle and crept all the way to the rim, and the light in the mirror went out. Then another *crack*: the Selfie Mirror in Xiaoman's palm split open from the missing corner and broke into seven or eight pieces, tinkling down onto the bluestone.

On the training ground, not a sound.

Whether anyone had seen that thread of silver between his fingers in that instant, Xiaoman didn't know. He only heard his heart, beat after beat, knocking against the wooden tablet on his chest.

Ma Xiaobao stood stupidly on the stage holding up the cracked copper mirror, his two eyes staring straight ahead, a grey film over the whites; nothing would show in it any more. He fumbled the mirror over, and over again, and slapped it hard twice. Then his legs gave and he went down on his knees, both hands over his face, white vapour coming out between his fingers. The blotches on his face had been branded white by the cold light, patch after patch, like frost-bitten aubergine skin. Clutching his face he pitched forward, his face against the bluestone, and didn't move.

Xiaoman stood at the stage's edge and didn't strike again. He crouched and looked at that face against the stone, the branded-white blotches patch against patch, tears running out streak after streak, lips white, gasping like a fish landed on the bank. He said low: "Those were the common fund. There's one more item, not in the common fund. The night of the second of the eleventh month, the Deadly Cliff, Meng Youtian." Ma Xiaobao turned his face away, towards the Si beyond the mountain gate, eyes open, seeing nothing. At the foot of the mountain the Ma family's boat was still moored, and at its bow stood the man as wide as a door, looking this way. He looked a while, then turned and ducked into the cabin.

Ma Xiaobao's face was still turned towards that boat, turned like a child lost at a market fair. Xiaoman crouched there, and swallowed the next thing he'd been about to say.

Xiaoman stood up, then crouched again, picked up the seven or eight pieces of broken copper one by one, and tucked them into his robe.

A very low voice said in his ear: "...You caught it with the mirror." Xiaoman said quietly: "Mm. I caught it." The voice said: "And the mirror's gone too." It was a long while before it said: "...Idiot." Then there was no more sound.

Master Chu stood at the very back of the crowd, one hand stroking his goatee; halfway through a stroke, it stopped in mid-air. A good while later a voice said timidly: "...He... wasn't that... backlash?" That one word opened a door for everyone: "Yes, yes, backlash! The mirror shone on people and ended up shining on his own face!" "Swallowed three bottles of pills in one night, his spiritual power's hollow, couldn't control the mirror, and the light turned back on him!" "Master Chu told us: at best you drop three layers, at worst you're crippled!" "I said those blotches on his face weren't right. Look, they've all been branded white!" The more they said it the more they believed it, and the more they believed it the more they felt they'd seen it coming all along. Master Chu's goatee came down at last. He coughed and said with a stern face: "In the way of pills, greed loses all. Take this as a warning, all of you." With that he turned and went, and as he went he looked back once at Xiaoman, and what he looked at was the hand hanging at his side. Only then did the steward at the stage's corner remember the gong in his hand and strike it once: "Ma Xiaobao can't fight on. Shen Xiaoman wins."

At the time, nobody stopped to think how a broken bit of copper with a corner missing could catch that beam. Besides Master Chu, there were two others who didn't go along with "backlash." One was the Abbot, Xuanchenzi, in the bamboo chair on the north side of the stage, who hadn't moved from start to finish, but now let out a soft "Mm." The other was Elder Qu on the steps of the mountain gate, his back to the training ground, sweeping all the while; at the sound of the mirror cracking, his broom stopped, stopped halfway. Those three hundred steps he had swept for fifty years. That day, he didn't finish them.

As the sun touched the western peaks, the other half finished too. The inner-sect seventh layer had worked a whole palm routine over Qi Changsong three times, worked himself into a sweat, and conceded.

The final: Shen Xiaoman against Qi Changsong.

When Xiaoman went up on the stage his right hand was clenched in a fist, hidden in his sleeve, and the new strip of cloth on his left arm had seeped a little red again. Qi Changsong was already standing across the stage. He was forty or so, square-faced, his eyes half open, half shut. He glanced at Xiaoman's sleeve and said: "That beam of Junior Brother Ma's. You caught it." Xiaoman said: "He shone himself dry." Qi Changsong said: "I won't hurt you. I'll see you off the stage." Having said it, he lowered his eyelids and said no more. The incense stick was lit.

*I'll see you off*: everyone below took it for contempt. Ahe's paper had said: no way to fight him; concede. She had looked at the records; Xiaoman had looked at the feet. He had watched his feet all morning. When Qi Changsong took a step, the bluestone under his foot sank a little; when he pushed out a palm, what he pushed wasn't a hand, it was the whole stretch of ground that step stood on. Only when the front foot was planted solid did the rear foot follow, and the instant the rear foot followed was when he was lightest, shorter than a blink, and in twenty years nobody had caught it.

But knowing was one thing and beating him another. Qi Changsong took his first step. Xiaoman threw spells first: the fireball and the ice spike he held in his two hands, one after the other. He backed away; one step back, one sign pinched, and a metal blade. Over Qi Changsong's body floated a layer of earth-yellow light, thin, like the glaze on a vat; the fireball, the ice spike, the metal blade struck it, *tang, tang, tang*, like pebbles on a vat, and bounced off. Xiaoman pinched another sign and threw a wood vine (wood eats earth); the tip of the vine made a white dot on that light and went limp. Qi Changsong took another step. Xiaoman set his teeth and flung the three Tickle Needles from his sleeve all at once. Three needles met a ninth layer's body-guarding light, *ting, ting, ting*, and went flying all over: one stuck in a crack in the bluestone, one flew off who knew where, and the third took a turn, flew down into the crowd, and stuck right in the cheek of an inner-sect senior brother. That senior brother said "Hm?" and put up a hand to feel; the corner of his mouth twitched, "Hee"; twitched again, "Hee hee"; and then he was bent double clutching his belly: "Hahaha— I don't want to laugh— hahahaha—" laughing till the tears streamed, and the people next to him couldn't hold him up. Qi Changsong took another step.

One step, one palm, not fast, not hurried. Xiaoman could dodge the first palm, but not the third; he could dodge the palm, but not the step: each step that came on took a piece of the stage away. He went round to the left, and Qi Changsong stepped left; he went round to the right, and Qi Changsong stepped right, like a hillside moving forward an inch at a time. Xiaoman went in to push him, and couldn't shift him; backed off three steps and drove in head first, and it was like hitting a wall. He bounced off and fell on the bluestone, and the wound on his left arm burst open and spattered blood over the ground. He got up and was pushed three feet by another palm; got up again, three feet again. Five palms, and there was blood in his nose and his mouth, and under his heels only half an inch of bluestone left. From start to finish, Qi Changsong had not moved back one inch.

At first they laughed below: "Fourth layer, come down, don't hold everyone up from their supper!" Later, nobody was laughing. Someone shouted: "Stop running at him!" Someone else shouted: "Run at him!" And as it went on, more and more were shouting "Run at him!" The incense stick was more than half burned.

Xiaoman stood at the stage's edge panting, took an oiled-paper packet from his robe, and drew out a sheet of yellow paper. Below, people craned their necks: a pig, with two pairs of wings on its back. "A talisman?" "A talisman drawn by a fourth layer?" "And it's a pig?" Qi Changsong's half-shut eyes opened. His spirit sense swept over the paper and found nothing: not a thread of spirit qi in it. Yet this boy held it as if it were treasure, and this boy had just cracked a mirror catching it. What he feared most were things his spirit sense couldn't read. The foot he'd raised went back down. What the paper did, Xiaoman didn't know; he had never once tried it. That night, pressing the copper belly, what he'd said was "give me something that can beat the inner sect," and that line was all he was betting on. He held the paper in his palm and licked the back of it wet.

He held the pig and waited. His father used to say: the moment the sun goes down, the mountain wind turns round. By day it goes up the mountain, by night down it; and at the moment it turns, every deer in the wood lifts its head.

When there was a finger's width of incense left, the sun sank. The banner at the edge of the training ground first drooped, and Xiaoman moved. He didn't circle, didn't dodge; from the stage's edge he walked straight at Qi Changsong, like a child beaten senseless who only knows to keep walking forward, the yellow paper in his hand, held out in front of him.

Qi Changsong's brows knitted for the first time. Twenty years. Twenty years he had walked forward in the inner sect and never stepped back once, waited twenty years for this pill, and with a finger's width of incense left, this blood-covered fourth layer, holding a paper he couldn't read, was walking up to him on his own; and the two hundred-odd below were all shouting "Run at him!" That paper mustn't touch him. His hand moved in his sleeve.

Qi Changsong struck.

Xiaoman saw it. This palm was what he'd been waiting for. Before it landed he was so afraid the soles of his feet went cold; in the instant it landed, his heart went still instead, still as crouching in the snow waiting for a deer.

The palm wasn't fast and it wasn't loud. It simply pushed out and landed flat on Xiaoman's chest. The contest forbade taking life, and this palm was measured: enough to push a fourth layer over, not enough to kill. But in the instant it landed someone below screamed: Xiaoman's whole body folded backwards and a mouthful of blood sprayed out, over Qi Changsong's sleeve.

He didn't fall.

His feet ploughed half a foot backwards across the bluestone, but the ground beneath them seemed to have put down roots: the pinch of earth from the secret realm, grown into a patch of deep ground in his dantian, that could hold water, could take this palm too. He swayed, bent double, and still didn't fall. His two hands groped forward and caught hold of Qi Changsong's robe, like a beaten child who can't stand up grabbing at a grown-up beside him.

As Qi Changsong pushed out that palm, he stepped forward with it, his front foot pressing the bluestone down; the earth qi under his feet had all gone, along with the palm, into his hand. His rear foot was just about to follow. That instant was the lightest he had been in twenty years.

The hand holding his robe slapped against his back. The Pigcopter stuck to Qi Changsong's back.

Just then the banner at the edge of the training ground flipped over with a *flap*. The wind had turned.

Qi Changsong's two feet left the ground.

Below, an "Ah!" went up, two hundred-odd mouths opening at once. Qi Who Doesn't Step Back, who had walked forward in the inner sect for twenty years and never stepped back once, was being lifted by a sheet of yellow paper with a pig drawn on it: an inch, two inches, his toes off the bluestone. He was a ninth layer after all; the moment his body began to float, one hand had already shot back like lightning and grabbed Xiaoman by the back of the collar. If he was going, they were going together.

The two of them floated up together.

It was not a pretty float. Qi Changsong's square face, which hadn't changed colour in twenty years, went green then white, white then green; head forwards, backside behind, one hand gripping Xiaoman, the other reaching over his back for the talisman. Xiaoman, held by the collar, kicked both legs in mid-air like a cat picked up by the scruff. The Pigcopter made a point of flying into trees. It fixed on the old cypress at the east corner of the training ground and flew straight into it, *thunk*, and bounced off; fixed on the one at the west corner, *thunk*, bounced off again. Below, people ducked every which way with their arms over their heads. The inner-sect senior brother who'd taken a needle just now and was still going "hee hee" was too slow; Xiaoman's foot knocked his topknot sideways, and he spun round three times on the spot clutching his head, laughing and yelling at once: "My head! Haha— my head!" Qi Changsong turned a circle in mid-air and at last reached the yellow paper on his back and tore it, *rrip*, in two.

He let go of Xiaoman's collar and dropped, and landed on the edge of the Five Phases Stage: not where he had taken off, but three feet behind it. The toes of one foot on the stone edge, the heel hanging out over nothing, he swayed once, swayed again, his sleeves spread like a crane landing on a branch, and stood his ground at last.

Xiaoman didn't come down. A branch of the old cypress at the west corner caught him under the belly and hooked his belt. He hung folded in two over the branch, head dangling on this side, legs dangling on that, arms hanging and swinging too, like a wet jacket thrown over a washing line, the blood from his nose dripping down drop by drop.

Just then the steward struck the copper gong. The half incense stick was done.

The steward looked at the one at the stage's edge with his heel over nothing, then at the one hanging in the tree, turned the gong stick round twice in his hand, and only then said: "...Qi Changsong wins."

As the words fell, the whole place went wild. Half of it was laughter, "Hahahaha," people rocking back and forth with it, some sitting on the ground pounding their legs, some laughing till they got the hiccups, one hiccup after another; half of it was roaring, and what they roared was "Qi Who Doesn't Step Back stepped back!" "One step back!" "Twenty years, and the first time!" Laughter and roaring churned together, rolled over the Five Phases Stage, rolled past the four old cypresses, rolled until the birds over by the mountain gate flew up.

Xiaoman hung in the tree and watched it all upside down. He'd lost. First his heart went empty: the breath he'd been holding in all day went out of him, went out until he couldn't lift a finger. After hanging a while, with the blood rushing to his forehead, it suddenly struck him how funny this really was. A fourth layer fights all day, and at the end of it he's hanging in a tree like a salted fish his mother had put out to dry. Hanging upside down he snorted out a laugh, and then "Hahaha," and couldn't stop. Every laugh hurt the bone in his side, and he laughed again, and it hurt again, and he laughed until he swung back and forth on the branch, for all the world like a salted fish swinging in the wind.

Qi Changsong stood at the stage's edge in a sweat, Xiaoman's blood on his sleeve. He looked down at his own heel, then raised his eyes to the bluestone slab three feet off where he'd taken off, looked for a long time, and only then lifted his head and looked at the boy hanging in the tree. He said: "Twenty years. The first step back." And then: "That talisman of yours. Where did it come from?" Xiaoman swung in the tree and answered upside down: "...Found it." Qi Changsong looked at him, didn't say he believed it and didn't say he didn't, gave him one nod, and walked off the stage; and as he went he reached out a hand to steady the senior brother with the knocked-over topknot who was still going "hee hee."

The champion: one Foundation Pill. The Abbot put the jade case into Qi Changsong's hands. The jade was bluish-white, and across the lid was a seal strip stamped with the Abbot's great seal. Before the case was even opened, a thread of clean, bitter medicinal fragrance had seeped from the crack, cold, like pine needles on an early morning in the twelfth month, and the front rows breathed it in without meaning to, and when they had, their chests went cool, then warm. Qi Changsong took it in both hands, knelt, and knocked his head to the ground once; when he got up, the rims of his eyes were red. Zhou Heng opened the blue-covered register and turned back a few dozen pages, to the page with the stroke drawn through it, looked at it once, and only then turned forward again to a new page and handed the brush to the steward who called the names.

Xiaoman was poked down out of the tree by the kitchen woman with a washing pole. As she poked, she muttered: "...You child."

He landed and walked off past the stage, and the crowd parted of its own accord to make a path. Wherever he passed, nobody laughed at him; a good many looked at him, looked for a long time. The Abbot rose from the bamboo chair, came up to him, and said mildly: "Fourth layer now. After New Year, move to the inner sect." He paused, and said: "In the Sunken Cauldron secret realm there's a stone chamber. For three hundred years, nobody has been in it..." He didn't finish. He patted Xiaoman's shoulder, turned and left. Xiaoman stood there, and all the sweat on his back broke out at once.

Ma Xiaobao was carried back to the long bunk between two hangers-on, his face smeared with the kitchen woman's badger fat, both eyes bound with cloth, the cracked mirror still gripped in his hand; even when he'd fallen he hadn't let it go. Master Chu lifted his eyelid and looked: "Scorched. Keep them bound three days, and he won't go blind." He was fifth layer, and his name had been added to the inner sect's register too; that same evening an elder named Cui sent someone to take him in as a disciple. But all the way back he didn't say a word, only turned his bound face away, towards the Si. The Ma family's boat had already gone.

Xiaoman looked over towards the mountain gate. Elder Qu stood on the steps, leaning on his broom, watching too. But what Elder Qu was watching wasn't him. It was the jade case in Qi Changsong's hands. It was a long look, too long to be a look at a case; more like a look at something from many years ago. Then Elder Qu turned round and went on sweeping.

At the foot of the steps, Master Chu was waiting for him. Everyone had scattered towards the kitchen, and the old master stood there alone and held out two fingers to him: "Give me your wrist." Xiaoman held out his left wrist. Master Chu didn't take it: "Not that one. The other." Xiaoman stood a moment, then brought his right hand out of his sleeve. Master Chu felt it for a long time, so long that over by the mountain gate Elder Qu's broom started up again. He let go and said: "The pulse says fourth layer, same as the register." He stroked his goatee once, turned and left; three steps off, without looking back, he added one thing: "The Ma boy. That wasn't backlash."

---

When it got dark, Zhou Heng lit the half candle in the stewards' office. When Xiaoman went in, he already had the night-watch book open and was reading down it line by line with his finger: "First to ninth of the tenth month, ten spirit herbs lost from the herb garden. From the eighth, Shen Xiaoman on night watch; after the tenth, not one lost, plus one spirit beast, a Zheng, entered in the register. The item to be settled separately, settled today—" He counted them out of the drawer one at a time. "—one, two, three... ten."

Xiaoman looked at the ten spirit stones and didn't reach for them. Zhou Heng said: "Accounts close at Laba. They close in the evening." He shut the ledger and, for once, said one thing more: "The *Book of Rites* says that in ancient times, at the year's end, the Son of Heaven held the *zha* sacrifice, and even the cats that ate the field mice were invited and thanked. When you've used what's someone else's, you must pay it back. Even the cats' accounts get settled." Xiaoman put the spirit stones in his robe, went to the door, turned round and said: "...Senior Brother Zhou." Zhou Heng said: "Mm. Something else?" Xiaoman said: "You'll go far." Zhou Heng coughed, and the roots of his ears went red all at once; he pushed two abacus beads the wrong way and waved him out.

Shopkeeper Qian was packing up his stall under the old pagoda tree, the abacus not yet back in his sleeve, when he saw Ahe coming and lifted his eyelids. Ahe unfolded the little square of paper and laid it flat on the steelyard pan, her face blank: "Shopkeeper, that copper I bet. It lost." Shopkeeper Qian said: "It lost." Ahe said: "Here's your slip back." Shopkeeper Qian took the slip; the copper was long since in his cloth bag. Ahe stood a moment, took her little notebook out of her robe, opened it, and wrote in it stroke by stroke with charcoal; when she had finished she closed it, turned and left.

Xiaoman stood under the old pagoda tree and first laughed, laughed out loud, laughed till the bone in his side twinged and twinged; and laughing, his eyes went hot, so hot he couldn't see the pagoda tree clearly. He wiped his eyes with the back of his hand, and when he'd wiped them, he was still laughing. That one copper coin her mother had sewn into the hem of her clothes; on the seventh she had picked the thread open with scissors. Ahe went a few steps, looked back, gave him one look, and said: "You didn't hide today." Xiaoman said nothing. His right hand was clenched, and he didn't open it. She said only two words: "Hide well."

That night the woodshed door was bolted, and on the chopping block stood an iron pot with a chipped rim that the kitchen woman had lent him. The jar of Beef-Tallow Nectar had broken on the Five Phases Stage that morning, half of it carried off on the sole of Lu Fang's shoe, and the bluestone at the stage's edge still smelled good now. But what the jar in the tenth month had been traded for, Xiaoman remembered: one beef bone. The kitchen woman had just finished a pot of broth and was tipping out the bones, and he'd begged two (boiled white, not a shred of meat left) and dropped them one at a time into the Rice Bucket and fed it two breaths of spirit qi. The ten spirit stones Zhou Heng had given him were in his robe, and he hadn't touched one. *Crunch. Crunch. Click.*

When the iron pot came to the boil, the smell of Sichuan pepper made the little Zheng sneeze three times running. Sun Ergou, whom Xiaoman had dragged along, held a bowl and looked at the pot and for a long while didn't dare move. Yinyue came out, crouched on the pot's rim, and raised both front paws over her head: "Hot pot tonight—yaaay~~" Only Xiaoman could hear it. She ate very fast. Sun Ergou picked up a slice of cured pork, blew on it three times, looked down, and his chopsticks were empty; the tofu in the pot went down a piece at a time before his eyes. Sun Ergou rubbed his eyes, looked at the pot, looked at Xiaoman, and said quietly: "Senior Brother Shen, this pot... does it leak?" Xiaoman said: "It leaks a bit." Yinyue picked her teeth with the toothpick (the very first thing the Rice Bucket ever spat out), and when she'd picked them she skewered three pieces of tofu on it.

By the end there were only two half-portions of broth left in the pot. Yinyue lay on her back on the pot's rim, her belly round, picking her teeth with the toothpick, and said lazily: "Not a bad broth base. Which shop's it from?" Xiaoman said: "Bones the kitchen woman threw out after boiling broth three times." Yinyue's toothpick stopped in mid-air: "...What this queen ate was the fourth boiling?" Xiaoman ignored her. He looked at the Rice Bucket under the woodpile and went over it in his head: a firewood stick for a toothpick, one leftover stalk for a Spring-Return Pill of nine rings, two spirit stones for two pills, a poison and its cure; one bone for one jar, two bones for two halves. It didn't spit things out at random: what you fed it, how much, of what quality, that was what came out. This account he had nowhere to write. He kept it in his head.

That night the whole abbey talked about one thing: an outer-sect disciple of the fourth layer had thrown Lu Fang off the stage, knocked Qin Yan off, cracked Ma Xiaobao's mirror, and at the end made Qi Who Doesn't Step Back take a step back. "How did he do it?" "Luck." "Luck four times running?" In the long bunk, Ma Xiaobao lay wrapped in his quilt shaking half the night, and said only one thing: "The mirror shone on him... and under his hand, something lit up." Nobody paid him any attention.

When everyone was asleep, Sun Ergou felt his way to Xiaoman's bunk and said quietly: "That stamp of Senior Brother Lu's. You paid it back for me." Xiaoman said: "He slipped by himself." "I was sitting on the steps. I smelled the Sichuan pepper." After a while the dark spoke again: "Senior Brother Shen, write a letter for me tomorrow. Just write: Ma, I fell down seven times and got up seven times." He paused. "Don't write who knocked me down."

---

When Sun Ergou had gone, Xiaoman lay on his front on the bunk and, by the moonlight from the window, wrote his first letter home. The first line he wrote was "Ma, I've become an immortal," and he rubbed it out. The second was "Ma, I beat Ma Xiaobao"; he thought about it, and if that got back to the village Ma San might come to the door, so he rubbed it out too.

Xiaoman wrote on the bunk, stroke by stroke: **Ma: I've got into the immortals' sect. Five taels a year; the abbey says it'll reach home in the twelfth month. Fix the roof. You can put the basins away now, all seventeen. Buy Pa a pair of knee pads, dog-skin ones. Take Granny Wu another leg of venison, and this time a bought one. I'm very well here. When people bully me, I keep an account. A few of the items are paid back already. —Xiaoman**

Xiaoman folded the letter, thought about it, opened it again, and added one line at the very bottom: **Ma, a leaky ladle can carry water too.** He put the letter under his pillow, to give to Zhou Heng first thing in the morning, to go down the mountain in the same cart as the Shen family's packet of five taels. The bow lay by his pillow; he reached out and felt its seventeen layers of ox sinew. He held the wooden tablet on his chest and said quietly: "That flatbread you owe me... still owe it, right?" The tablet went warm, gently, once. As if to say: **Still owe it.**

After a while Yinyue's voice came, very low, not lazy at all: "When the mirror cracked, there was someone at the other end of it." Xiaoman sat bolt upright: "Which end?" Yinyue said: "Far away. West. This queen only heard one sentence." She paused, and mimicked the voice, very flat, flat without a single rise or fall: "Tell the one in Xu to go to Ye in the tenth month and wait."

Xiaoman sat in the dark a long time before he said: "That mirror. What it was looking for was you."

"It was this queen," said Yinyue. For the first time she didn't put on this queen's airs; her voice was flat. "On the fifth of the first month, the one holding the mirror under the woodpile said this queen was dead. That's what he went back and reported. Today the mirror came onto the stage and shone once, and this queen isn't dead. She's on you."

"What does he want you for?"

"His master wants this queen's life." She paused. "Whoever this queen is on, he looks for them."

"Thousands of eyes below the stage today all saw it: Sunken Cauldron Abbey, outer sect, fourth layer, Shen Xiaoman," she went on. "The person at the other end of the mirror saw it too."

Xiaoman said nothing in the dark for a long time.

"This queen owes you a flatbread," Yinyue said again, her voice dropping. "That debt, I'm afraid, can't be paid now."

Xiaoman gripped the tablet tight, till his knuckles went white: "Owe it. Interest extra."

The tablet didn't go hot, and didn't go cold. It just lay against his palm, warm, like a small curled-up thing. Tell the one in Xu to go to Ye in the tenth month and wait. Where Ye was, he didn't know. He thought: that sentence, I've got it.

---

Early on the ninth of the twelfth month, before the stewards' office was open, the steward who called the names arrived alone. He turned the outer sect's register to the end, to the line with the two small words *talked back* added after it, picked up his brush, and on the three characters of "Shen Xiaoman" he added a stroke of his own: no words added, only those three characters traced over again from start to finish, traced thicker than any name on the page. When he'd done, he blew the ink dry, closed the register, and put the brush back on its rest. Outside the window, the kitchen smoke was only just rising.

---

On the third day after the contest, the eleventh of the twelfth month, Xiaoman was carrying water past the mountain gate. Elder Qu was sweeping the steps of the mountain gate, and his broom suddenly stopped: "Put the buckets down." Xiaoman put the buckets down. Elder Qu said: "Come with me." He shouldered his broom and set off up the back of the mountain. The path got narrower and narrower, until there was room for only one foot, and Xiaoman, following behind, didn't even breathe hard. Elder Qu didn't look back: "Fourth layer now. Your legs are steadier than last time."

On the top of the cliff stood a grass hut, its door half open. Inside, a bamboo bed, a wooden table, and on the table two teacups. One stood mouth up, with half a mouthful of cold tea left in the bottom. The other was turned mouth down, set square and precise, under a thick layer of dust, as if nobody had turned it over in many years. At the hut's door was a bluestone, swept so clean there wasn't a single fallen leaf on it, and on the stone a censer with three sticks of incense already lit, as if it had been known all along that he'd come. Xiaoman walked up to the bluestone, stopped short, lifted his foot and scraped the sole on a stone to the side, scraped it clean, and only then stepped onto it. Elder Qu said: "On the ninth of the ninth month, on the steps, you scraped them too. More than three thousand children have walked over the steps I sweep. Those who scraped their soles before they stepped: two." Xiaoman asked: "Who was the other one?" Elder Qu didn't answer.

He sat down at the edge of the bluestone: "Outer disciples don't have a master. The way is not passed to six ears; in a lifetime a master can only teach a few people well. If you want a master, someone has to want you." He took a sheet of red paper from his robe and laid it on the bluestone: a discipleship card, the words already written, only a name left blank. "I haven't taken a disciple in more than forty years. Whether you write it or not, decide for yourself."

Xiaoman took up the brush and wrote his own name in the blank space, more carefully than he had ever written it.

Elder Qu stood up, lit three new sticks of incense from the old ones, and faced out beyond the cliff (below the clouds and mist was the Si, and by the Si the main hall of the Sunken Cauldron Abbey), and stood up straight: "Qu, disciple of the eleventh generation of the Sunken Cauldron Abbey, today takes one disciple: Shen, of Mount Meng in Xu Province, named Xiaoman. Reported above to the founder." He said it slowly, like reading out a very old list. When he had finished, he gazed at the clouds beyond the cliff and said low: "...More than forty years ago, in front of this stone, I said it once before." Xiaoman didn't catch it: "What did you say, Master?" Elder Qu said: "Nothing. Kneel." Xiaoman made the three kneelings and nine knockings, then knocked his head three more times towards the founder. Elder Qu's hand pressed down on his shoulder once, very hard.

Elder Qu tossed him a thin scroll of a book: "Inner work. The *Yellow Court Classic*. Learn it by heart first." Xiaoman opened it, and the very first line was "The clear water of the jade pool waters the spirit root." He looked up: "Master, it talks about spirit roots here too?" Elder Qu said: "The spirit root in the *Yellow Court Classic*{典=huangting-linggen} is the spittle under the tongue. Everyone has one." Then he went into the hut and brought out an old cloth bundle and unwrapped it layer by layer: half a roll of bamboo slips, older even than the *Breathing Classic*. "A sword art left by the abbey's founder. In three hundred years, nobody has mastered a single palace of it. It needs all five phases, not one missing, before the sword qi will turn. A five-phase mixed spirit root: the kind the whole world laughs at. I've waited fifty years."

Yinyue came out, crouched on his shoulder, looked at Elder Qu, looked at the bamboo slips, and narrowed her eyes: "...This queen's disciple. Is he good enough?" Xiaoman glanced at his shoulder without thinking. Elder Qu looked at his shoulder, saw nothing, looked again: "Is there something on your shoulder?" Xiaoman said quickly: "...A, a bug." Yinyue's whiskers stood up one by one in a fury: "Who are you calling a bug?!" When she left, she stamped hard on his collarbone through his clothes.

Elder Qu asked nothing more and pushed the bamboo slips in front of him: "The *River-and-Luo Sword Art*. Read the opening."

Xiaoman bent to look, and his hand stopped. Some of the characters on the slips he couldn't read; but what was cut on the first slip wasn't words. It was nine numbers, three to a row, with a five in the middle. This sword art was laid out by the Luo Writing. He didn't need to look at the slips. He said it straight out: "Wear nine, tread on one; three on the left, seven on the right; two and four are the shoulders, six and eight are the feet; five sits in the middle."

It was very quiet on top of the cliff. A wind blew past, and the smoke of the three incense sticks in the censer leaned over all together. Elder Qu's face had never had much expression on it; this time, it did: "...You didn't look at the slips." Xiaoman said: "My ma taught me. She said her pa taught her. And her pa got it from an old book." Elder Qu was silent for a long, long time, so long that a length of incense ash broke off without a sound and fell on the bluestone. Then he said: "...Your ma. Who is she?"

Xiaoman opened his mouth. His mother was a hunter's wife, daughter of a down-at-heel village schoolmaster. Who else could she be? He thought for a long time and could only say: "...My ma is my grandfather's daughter." The corner of Elder Qu's mouth moved. He didn't ask anything more. He rolled up the bamboo slips, and his finger paused at the edge of them, as if it had touched a very old notch.

Elder Qu picked up a flat bluestone from by his feet (picked up on the bank of the Si) and tapped it lightly with the broom handle. "*Tong*—" The sound wasn't loud, but it was long, and went far. "You're from Xu Province," said Elder Qu. "In the nine palaces, Xu Province is Xun Four. Today I'll teach you Xun Four first."

He turned the broom upside down in his hand and moved just once. The leaves that had gathered all winter at the cliff's edge, every last one, were swept up and set out in mid-air in a square grid, three to a row, nine squares in all, the middle one empty; then they came drifting down and fell into the shape of one character: **Xu**, as in Xu Province. That character was missing its middle stroke. Elder Qu leaned the broom back in the corner: "I've practised forty years and only got this far. This sword art wants the five phases each to give birth to the next, turning in a ring inside the body." With the broom handle he drew a ring in the fallen leaves, put five dots on it, then wiped out one. "What I lack is fire. Forty years, and there's always a gap in the ring."

Xiaoman looked at the ring with one dot missing and thought of the stove-fire ring that had started turning in his body in the stone chamber of the secret realm. So in all the world he wasn't the only one who leaked. Elder Qu shouldered the broom and walked towards the grass hut, not looking back: "You've got all five phases. When you've filled it in, come and find me."

At the door he stopped again, his back to Xiaoman, and said: "The Foundation Pill. You didn't get it." Xiaoman said: "No, I didn't." Elder Qu said: "Do you want it?" Xiaoman said: "I want it." Elder Qu was silent a while: "...You'll get one, later. Before you take it, come and ask me first." With that, he went into the hut.

Xiaoman went down the mountain. Halfway down, he looked back. The door of the grass hut on the cliff top was open, and Elder Qu sat at the wooden table with the two teacups before him, one mouth up, one mouth down. He sat for a long time, then reached out and touched the cup that was mouth down, touched it once, and drew his hand back. That cup was still turned down.
