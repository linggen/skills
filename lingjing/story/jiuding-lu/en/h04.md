# Chapter Three: Three Trials Passed on His Own at the Immortal Gate / Five Faint Colours Set the Whole Hall Laughing

At noon on the seventh day of the ninth month, Shen Xiaoman was walking with his uncle on the high road to the Si River when someone behind them started shouting, and a litter came bouncing through the middle of the crowd. On the litter sat Ma Xiaobao with a piece of osmanthus cake in his hand. Ma San walked alongside, his chest puffed out where a letter of recommendation sat tucked inside his coat.

The Ma family's letter didn't frighten Xiaoman much. What he feared most was a stone. His uncle said that after you passed the three trials you went into the main hall and put your hand on a spirit-testing stone, and the stone lit up with whatever spirit qi was in you, and the colour it showed was your spirit root. Before she went to sleep, Yinyue in the wooden tablet at his chest had left him only one thing: "Keep it hidden. Don't let anyone see." If that stone lit up and showed a streak of silver, or a fox's tail, the whole hall would see it. He didn't dare think further. He had asked the tablet three times. It hadn't answered once.

The further south they went, the more people were on the road to the Si River, until in the end there was no seeing where they stopped: thousands of households, all hurrying towards the same day.

---

At dusk on the eighth day they reached the Si River.

Xiaoman smelled it first. The wind came down off the mountain, and when he breathed it in it was cool and sweet, like a mouthful of water fresh from the well in the dog days, cold all the way down below his navel. Before he had even looked up, his feet had stopped.

Sunken Cauldron Mountain wasn't very tall, only steep. In the pines at its foot hung a faint white haze, drifting slowly between the treetops. Above the mountain's waist the cloud was wound on tight, and a flight of stone steps ran straight up from the foot into the mist, with no end in sight. Somewhere in the cloud a speck of white flashed, and flashed again: two cranes, circling the summit no one could see before they slipped back into the mist. At the top of the steps was the gate of the Sunken Cauldron Abbey. Xiaoman stood with his mouth open for a long time.

The inns at the foot of the mountain had long since filled, and the best room of all had been taken by the Ma family of Mount Meng. People lay packed under the eaves of the temple too. Beside one pillar burned a fire, and beside the fire sat a man with a face full of hard meat and a short knife at his belt, its sheath wrapped in red silk. His name was Ge, sixth of his brothers, and he was the escort the Ma family had hired to see the young master safely there. The Ma family hadn't kept a bed for him, so he sat under the eaves drinking the Ma family's wine.

A few children gathered round the fire to warm their hands and asked if he had come for the trials too. Ge Liu said, "Took them. Twenty years ago. Outer sect, third layer of Qi Condensation." He fished a walnut out of his coat and pinched it between two fingers. Crack. It broke. "Third layer. That's all it gets you. The threshold to Foundation Establishment, I never got near it. Sat on the third layer six years, then came down the mountain to guard caravans."

A father asked, "And Foundation Establishment... is it hard?" Ge Liu held up one finger and moved it across the ring of children's faces by the fire. "There are thousands of you. Tomorrow, the ones who climb up, get tested and get through the gate: a hundred or so. Out of that hundred or so, the ones who'll ever reach Foundation Establishment: one."

It went quiet under the eaves. A stick in the fire popped. Xiaoman couldn't hold it in. "Mister, the hundred days to Foundation Establishment: does that mean a hundred days?" Ge Liu laughed. "The hundred days are for laying the groundwork. I laid mine for six years." In his head, Xiaoman struck out the eighty-three days he'd thought he had to spare.

A barefoot boy suddenly stood up and said loudly, "Then I'll be the one!" Everyone under the eaves laughed. The boy didn't. He said, "There are seven mouths at home and two mu of hillside. This year we got fewer beans in than we put in the ground." He lifted his chin. "Five taels a year. That feeds them three years." It went quiet under the eaves again. Ge Liu didn't laugh. He said, "...I said that too, back then."

Just then one of the Ma servants came looking for him with a lantern and said in a low voice, "Escort Ge, the Third Master asks: that layout you talked about, have you drawn it?" Ge Liu patted his chest. "Drawn. Five taels of silver, and not one square off." He followed the lantern away. All Xiaoman remembered was "five taels of silver" and "square".

---

For ten coppers his uncle rented half a straw pallet in somebody's cowshed. At the other end of the pallet lay a thin boy of fourteen or fifteen, thin as a clothes pole, with a bundle on his back bigger than he was. He didn't make a sound.

That night Xiaoman took out the last flatbread, broke off half and stuffed it in his mouth, and when he looked up the thin boy was still awake, staring at the half left in his hand, his throat working.

Xiaoman thought of that night at the bottom of the ravine, and the half flatbread he'd broken off for the little fox with her tails cut away, and he handed over the half in his hand. "Eat it. We've got three hundred steps to climb tomorrow." The boy stuffed it in his mouth in one go and only when he'd finished chewing remembered to say, "...Thanks. I'm Sun Ergou." Xiaoman said, "Shen Xiaoman." Sun Ergou said, "I'll remember."

Xiaoman had only half a flatbread in his belly, and only a stone on his mind.

---

The ninth day of the ninth month was the Double Ninth. Before it was properly light, the landing on the Si River was already crowded, the mist creeping along the water and every face a grey one. All night, boat after boat had come up from downstream and put people off here. A li or so up the bank, the patch roped off at the foot of the steps was the Sunken Cauldron Abbey's ground.

A space had been cleared on the landing. At the edge of it, on a bluestone mooring block, stood a man in a white robe, so white that on a landing full of muddy footprints it hadn't a speck of mud on it. In his hand he held a grey stone the size of an egg, and in front of him stood a row of small children, the biggest seven or eight, the smallest still being carried, all brought there early by their parents. He pressed the stone into a child's palm, pressed once, looked once, shook his head: next. From start to finish he didn't make a sound.

The line reached a man who looked like a stonemason, with two sons. The elder was twelve or thirteen, dark and solid, with hands like his father's, calluses on every knuckle. The stone was pressed to him and stayed a grey stone. Nothing lit. The man in white shook his head. The younger was five or six, his nose still running. The stone was pressed to him and all at once it lit: lit up green, a sheet of green like the first wheat shoots in spring, one colour, clean, without a trace of anything else in it.

Only then did the man in white nod. He took an ingot of silver from his sleeve, set it in the stonemason's palm and closed the man's fingers over it one by one. Xiaoman was standing close, and he saw the bottom of the ingot as it turned: there was a mark punched into it, a circle with a line across it.

He went cold. He had never seen that mark, but he had heard of it. The pedlar by the well in the eleventh month had said that over by the Min River a man in white had taken away a baby only seven days born, for a packet of silver, and on the bottom of that silver was punched a circle with a line across it.

At that white robe, the hairs on Xiaoman's back stood up before anything else did. He shrank half a step back into the crowd, behind a man with a carrying pole, and when he looked again the man's face was blurred by the mist; his voice, he hadn't used once. There was more than one man in white under heaven. All the same, he thought of what his father had said on top of the woodpile: what it hears isn't your breathing, it's your fright. So he let a breath out thin and long, let it out to the bottom, and slowly drew it back in. From start to finish, the man in white never looked his way.

The stonemason, holding the silver in both hands, knelt down and knocked his head once on the ground before the man in white. Then he turned round and knocked his head once on the ground before his little son. The child didn't understand. He looked at the back of his father's head and giggled. The man in white picked him up and walked up the bank, where a carriage with a green canvas hood stood waiting. Halfway there the child finally felt something was wrong. He twisted in the man's arms, stretched out both hands, and burst out crying: "Brother!"

The elder boy ran two steps after him before his father grabbed him by the collar. The carriage left. On the bank stood a man of about fifty in a grey robe, with a face that had never quite woken up. Before he got into the carriage, the man in white gave him a small nod. He gave a small nod back and turned away towards the steps.

Someone in the crowd said softly, "From the Taiyi Palace on Mount Taihua. Comes every few years. Only picks children of one colour." Someone else said, "He doesn't go up the mountain. Below the gate is Sunken Cauldron ground, but the landing isn't. Gets in ahead of the gate opening and picks first." Someone else said, "One ingot. A full ten taels."

The stonemason tucked the ingot into his coat, tucked it deep, and turned to his elder son. "You go up too. If you pass, it's five taels a year. If you don't, don't come back. There are still three mouths at home." Then he walked off north and didn't look back once.

The elder boy stood on the landing and didn't cry. He turned his head and saw Xiaoman watching him, and said, "That stone... if it lights up, that's who the immortals want?" Xiaoman said, "...Yes. That's how it is." The boy said, "Where've they taken Xiaodou?" Xiaoman had no answer. The boy looked down at his own hands. Those hands had just been pressed to the stone, and the stone hadn't lit. He said, "If I'd lit up too, they'd have taken me as well."

Xiaoman asked him his name. He said, "Shidan."

---

Before the hour of the Dragon, the foot of the steps was packed so tight not even water could get through. People selling griddle cakes, people selling tea, and people selling "Ascension Charms" (a sheet of yellow paper with a few red bars painted on it; stick it to your calf, they said, and you'd climb the steps without getting out of breath, ten coppers a sheet) wormed their way through the gaps in the crowd, and business was remarkably good. His uncle stood in front of the charm-seller for a long while and in the end didn't buy. He turned to Xiaoman and said, "That one's a swindle." Xiaoman said, "How do you know?" His uncle said, "I stuck on three, back then."

At the foot of the steps a patch of ground had been roped off, and a dozen or so stewards in grey robes stood inside it checking ages (twelve and up) and home districts. Each child was given a bamboo slip called an entry tag, with their name branded on it then and there with a red-hot iron spike. The steward calling the names was about fifty, with a face that had never quite woken up, and he yawned as he called. Xiaoman knew that face: before dawn, on the landing, this was the man the one in white had nodded to before getting into the carriage. He tapped the bamboo tag against his palm and said, "Three trials, one stroke for each. Three strokes and you go into the main hall to be tested. Lose your tag and you were never here. Those with a letter of recommendation who fail a trial may make it up. How they make it up is for the steward to say." Knives, swords, bows and staves were all to be taken off and hung on a wooden rack, each marked with a bamboo slip bearing the owner's name; they would be carried up to the gate at dusk, and whoever got in could collect his own. Xiaoman hung up his grandfather's bow. Its back, wound all over with ox sinew, hung in a row of gleaming knives and swords like a pair of patched trousers in among new clothes. Xiaoman made a note that the bow hung third row, seventh from the left, and tucked the entry tag inside his coat, against the wooden tablet.

Just then there was a stir at the back of the crowd. "Make way! Young Master Ma is here!" The Ma litter had arrived. One servant held an umbrella over Ma Xiaobao and another fanned him, though that day it was neither raining nor sunny. Ma San walked beside the litter, every so often pressing a hand through his clothes against the letter in his coat, as if he were holding down a bird that might fly off. Behind the litter walked Ge Liu. Ma Xiaobao spotted Xiaoman at once, lifted his chin and aimed both nostrils at him. "Well, Shen Xiaoman. That door of yours, did you carry it back home?" Xiaoman said, "Carried it back, Brother Xiaobao. It hangs a bit crooked."

A quarter before the hour of the Dragon, the Si River suddenly went still.

It wasn't the wind dropping. It was the water stopping. A broad river, flowing east, suddenly stood as if someone had pressed a hand on it: not a single wave, flat as a polished bronze mirror. Thousands of people on the bank, and those who were talking stopped talking and those who were crying stopped crying; nobody told anybody, and they all looked at the river together. In the middle of it the water parted to either side, opening a trench, and up from the bottom of the trench something slowly rose. At first it looked like a reef, blue-black and thick with water moss; as it came up you could see it was the back of a shell, bigger than the ferry boats moored along the bank, its pattern laid out square by square like a field. It was a great turtle. It pushed a head bigger than a millstone out of the water, narrowed its two old eyes, and swam slowly towards the bank, and wherever it passed the water closed behind it without a ripple. On its back stood a man in a grey robe, with both hands tucked into his sleeves. As the turtle swam past the landing, his eyes fell on the cleared space there (the wheel ruts of the green-hooded carriage were still fresh) and his face darkened at once. His brows rose, and he said in a low voice, "Insolent wretches, to dare snatch people at the very door of the Sunken Cauldron Abbey. One day I'll come to their gate and have an answer for this."

Xiaoman felt his chest go heavy, as if someone had laid a door across his back, heavy enough to buckle his knees. The people in front of him and behind him were already going down on their knees one after another, and nobody had told them to kneel. Xiaoman knelt too, and only once he was down did he understand it wasn't his own doing: it was that unseen weight, pressing in from the river, so that nobody along the whole bank could straighten up. The turtle reached the bank, and the man took one step from its back to the foot of the steps, another step and he was halfway up the mountain, one more and he had gone into the cloud. The turtle drew its head back in and sank into the Si River without leaving so much as a whirl on the surface. The river flowed again, and the door laid across their backs went with it. People got up one by one and brushed the mud off their knees, and nobody spoke. After a long while an old man said softly, "...The Abbot. The [Abbot]{注=xuanchenzi} of the Sunken Cauldron Abbey."

On the hour of the Dragon a bell rang out from the gate, dang, and thousands of children went quiet in an instant. At the top of the steps, though no one had seen when, stood an old man with a broom: a short grey cotton jacket, a bamboo broom to lean on, a bent back. He didn't so much as glance down. He just went on sweeping the step under his feet, stroke after stroke: the three-hundredth step, the very top one. These three hundred steps, and the stone terrace in front of the gate above them, he had swept since before dawn, starting from the bottom and coming up step by step; at this one he stopped, and swept it longest of all. The old disciples of the abbey said the old man had been sweeping these three hundred steps for fifty years. Only after quite a while did he speak. His voice wasn't loud, but each word dropped clear to the foot of the mountain. "The Sunken Cauldron Abbey takes people. It doesn't look at birth, it doesn't look at letters of recommendation..." A hum went through the crowd. Ma San's hand froze on his chest. The old man went on: "...it looks at three things. Legs. Head. Nerve." Having said that, he lowered his head and went back to sweeping.

At the top of the steps a steward lit a stick of incense, stood it in a bronze burner and shouted, "Begin!"

---

The first trial tested the legs: three hundred stone steps, each as high as a knee, one stick of incense. Before the word "Begin" had even landed, thousands of children rushed up in a swarm, pushing and shoving, crying for their fathers and mothers, treading on other people's shoes and having their own shoes trodden on. Straw sandals and cloth shoes fell all the way up. One boy went head over heels onto a step, got up and kept charging with one bare foot. It was a pot of porridge, a flock of ducks with somebody's cage door flung open. Outside the rope, the parents' shouted names rose wave on wave: "Erniu! Slow down!" "Bao'er, don't look back!" "Three steps, one breath!" That last one was his uncle.

Shen Xiaoman didn't charge. He walked at the very back. His father had taught him: chasing a hare is about speed, chasing a deer is about steadiness; a mountain path belongs to the mountain, and if you fight the mountain for it the mountain won't give way. Let them charge, there are three hundred steps; let them be fast, there's only one stick of incense. Xiaoman turned the two lines over and over in his head and went up one step at a time, three steps breathing in, three steps breathing out, looking only at the step under his feet.

The children who had charged up in front, at around the hundredth step, began one by one to look as if stones were tied to their legs. Some sat on the steps panting, gaping, unable to straighten up; some held on to the cliff face and shuffled one step at a time. Ma Xiaobao had been carried up by two servants, one on each side, his feet half a foot off the ground, swinging in the air, still chewing his osmanthus cake. The old sweeper glanced at them from far up at the top, and the servants let go, and Ma Xiaobao dropped, thump, onto his backside on the hundred-and-twentieth step, as slack as a puddle of mud mixed too thin. Xiaoman walked past him and didn't stop.

At around the hundred-and-thirtieth step he saw a boy from Yan Province. The boy had charged up with the first wave and was now holding on to the cliff, his face white as paper, his legs shaking like a sieve. When he saw Xiaoman his eyes lit up as if he'd seen a rope thrown down to save him, and he shouted, "Brother! Give me a pull!"

Xiaoman wanted to pull him. But he knew that if he did, that breath of his, three steps in, three steps out, would break, and once it broke he would be sitting down here as well. His father had said: in the mountains, when you meet someone who's fallen in a pit, first look at whether your own feet are steady; if you aren't steady yourself and you reach out to pull, the two of you go in together. So Xiaoman didn't reach out. He only said, "Stop charging. Three steps, one breath. Follow my feet." The boy followed him for ten steps or so, and at the hundred-and-fortieth he sat down after all, waving his hand. "Brother, you go, you go..." Xiaoman looked back at him once and went. A dozen steps further on he heard the boy crying behind him, very quietly, as if afraid someone would hear. He didn't look back, but he thought: one day, when I'm steady enough, I'll pull. However many I can pull, I'll pull that many. The thought flashed past and was gone. At the time he didn't take it seriously.

At the hundred-and-fiftieth step a girl with two little plaits rushed past him. She didn't sit down and she didn't cry. All he saw was a side view of a face with the tip of its nose red with cold, teeth gritted, rushing up in one breath, plaits flicking. Xiaoman's heart jumped. Ahe? Some days before he left she had stopped him by the well and asked how many days it was to the Si River and how many flatbreads you ate a day, and written it down in a notebook he had never seen before. He'd thought she was asking for her father. He called out, "Ahe!" The girl didn't turn round and rushed on into the mist. A few steps higher a dark, solid figure brushed past him too, head down, calluses on every knuckle. It was Shidan. He wasn't panting and he wasn't looking at anyone. Each step he took landed hard, as if he wanted to stamp the stairs into the mountain.

The hundred-and-sixtieth step came. This step was no different from any other, except that on the cliff face beside it, from who knew what years, people had carved writing with stones, with knives, with fingernails, packed close, layer over layer. Some were names, some were dates, some were just a single stroke. All of it had been carved by people who had sat down on this step. As Xiaoman stepped over it he turned his head and took one more look into the carvings. He didn't know what he was looking for. He didn't find it.

Past the hundred-and-sixtieth step the stairs grew steep, each half a finger higher than the last, until by about the two-hundredth they had piled up into a ladder. The cliffs on either side drew in too, until at the narrow places only two people could get by, sideways. He didn't look up and he didn't look down. He only counted: one breath in, two, three; lift the foot, set it down, lift it again. Far off in the mist came a rasp, rasp, again and again: a broom on bluestone. Each step he climbed, it came a little nearer.

At around the two-hundred-and-sixtieth step there was suddenly shouting behind him. "Out of the way! Out of the way!" It was that litter, coming up again. Down at the foot of the steps, Ma San had brought out the letter. The steward had looked at the signature and said, "A letter of recommendation. He may make it up." So four bearers were carrying Ma Xiaobao up, jolting all the way. The litter squeezed past Xiaoman and pressed him against the cliff, scraping off some skin. Having squeezed past, it stopped at the narrowest stretch ahead. Ma Xiaobao turned round on the litter and grinned at him. "Carry slower. This young master wants to look at the view."

There was no view in the mist. The four bearers stood across the steps, blocking them front and back so tight a cat couldn't have got through. Xiaoman looked up. He couldn't see the bronze burner; the mist was too thick. But he could work it out: by now the stick of incense was probably down to a finger's length.

He stood there and thought for a moment. His father had said: something blocking you up ahead doesn't always have to be climbed over. He crouched low, lay flat on the steps, and through the gap between the two front bearers' legs and under the litter's poles, the way you go through brush after a badger, he rolled through. The litter rocked. By the time Ma Xiaobao turned round to look for him, Xiaoman was five steps above. Ma Xiaobao didn't shout, though. He was looking down at the step beneath the litter, where he'd seen something, and he reached down, scooped it up and tucked it into his sleeve. Xiaoman looked back and saw, but had no time to think about it.

The last few dozen steps he climbed in the mist. Then, as he walked, the mist suddenly thinned. He looked up, and the mist was below his feet, one unbroken white, spread flat all the way to the edge of the sky. Mount Meng, the Si River, the county town, Shi'ao Village, all covered over beneath it. Above the mist was blazing sun, and halls rising tier on tier, their eaves curling up corner after corner like a flock of birds about to take off. He looked just once and then lowered his head again: the last step had come.

The very top step the old man had swept just before dawn, and its bluestone face shone bright enough to show the sky. The few hundred children who had come up first had stepped onto it one after another and left a mess of muddy footprints, and the old man followed behind them, sweeping, stroke after stroke. Xiaoman looked down at his straw sandals: three days on the road, caked in mud, with a hole in the front and one toe poking out of it. On the terrace, the steward watching the burner had already raised his hand. Xiaoman lifted his foot, then put it down again, bent over, and scraped the sole of his sandal against the edge of the step below: scraped once, looked, scraped again. When it was clean, only then did he lift his foot and step lightly up. The old sweeper had just swept his way to Xiaoman's side, and the broom paused.

The steward's hand came down. "The incense is out!" The last bit of ash in the burner broke off just as his foot touched the stone.

The steward came over and held out his hand. "Tag." Xiaoman felt inside his coat. The wooden tablet was there. The entry tag was gone. He felt through his clothes inside and out three times, and broke into a cold sweat. The steward waited a moment and said, "Lose your tag and you were never here." He made to move on to the next one.

Just then the litter came up as well. Xiaoman remembered the thing Ma Xiaobao had bent to scoop into his sleeve under the litter and said, "Steward, my entry tag is in Young Master Ma's sleeve." Ma Xiaobao shouted from the litter, "Liar!" The old sweeper had just swept his way to the side of the litter, and his broom came down, tok, neither hard nor soft, on the litter's pole. The litter shook, Ma Xiaobao's sleeve jerked, and a bamboo tag fell, clack, on the bluestone. Ma Xiaobao's face went red, then white, then green. The steward picked it up, looked at the name branded on it, handed it to Xiaoman and marked a stroke. The old sweeper didn't say a word. He lowered his head and went back to sweeping.

Xiaoman stood at the edge of the terrace and looked back down once. The steps fell away and disappeared into the white mist, and beneath the mist were thousands of fathers and mothers, as small as sesame seeds on the ground. Which seed was his uncle, he couldn't tell at all. When he turned his head, the old sweeper was leaning on his broom and, for the first time, had raised his eyes. He was looking at him: at his feet, then at his chest, at that breath of his going in and out.

Xiaoman's heart gave a jolt. Three hundred steps, and his breath was still even. He had had his marrow washed, and he'd done a year of breathing out and in. Could the old man tell? He quickly opened his mouth wide and started panting hard, panting like a torn bellows, his tongue half hanging out, panting exactly like the children who had sat down on the steps just now, and as he panted he wiped sweat off with his sleeve, wiping with great effort. The old sweeper watched him for a while and said, "Good panting." He went back to sweeping. Xiaoman never did work out whether those two words were praising the panting or praising the act.

---

After the first trial, no more than four or five hundred were standing at the top of the steps. The families who had brought them now came round by a small path up the side of the mountain to wait outside the gate.

Above the terrace, a man in a dark robe came skimming over from the back of the mountain standing on a sword, three or four zhang off the ground, not a corner of his robe out of place. He landed in front of the main hall, put his sword away and went inside. The children stood with their mouths open for a long time.

The second trial tested the head, on a stone terrace inside the gate. On the terrace stood several dozen stone walls, and on each wall nine empty squares were carved, three across and three down, like a game board. At the foot of each wall lay nine stones, each carved with a number from one to nine. An old steward in charge of the second trial, his brows and beard all white, walked back and forth in front of the walls with his hands behind his back and said, "Add across, add down, add corner to corner: all the same. One stick of incense."

Some laid the stones out in a flower, some stacked them into a pagoda, some swapped them two by two, back and forth, for half a stick of incense, getting more muddled with each swap until they swapped themselves into tears. At the foot of the wall next to Xiaoman's squatted Shidan. He stared at the nine stones for a long, long time without placing a single one, and then he put two in his mouth. The third wouldn't fit, and he was still pushing at it with his cheeks bulging. The old steward went over and slapped him on the back. Pff, pff: two stones spat out on the ground. A steward nearby laughed. "Every three years there's one." Shidan didn't laugh. He wiped his mouth and said, his eyes red, "Swallow them and it doesn't count as wrong." The old steward looked at him for a while and said nothing. He sighed and walked away.

Xiaoman heard that. He thought of what Shidan's father had said where the roads parted: if you don't pass, don't come back.

At the edge of the terrace, a low wall divided inside the gate from outside. Two heads poked over the top of it: one was a Ma servant, one was Ge Liu. Ge Liu was signalling with his fingers, so many fingers, row after row, fast and sure, like caravan guards trading secret signs. Ma Xiaobao squatted at the foot of his wall, chewing osmanthus cake and laying the stones out by the signals. The incense had burned down only a little way when he dusted off his hands and stood up. Add across, fifteen; add down, fifteen; add corner to corner, fifteen again. When he'd finished he strolled over to Xiaoman on purpose, craned his neck to take a look, threw back his head with his nostrils to the sky and snorted two words: "Child's play."

His mother had taught him this. She called it the Luo Writing, and said it was nine numbers that had come up out of the Luo River in ancient times: "Nine on the head, one under the feet, three on the left, seven on the right, two and four for the shoulders, six and eight for the feet, five in the middle." Xiaoman laid them out that way: five dead in the centre, nine at the head, one at the feet, three at the left hand, seven at the right. When he got to the shoulders he was stuck. "Two and four for the shoulders": his mother hadn't said which went on the left. He put the two on the left shoulder first and added: the left column, two plus three, only five, and whichever stone went at the foot, it couldn't make fifteen. The stick of incense in the bronze burner was already more than half burned.

On top of the wall Ge Liu's fingers were still signalling. They were meant for Ma Xiaobao, but Xiaoman could see them too. Copy them and you wouldn't need the whole stick of incense. Xiaoman watched those fingers for a while, then moved his eyes away. Something bought with the Ma family's silver: if it was right, it was the Ma family's; if it was wrong, you went down together. He didn't have five taels of silver, and he didn't want to owe the Ma family a single square.

He lowered his head and looked only at his own wall. If the two didn't add up on the left, then it was on the wrong side. He swapped the shoulders, four on the left and two on the right, and the left column came to fifteen: add across, fifteen; add down, fifteen; add corner to corner, fifteen. In the burner there was a short stub of incense left.

The old steward strolled over to Ma Xiaobao's wall first, looked at it for a while, nodded, and asked, "Why is the five in the middle?" Ma Xiaobao opened his mouth. Sweat broke out on his forehead bead by bead, and his eyes slid towards the top of the wall. The old steward followed his eyes to the top of the wall, saw Ge Liu, and raised an eyebrow. "Ge Liu?" The head on the wall vanished, whoosh. The old steward said, "Twenty years ago, that 'five' of yours was learned by rote too." He made a mark on Ma Xiaobao's entry tag. It wasn't a stroke. It was a circle.

The old steward strolled over to Xiaoman and asked the same thing. "Why is the five in the middle?" Xiaoman said, "One goes with nine, two with eight, three with seven, four with six. Every pair makes ten, and they face each other across the middle one. Nine stones: pair them off, and what's left is five." The old steward said nothing for a long while. At last he said, "Who taught you?" Xiaoman said, "The rhyme, my mother taught me. The why, I worked out just now."

The old steward said "Mm," and made a stroke on his tag, but he didn't leave. He sat down on a stone in front of the wall, thumped the small of his back and said, "Later people said that when Yu was taming the floods he got these nine numbers, and by these nine squares he divided the nine provinces and cast nine cauldrons, one for each province. When the Zhou dynasty failed, the cauldrons were gone. Some say they sank into the river at the foot of this mountain, the Si. The First Emperor of Qin passed through here and sent a thousand men into the water to fish them out. In the *Records of the Grand Historian*{典=shiji-sishui} it comes to two words: not found. The founding master of this abbey built a hut on this mountain and kept watch over the river." Here he paused, glanced back at the mountain behind him, and lowered his voice a little. "Those are old folks' tales. Your mother taught you well. And you worked it out well."

Xiaoman chewed the words "nine cauldrons" over once in his mouth.

He turned round. At the far end of the terrace, in front of another wall, someone had long since finished: red nose, two little plaits, squatting at the foot of the wall with her head down, writing something with a stick of charcoal in a little notebook with a blue cloth cover. It was Ahe. Xiaoman called out and pushed two steps that way, the crowd surged, and when he looked up again the foot of that wall was empty. On the ground lay only a worn-down stub of charcoal. She was avoiding him.

At the foot of the wall next door, Shidan was still squatting. He had spat the nine stones out and laid them back again, crooked, with the five at the edge. On his tag there was only the stroke from the steps.

---

After the second trial, fewer than three hundred were left. On Ma Xiaobao's tag was a circle. Outside the gate, Ma San brought out the letter again. The steward looked at the letter, looked over towards the main hall, said nothing, and let him into the line. How they made it up was for the steward to say.

The third trial tested nerve, in an old wood west of the gate. The trees in that wood were each older and darker than the last; walk in at midday and it was like walking into evening. In the middle of the wood stood an ancient pine, and under the pine, on an iron chain thicker than a man's leg, a demon beast was tied up: a body like a fox's, but bigger than an ox; nine heads, nine tails, and four feet with a tiger's claws. The nine heads turned together to stare at every child who walked into the wood; the nine mouths opened together, and when they cried it sounded like nine babies crying all at once. The crying knocked about the wood until your scalp crawled and the soles of your feet went soft.

The wood was kept by another steward, who stood at its edge and said, "What's tied up in there is a [longzhi]{注=longzhi}. It's been tied there decades. Go in, walk to the foot of the old pine, touch the bark, come out, and you get a stroke. One at a time." The first child in went fewer than ten steps and ran out crying. The second went five. The third stood at the edge of the wood with his legs shaking for a long time, never took a single step in, and was dragged off through the crowd by his father.

While they queued, a boy in a silk gown whose family had hired him a tutor saw that everyone was afraid and decided to show off a little. Wagging his head, he began reciting from the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*{典=shanhai-longzhi}: "There is a beast there, shaped like a fox, with nine tails, nine heads and tiger's claws, called the longzhi. Its voice is like a baby's..." There he stopped. A child beside him asked, "Its voice is like a baby's, and then?" The boy in silk slowly went pale. His lips trembled twice before he squeezed out the last three words: "...It. Eats. People." A dead silence fell all around. After a while somebody, no one knew who, burst out wailing first, and then they were all crying.

Ma Xiaobao went in. He took three steps. As his third step came down, the longzhi's nine heads turned towards him together. Ma Xiaobao stopped and didn't move, and after a good while everyone outside the wood could see a pool of water slowly spreading from under the legs of his silk trousers. Two servants rushed into the wood and carried him out between them. Ma Xiaobao, carried through the air, kicked wildly with both legs and kept shouting, "I didn't! It's dew! It's dew!" This time nobody outside the wood could hold it in. They laughed till they staggered, ha ha ha ha, some squatting with their arms round their stomachs, some laughing so hard they were slapping the backs of the people next to them. Only one person didn't laugh. That was Ma San.

But Ma San didn't leave. He stood in the crowd and moved his eyes off his son's wet trousers, moved them inch by inch, until they reached Xiaoman at the back of the queue. He beckoned Ge Liu over and said a few words in a low voice. Ge Liu's face changed and he shook his head. Ma San said something else and took an ingot of silver from his coat. Ge Liu looked at the ingot for a long time and didn't take it. Ma San's face darkened. He said, "Then you can forget those five taels too." Ge Liu didn't answer. He fell back to the rear of the crowd, but he didn't leave. Ma San pressed the ingot on a servant beside him, then took out a small piece of silver, walked to the edge of the wood and slipped it into the sleeve of the steward who kept it.

Before Xiaoman's turn came, he waited outside the wood for nearly an hour. While they waited, some of the other children cried and some shut their eyes and prayed to the Buddha. Xiaoman did nothing. He squatted at the edge of the wood with his head tilted and listened quietly. He saw the servant with the silver too: squeezed in at the very front of the crowd, feeling about on the ground for a pebble, weighing it, feeling for another, picking out the roundest one and clenching it in his palm, his eyes on Xiaoman the whole time. Xiaoman understood. That pebble was meant for him. Once he was in the wood, standing with his back to the crowd, the pebble could only hit him in the back. Only once did he raise his eyes: Ahe walked into the wood with her eyes shut, one step after another, neither fast nor slow, walked to the foot of the old pine, laid a hand on the bark, and walked out again with her eyes still shut. From start to finish she never once looked at the beast.

Shidan was behind him in the queue and squatted down next to him. There was only one stroke on his tag; even if he passed the third trial he couldn't make three. But he queued all the same. He said quietly, "You can work it out." Xiaoman said, "What?" Shidan said, "Those nine numbers. You can work them out, and you can hear it too." He rubbed his callused hands on his knees, over and over. "I can't make three strokes, but I'm going in to touch it anyway. Once I've touched it, at the testing I'll go and kneel and beg. That steward who nodded, he knows the people from Mount Taihua." Xiaoman opened his mouth and couldn't find anything to say.

His father had taught him how to tell a wolf's howl from the wind. A wolf's howl comes out of the chest; it's warm. The wind has only a hole; however loud it gets, it's hollow. He listened for an hour. Each time the nine heads cried, he listened. In the end he noticed something strange: each time the nine mouths opened together and nine cries poured out together, the needles of the old pine deep in the wood trembled with only one of them. The other eight, however loud they cried, didn't stir a needle.

It was his turn. He walked into the wood. He didn't charge forward. He stopped where the chain couldn't reach, planted his feet and shut his eyes. Nine cries poured into his ears together. He listened to them one by one. The first was hollow, like wind blowing into a broken clay jar. The second was hollow too, like someone shouting down a dry well and the answer that comes back up from the bottom. The third, the fourth, the fifth, the sixth, the seventh: hollow. The ninth: hollow. Only the eighth wasn't. The eighth came out of a chest. Between breath in and breath out it carried warmth, and a rankness he couldn't put a name to, and a little hunger. It was a very, very deep hunger, a frantic hunger, a reckless hunger, as if it could eat the whole wood and the whole mountain and still not be full. The other eight were all its shadows.

He had stood too long. At the edge of the wood the steward looked up at the sky. The sun was already slanting west, and there were still a hundred-odd waiting. He waved his hand. "What good is standing still? Next one, go in together, hurry up." So Shidan came in after him, treading in Xiaoman's footprints, one step at a time, and stopped two paces behind him. He said quietly, "I'll follow you."

Xiaoman opened his eyes, bent down and picked a stone up off the ground.

Just then, from outside the wood, whoosh, a pebble flew in from the crowd and struck the longzhi square on the hind leg. The beast felt the pain and all nine heads reared up together. The iron chain snapped taut with a clatter, the old pine itself swayed with the pull, the iron stake at its roots came half a foot up out of the earth, and the chain grew longer by a length. It didn't go for the one who had thrown the pebble. It went for whoever was nearest. Xiaoman had worked out that the pebble was meant for him. He hadn't worked out that it was meant for the beast.

A tiger's claw came sweeping at Xiaoman's head with a rush of wind. Just then the wooden tablet at his chest went suddenly hot, hot enough to make his heart jump, and a very small, very sleepy voice, rising from somewhere very, very deep, said in his ear:

"...Left."

The claw was coming from the left. Without thinking he rolled to the right. The tiger's claw swept past his left shoulder, tore open his jacket and took off a layer of skin. He rolled across the ground, and the stone in his hand was already flying. It struck dead on, square on the tip of the eighth head's nose.

The longzhi froze. Its lunge stalled in mid-air, as though it had suddenly forgotten what it meant to do. Then the other eight heads all at once scattered, like breath on a winter window, and in a blink there was nothing there. All that was left in the wood was a fox-shaped beast, one head, one tail, bigger than an ox, shrinking back behind the old pine.

The wood was suddenly very quiet.

Xiaoman got up and turned round.

That claw had missed him. It hadn't missed. Two paces behind him Shidan lay on the pine needles. From his left shoulder to his right hip, three gashes: jacket, skin and flesh all laid open together. His eyes were still open. He was looking at the little patch of sky above the wood, as if he didn't understand how he had come to be lying down.

Xiaoman threw himself down and pressed both hands on the three gashes. He couldn't hold them. The blood was warm. It welled up between his fingers and onto the pine needles, and the needles turned red one by one. Shidan's lips moved. Xiaoman put his ear close.

"...Taihua... how far?"

Xiaoman said, "Not far."

Shidan's eyes rested on his face a moment, as if he believed it, as if he didn't. Then those eyes stopped moving. In one hand he was still holding his entry tag, holding it tight. The one stroke on it he had climbed up for himself, step by step.

Xiaoman knelt there, his hands still pressing. It was a long while before the voices outside the wood came flooding back into his ears.

The steward who kept the wood had already seized the Ma servant by the wrist and dug a second pebble out of his palm. The servant's face was ashen. He said, "M-my hand slipped..." Ma San pushed forward and slipped something else into the steward's sleeve. The steward weighed his sleeve, let go, glanced down into the wood, opened the register, picked up his brush, and after one line wrote: **Lost in the wood by his own fault.** He also said, "One at a time. He followed the other one in on his own." Xiaoman, kneeling on the pine needles, had only one thought: go in together. He was the one who shouted it.

At the back of the crowd someone said, "The servant threw the pebble." It was Ge Liu. He walked step by step from the back of the crowd to the front, the red-silk sheath at his waist, not a trace of wine in his face. "The Third Master came to me first with an ingot. I didn't take it. I saw." It went quiet all round. Ma San laughed. "Escort Ge's had too much to drink." The steward looked at Ma San, then at Ge Liu, laid down his brush and said, "Once it's written in the register, it's written."

Xiaoman knelt on the pine needles and didn't look round. He tried to prise open the hand Shidan was gripping his tag with. He couldn't prise it open.

The steward shouted from the edge of the wood, "Shen Xiaoman. You didn't touch the bark. It doesn't count."

He got Shidan onto his back. Shidan was half a head taller than him, and on his back his feet dragged along the ground, his heels ploughing two furrows through the needles. Step by step he carried him out of the wood, laid him on the grass at the edge, and covered the three gashes with his own torn jacket. Then he turned round and went back into the wood, past the beast shrunk behind the old pine licking its claws, and pressed his hand once against the bark. The blood on his hand left a print on the tree.

Shen Xiaoman walked out of the wood, and the steward made the third stroke on his tag. Three strokes, all there.

---

The main hall stood on the highest terrace inside the gate. The hall was very big and very old. The paintings on its beams had faded until only their shadows were left, and there was no god's image in it, only, in the very middle, an empty stone stand. The stand was square, on four feet, big enough for a man to lie on, and in the middle of its top was a patch darker than the rest, square as well, as if many years ago something very, very heavy had once stood pressing down on it. In front of the stand rose a spirit-testing stone taller than a man, dull grey, with nothing remarkable to look at. On a prayer cushion beside the stand sat the Abbot: the man from the turtle's back that morning. A grey robe, a mild face, looking even friendlier than the cloth merchant in the town below; and yet of the hundred or so children in the hall, not one dared look up at him twice. The old sweeper had come into the hall too, nobody saw when, and was leaning in the doorway on his broom.

The children who had passed the three trials, a hundred or so all told, stood in a long line in the hall. Xiaoman was at the very end; only Ma Xiaobao, carried up on his litter, stood behind him. The steward calling the names was still the one from the foot of the steps that morning, leaning against a pillar.

A child asked what the stone stand was for, and why there was no immortal on it to worship. The name-calling steward said, "It's kept for a cauldron. Three hundred years now."

The steward pointed his chin at the testing stone, yawned, and recited the rules as usual; he recited them once every three years and they came out like a sutra. "Put your hand on it. Whatever's in you, that's what lights." Another yawn. "If only one colour lights, that's a heavenly spirit root. Don't bother looking. In three hundred years this abbey's never tested a single one. Heavenly-root babies get carried off in swaddling clothes for silver by the big sects. They never get to climb steps here. Outside the gate, others pick first; what's left over comes in through the gate, and that's this abbey's." A child asked, "Babies in swaddling clothes can't even talk. How can anyone tell?" The steward didn't so much as lift his eyelids. "It's written in the *Baopuzi*{典=baopu-bianwen}: 'All who attain immortality happened, when they received their fate, to meet with the qi of the immortals; it is what they were endowed with by nature. So in the womb they already hold the nature that opens onto the Way.' It's brought from the mother's belly. The stone just shines a light on it."

Nobody in the hall laughed at that. Xiaoman looked at that face that had never woken up. He thought of the sheet of green on the landing, and that cry of "Brother!" Brought from the mother's belly; the stone shines a light on it; set down the silver, and the child is carried off. So this was the rule, and the rule was even written in a book.

The steward went on reciting, faster and faster, until it was a droning string of sutra. At the end his eyelids lifted and his eyes made a round of the line. "This year the outer sect takes a hundred and eight. If Abbot [Xuanchenzi]{注=xuanchenzi} nods, you're taken." Brush in hand, he counted down the register one by one, and for each one taken, he struck a line. Partway through, the tip of his brush slid over one line without stopping; under that line were four freshly written characters.

The line inched forward. Each time the stone lit up at the front, the hall made a sound. The light didn't float on the face of the stone; it came through from deep inside it, layer after layer, and each time it came through, the whole hall grew a little cooler, as if it had just rained. When it lit up well, a ripple of wonder; when it lit up dim, a ripple of laughter. The barefoot boy from under the eaves last night was near the front, grass still stuck to the soles of his feet. He pressed, and four colours lit. He grinned and turned round to hold one finger up to the crowd. The steward asked for his birth date as usual, and he blurted out a year (at the foot of the steps he had said twelve) and when he'd said it his face went white at once. The steward counted on his fingers. "Eleven. The notice says twelve and up." The boy said, "I'll be twelve after New Year!" The steward said, "After New Year, and three years after that, next time the gate opens, come back." The Abbot looked at him for a while and didn't nod. The boy didn't cry. He walked to a corner of the hall, gripping his entry tag with its three full strokes, and stood there without moving.

Along both sides of the hall was another row of prayer cushions, and on them sat five or six people, also in grey robes, each one stiller than the last, still as the hall's own pillars. These were the abbey's elders, who usually sat in seclusion on the back of the mountain and in three years would come down on this one day only. When the stone lit muddy, they didn't lift their eyelids; when it lit clean, someone would put down a teacup. A moment ago a child with a double spirit root, metal and water, two clean colours, had just taken his hand away when the first elder on the left, in an ochre robe, spoke up: "This one, put him down under my name." The child stood stunned for a long while before someone pushed him over to the cushion to knock his head on the floor.

Xiaoman inched forward and counted as he went: thirty-one people ahead of him, thirty-two places left in the register. Enough; with him, just enough. When he'd inched up to twenty left ahead, there was a shove from behind and a Ma servant squeezed Ma Xiaobao in front of him. Xiaoman counted again: twenty-one ahead, twenty-one places. He himself was the twenty-second. Sun Ergou, not far ahead of Xiaoman, turned round to grin at him and said quietly, "Three steps, one breath." In the second trial he had put the five in the middle "because five's right in the middle of one to nine, same as you put the rice bowl in the middle of the table". The old steward had listened for a long while, said the reasoning was wrong but the idea was right, and given him a stroke. In the third trial he had shut his eyes and charged in, charged to the foot of the old pine and run his head into the tree, raised a lump, and that counted as touching it.

It was Sun Ergou's turn. He put his hand on the stone, and for a long time the testing stone did nothing. After a good while it squeezed out the faintest light: four colours, pale as if seen through a paper window. The steward thought the stone was broken and slapped it twice, but the light got no brighter. The steward sighed and read out, "Metal, wood, fire, earth. Four kinds. False spirit root, and very pale at that." Someone in the hall laughed. Sun Ergou didn't laugh, and he didn't lower his head. He only hitched the bundle bigger than he was a little tighter on his back. In that bundle were three pairs of shoes his mother had sewn for him, each pair bigger than the last, so that he'd have them as he grew, year by year, up on the mountain. The Abbot looked at him for a while and nodded. The steward struck a line.

Third ahead of Xiaoman was Ahe. Her eyes were dark and bright, like two stars reflected in a well. She flicked her two little plaits back over her shoulders and walked up to the stone, but she didn't press it straight away. First she looked down at her hands (the backs were cracked line after line, from a whole autumn guarding cabbages) and wiped them twice on her robe. Only then did she press.

The testing stone lit. It lit only one colour.

Green. At first only a speck deep inside the stone, like the tip of a shoot pushing up through frozen ground on the first day of spring; then the speck swelled outward, filled the stone, swelled out of the stone and filled the hall. The green wasn't muddy and wasn't mixed. It was clean, without a thread of any other colour, so bright that everyone in the hall raised an arm to shield their eyes, and still it was bright, pushing in between their fingers until even under their eyelids it was green. The coolness in the hall turned at once into a different smell: fresh-turned earth, the juice of a snapped blade of grass, as if someone had planted a whole field overnight in this hall where no god had been worshipped for three hundred years.

Xiaoman had seen that sheet of green on the landing that morning. That had been a stone the size of an egg.

There was no sound in the hall at all. The five or six elders on the side cushions had all opened their eyes, nobody saw when. The name-calling steward's mouth was still half open. "In three hundred years this abbey's never tested a single one": he had finished reciting that less than a stick of incense ago, it was still hanging on his lips, he hadn't had time to take it back, and this sheet of green had slapped it straight back into his mouth. His eyes that had never woken up were truly awake this time, awake and staring. But the first thing he did wasn't read it out. It was to turn his face and glance outside the hall door.

There was nothing outside the hall door. Only sunlight, and the old sweeper leaning in the doorway. But Xiaoman knew who he was glancing for. Before dawn, on the landing, a man in white had carried off a child who lit green, and before getting into the carriage he had nodded to this man.

The steward opened his mouth. "Wood... wood..."

Before the word "heavenly" could come out, the man on the prayer cushion stood up. A hundred or so children had been tested over most of the day, and Xuanchenzi hadn't shifted once. When it lit up well he nodded, when it lit up dim he nodded too, like a clay idol in a temple that nods. Now he stood. He came down from beside the stone stand, not fast, the hem of his grey robe brushing the grey bricks without a sound. He came up to Ahe. The sheet of green still hadn't faded, and it turned his mild face green too. He put out a hand and laid it on top of Ahe's head, between the two little yellowish plaits, and said:

"This one, I'll take."

On the side cushions an elder with white eyebrows had already half risen. Hearing that, he slowly sat back down.

When he had said it, the Abbot followed the steward's glance of a moment ago and looked out of the hall door. His voice wasn't loud, and it didn't sound meant for the people in the hall:

"Sunken Cauldron's."

In the doorway, the old sweeper's broom came down on the threshold with a tok. The steward's brush hung over the register and for a long time wouldn't come down. At last, under that line, he wrote two characters: personal disciple. The words were smaller than he usually wrote them. When he'd written them, he struck a line through one of the places as usual. Xiaoman counted again: he was still the twenty-second.

Only then did the hall burst open with a roar. Some counted off "three hundred years" on their fingers; some stood on tiptoe and pushed forward to see how many eyes the girl had. The few at the front who had tested true spirit roots and weak spirit roots, chests puffed out a moment ago, somehow slowly deflated. Ma Xiaobao, two places behind her, forgot to chew halfway through a mouthful of osmanthus cake; it bulged in his cheek, and when he finally swallowed it he choked and craned his neck. He turned to look for his uncle. His uncle wasn't looking at him; he had his head down and was wiping that brush with his sleeve, wiping it over and over. A hundred or so pairs of eyes fell on the red-nosed girl, and the look in them was exactly the look the people on the landing that morning had given the ingot of silver.

The green light had turned Ahe's face green. She tipped her head back and looked at the hand on top of her head, then at that mild face, thought a moment, and spoke. It was the first thing she had said since entering the hall:

"Does becoming your disciple... cost money?"

The whole hall stared, stunned, several mouths hanging open for a long while. Xuanchenzi was stunned for a moment too. He didn't take his hand away, and slowly a small smile came into his mild face. "No." Ahe nodded. Xuanchenzi went back to his cushion and sat down, and she stood beside the cushion, holding the blue cloth notebook in both hands, and shrank half a step back behind the Abbot. Only then did the sheet of green ebb, little by little, back into the stone.

Xiaoman, three behind her, saw it all clearly. He wanted to shout. The word "Ahe" had already rushed up his throat as far as the back of his teeth, and stopped. The whole hall was looking at her, and the steward was looking outside the door. Shout now, and he'd be telling the whole hall: someone knows this girl. She's from Shi'ao Village. This is where her family lives, this is her father's name. He opened his mouth and in the end didn't make a sound. He swallowed the word back down, and it hurt his throat going down.

Last winter, the man in white had come into Shi'ao Village twice, and both times he had stood in the Shen family's yard. On the other side of a fence was old Zhou's house. He had never once looked that way. He was looking for a fox.

It was Ma Xiaobao's turn. The steward looked at the register and said, "A hundred and seven. One left." Ma Xiaobao slapped his hand down on the testing stone, smack, as if he were slapping his own family's table. The stone lit: metal-white, red and yellow, three colours, but muddy, as if filmed with dust. The steward read out, "Metal, fire, earth. Weak spirit root, colour murky." He held out his hand for Ma Xiaobao's entry tag, looked at it (one stroke, one circle, one space still empty) and frowned. The servant behind Ma Xiaobao stepped up and showed the letter of recommendation once more. The steward looked at the signature on the letter (he knew that signature), then looked at the Abbot. The Abbot said nothing. So the steward picked up his brush and added a stroke in the empty space himself, saying, "The letter's here. This space counts as made up." Then he wrote a word in the register and struck out the last place. "A hundred and eight. The places are full. The rest don't need testing."

That left only Xiaoman. He didn't go. He worked it out in his head: don't get tested, and the three strokes on his tag were three strokes for nothing; get tested, and even if he wasn't taken, there'd be a line in the register with Shen Xiaoman on it. He said, "Steward, I've got three strokes. I'll be tested." The steward looked at the tag in his hand and pointed his chin. "Go on, then. Then go back down the mountain."

As he walked forward, the stone on his mind pressed down again. Not the places: this testing stone in front of him. It tested the spirit qi in a person. Did the one asleep in the tablet at his chest count as something in him? "Hide. Until you can beat everyone, no one is to know about this queen." At once a film of cold sweat broke out on Xiaoman's palms. Through his clothes, he pressed the wooden tablet deeper into his coat.

He put his hand on the testing stone. The testing stone lit, very dimly.

Metal-white, green, black, red, yellow: five colours, not one missing, a little of each, none of them much, none of them bright, crowding each other, leaning on each other, stirred together into a dull grey, like a rag his mother had pieced together out of five kinds of leftover scraps from around the house. The hall went quiet for a moment. Then someone laughed out loud. The loudest was Ma Xiaobao. He rocked back and forth laughing, laughed till the crumbs of osmanthus cake in his hand dropped all over the floor, and pointed at the testing stone as if pointing at the biggest joke in the world. When he laughed, all the children in the hall laughed, one setting off the next, the laughter spreading patch by patch until it struck the faded great beam and fell back down again.

The name-calling steward smiled too, and drawled out, "Metal, wood, water, fire, earth: all five elements. All five kinds, a mixed spirit root." He picked up his brush. "The places are full. Not taken."

The tip of the brush went down towards the "Not taken" column of the register. Xiaoman stood in front of the testing stone with his hand still on it, not taking it off. The three hundred steps he had walked up himself, the Luo Writing he had worked out himself, the longzhi's true head he had heard out himself, and at the end of it, a stone and a brush. He thought of the stick of incense his uncle had sat out on the hundred-and-sixtieth step, and the torn jacket laid over the grass at the edge of the wood. All over the hall, people were laughing or staring; only two weren't laughing. One was the Abbot on his cushion. The other was Ahe. She stood by the Abbot's cushion with her head down and made a mark in her notebook with the charcoal, pressed so hard the charcoal broke.

In his head Xiaoman quickly did the sums. Say nothing and he was out. Speak, and at worst he was out. He took his hand off the testing stone, took the entry tag out of his coat, held it up and said, "Steward." The laughter in the hall died down a little. He said, "This morning, at the top of the steps, the old gentleman said the Sunken Cauldron Abbey takes people without looking at birth, without looking at letters of recommendation; it looks at only three things: legs, head, nerve." He turned the tag over so the steward could see the three strokes. "Three things. My tag's got all of them." He paused, didn't look at Ma Xiaobao, and only said, "That last place: the tag has a circle on it, and one stroke the steward added himself."

The hall went silent at once. After a while, near the front of the line, Sun Ergou, without a word, raised his tag too. Then the barefoot boy in the corner of the hall raised his. He hadn't been taken, and he held it higher than anyone; the three strokes on it he had climbed out of himself, trial by trial. Only those two raised their tags. Everyone else kept their heads down.

The steward's face swelled the colour of pig's liver. He swung the tip of his brush round at Xiaoman and barked, "Insolence! The places are no business of a mixed spirit root. Somebody..."

The brush was an inch from the register when the Abbot spoke. "Wait." He looked at the testing stone, then at Xiaoman. He looked at Xiaoman much longer than he had looked at the stone, so long that Xiaoman's skin began to crawl. Then he turned his head and asked the one leaning by the hall door, "The three trials. What does Elder Qu think?"

Only then did the children in the hall learn that the old sweeper had a name. [Elder Qu]{注=qulao}, leaning on his broom, said just four words: "Steady legs. Careful heart." The few old stewards by the hall's pillars looked at one another. Elder Qu hadn't taken a single disciple in more than forty years; in those forty-odd years he had passed judgement on a child no more than three times all told, and never more than two words at a time.

Xuanchenzi nodded. "Take him. Outer sect." The steward's brush stopped in mid-air. He said, "Abbot, the places..." Xuanchenzi said, "Add one."

The steward opened his mouth and in the end said nothing. He lowered his head and added a line at the end of the register, and after that line he added two small characters: answered back. Then he went round collecting the new disciples' entry tags. When he got to Xiaoman, Elder Qu at the door said, "That one, let him keep it." The steward's hand drew back. When Ma Xiaobao walked past Xiaoman, Elder Qu swept the threshold with his broom, swept it loudly. Ma Xiaobao lowered his voice and drew out his words. "Shen... Xiao... man. A mixed spirit root, through the gate too?" He took a bite of osmanthus cake. "Life up the mountain is lo... ng." Xiaoman drew his words out too, every one the same: "Brother Xiaobao, it's lo... ng."

That night an old disciple, up to relieve himself, passed the main hall and saw the Abbot sitting alone on the prayer cushion beside the empty stone stand, not meditating, not chanting, looking at the stone stand. He sat there until the third watch.

---

At dusk that day the newly admitted disciples were allowed out of the gate to say a word to the families who had brought them, for as long as one stick of incense. The stewards had carried up the weapons rack, and Xiaoman took back his grandfather's bow (third row, seventh from the left, not a finger laid on it), slung it on his back and ran out of the gate.

His uncle was standing by the three-hundredth step, and as soon as he saw him he asked, "...You passed?" Xiaoman said, "Outer sect." His uncle grabbed his arm, and only then saw the blood on his sleeve, and his face went white at once. Xiaoman said, "It's not mine."

He told him what had happened in the wood, and when he got to "Taihua, how far?" he couldn't go on. His uncle didn't make a sound for a long while. At last he said, "...I'll see to it."

Xiaoman said, "Uncle, at the hundred-and-sixtieth step the cliff's carved all over with writing, all of it by people who sat down on that step. I walked up for you." His uncle turned his face away towards the Si River, and only after a good while said, "...Windy." He wiped his face with his sleeve, straightened the bow on Xiaoman's back and said, "Go on back. The incense is out."

After the gate had shut, his uncle found the barefoot boy from under the eaves and said, "The Jishi Hall needs a boy to fetch and run, fill prescriptions. Food and a bed, three hundred coppers a month. Tonight, first help me dig a hole." The two of them went to the edge of the wood to ask for Shidan, and dug under a willow beside the Si River until the middle of the night. His uncle couldn't carve letters, so he wrote two characters on a stone in charcoal: Shidan. At the door of the inn at the foot of the mountain, the Ma servants snapped Ge Liu's short knife in two. Ge Liu picked up the half with the blade and went off north into the dark.

---

On the boys' long communal bed the snoring rose all round. Xiaoman slept in the place nearest the door, with the wind coming through the crack all night. The best place, by the brazier, was Ma Xiaobao's. His servants weren't allowed to stay the night on the mountain and had gone down at dusk, but after dark two disciples three years in, smelling of wine, crouched by his bed and talked in low voices. Xiaoman only caught a few lines. "...a hundred-odd new ones, one stone each..." "...first month, thirty per cent is yours..." "...that mixed root..." Ma Xiaobao's voice: "His, I'll collect myself."

On the place next to Xiaoman's, Sun Ergou wasn't asleep either. He rolled over and whispered, "Tomorrow, that stone: are you handing it over?" Xiaoman said, "Are you?" Sun Ergou thought about it. "I'll hand over the smallest one." With that he rolled back, and soon he was snoring.

Xiaoman took out the notebook his mother had given him, bound in the paper from brown-sugar packets, its cover still faintly sweet. By the moonlight through the crack in the door he opened it to the first page. In it lay the goose feather, and nothing else. His mother had said: it's empty; you write in it yourself. He had no brush, and even if he had, this wasn't something two words could set down. He shut the notebook and recited in his head, word by word: Shidan. Xiaodou. Taihua.

Shidan had asked how far, and he had said not far. How far, he didn't know; that was his line, and it was a debt too.

He took the wooden tablet out from his chest and held it in his palm. The whole day it had gone hot only once, and that was one word: "Left." That word had saved him, and it had also handed that claw to the one behind him. He knew it wasn't her fault, and it wasn't his, but that place in him just wouldn't lie flat. He said quietly to the tablet, "Right now I can't beat him. He's got the letter, he's got people, he's got his family's money. I've got nothing." He stopped, then said, "But... I'm keeping the accounts. All of them."

Outside the window, on the outer sect's notice board, a new notice had been pasted up:

**Outer Sect Common Fund: new disciples, once you have drawn your spirit stones, each hands over one first. Tomorrow at first light, at the door of the dining hall.**

It was signed with three characters: Senior Brother Ma. The one who wrote them had been inside the gate less than half a day.
