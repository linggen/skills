# Now · 3: When the Lights Went Out I Walked Eleven Steps; Then I Trained in Secret for Three Days and Passed a Bottle of Cola

At eleven at night on the twenty-fifth of November, Shen Mang looked at a toilet bowl of cola-coloured urine, and his first thought wasn't "I'm going to die." It was: Ahe mustn't find out.

He had said that to himself once already, four days earlier.

---

Saturday, the fourteenth of November. Inter-department cup, second round of the group stage: Acupuncture and Tuina against Nursing.

Halfway through the second quarter, behind the scorer's table, Ahe turned a page of her notebook and said something to the captain, Xing Bin. Xing Bin waved at the bench: "Number Zero, you're on."

Those five minutes on court were time Ahe had asked the captain for, on his behalf.

When Shen Mang stood up his legs were jelly; once he was on court, they weren't. The Nursing players were all taller than the last, but the way they played, they were more scared of the ball than he was. For the first two minutes he ran after the ball, wherever the ball went he went, like a dog chasing its own tail. In the third minute, Nursing's tallest guy shot from near the free-throw line, the ball hit the back of the rim and bounced up high.

A crowd under the basket all jumped at once. Shen Mang jumped too. He didn't know where the nerve came from, jumping with a bunch of guys half a head taller than him, both hands reaching for the sky, and the ball actually landed in his hands. He hugged it to his chest.

It was the first rebound he'd ever won in the inter-department cup.

When he came down, his right foot didn't land on the floor. It landed on the big guy's foot. His ankle rolled outwards, *crack*; he heard it himself.

Only then did the pain come, so bad everything went white, and he sat down on the floor still holding the ball. He kept it clamped to his chest and wouldn't let go no matter who tried to prise it off him. Pain or no pain, something in his chest was burning, burning so hard tears pushed up: he'd got it. The first rebound of the inter-department cup was Number Zero's. He looked up towards the scorer's table. Ahe had her head down, writing, not looking at him.

Acupuncture and Tuina beat Nursing by nine that day. He didn't see the final whistle. Ahe had two of the subs hold him up and take him straight to the campus clinic by the main gate, and she followed behind with her notebook under her arm.

The clinic doctor squeezed his ankle, pressed along the edge of the outer ankle bone, and had him walk four steps holding on to the wall: "The bone doesn't hurt much when I press, so no X-ray for now. You've sprained the ligament on the outside of the ankle." He prescribed an ankle brace and a pair of crutches. Shen Mang asked: "How many days till it's better?" The doctor said: "It'll stop hurting in a week or two. For it to heal properly, three months at least."

He only heard the first half: the third round was a week away, and if it didn't hurt, he could play.

Ahe heard the second half. She asked the doctor: "Ice it for the first few days or not? How many days before he can put weight on it? Does it need an X-ray?" When she'd asked everything, she wrote a line in her notebook. Outside the clinic, Shen Mang caught up with her on his crutches: "The third round—"

"The doctor said three months," said Ahe. "I've already written it in my notebook."

He wanted to ask what she'd written, but his crutch skidded on the step first.

---

Tuesday morning, the seventeenth of November. Third floor of the lab building, acupuncture practical.

Teacher Yan's white coat was buttoned to the top button as always. The timetable said it plainly: "Acupoint location and filiform needle technique practice (Zusanli)." In pairs: first find the point on your own leg; no needling people, needle paper pads and cotton balls instead, to train finger strength and insertion; and at the end Teacher Yan would choose a volunteer and needle Zusanli on their leg once, as a demonstration.

Shen Mang came in on his crutches with an idea in his head. Wasn't this exactly what acupuncture treated? On the sign outside the tuina department at the campus clinic it said "ankle sprains," clear as day. Today, when Teacher Yan asked for a volunteer, his hand would be first up; once the needle went in, maybe it'd clear his ankle too, and he could play on Saturday.

He was paired with Old Cai.

Teacher Yan did location first: from the hollow on the outer side below the kneecap, three cun down, one finger's width out from the front edge of the shin bone. The whole class bent over feeling their own legs, a chorus of "Ow." Shen Mang couldn't memorise books, but this he found: he pressed his fingers to the strip of muscle along the outside of his shin, flexed his toes up, and the muscle bulged under his fingers. In anatomy class this muscle had a name: tibialis anterior. The point sat right on this muscle, with a nerve and a blood vessel running past deep beside it. It suddenly struck him that these points hadn't all just dropped out of the sky: a lot of them sat where nerves and vessels passed, or in the gaps between muscles. Things a person could reach out and feel.

"Zusanli, the He-Sea point of the Stomach meridian." Teacher Yan tapped the leg on the blackboard with his chalk. "From the fingertips and toes up to the elbows and knees, each meridian has five key points in a row: Jing-Well, Ying-Spring, Shu-Stream, Jing-River, He-Sea. Like water bubbling up from a spring, running into a little ditch, gathering into a river, and finally entering the sea. He is where it enters the sea. Why they're arranged like that, you'll get in detail next term in Meridians and Acupoints."

Old Cai looked down at his own calf: "I've got a sea in my leg?"

The class laughed. Teacher Yan didn't laugh, didn't scold either. He handed out needles. The paper pads were stacks of rough paper bound tight with thread; you held the needle by the handle and fed it in, twirl by twirl. Once through, you switched to a cotton ball, to practise inserting fast and straight. Opposite him, Old Cai held a needle with a hand shaking like it was making the tip dance; he twirled for ages and the needle bent into a bow on the paper pad. Shen Mang's needle went in straight: he'd spent a month scrolling needling videos on his phone, and his hands had a bit of the look.

At the end, Teacher Yan said: "Who'll volunteer?"

Shen Mang's hand was the first up. He sat on the lab bed on his crutches' strength and rolled up his trouser leg. Teacher Yan swabbed, and the needle was in, too fast for him to see. His leg went sore, then full, and an indescribable tingle ran down his calf.

"That's getting the qi," Teacher Yan told the class. "Sore, full, tingling, travelling downwards. That's what needling is after."

Old Cai's eyes were shining beside him: "Busy! What's it feel like?"

"Sore. Sore and full."

The second hand up was Old Cai's. He said he wanted to experience it in person too. He sat up on the lab bed properly, and the instant Teacher Yan's needle touched the skin, Old Cai's face went white first, a layer of cold sweat on his forehead, his body went limp and he toppled sideways. Teacher Yan was there in one stride, laid him flat, propped his legs up, opened the window and sent someone for sugar water. After a few minutes Old Cai's colour came back. He lay at the edge of the bed and said weakly: "...I didn't have breakfast."

"That's needle fainting," Teacher Yan told the class. "Empty stomach, tired, nervous: that's when it's most likely. So eat before you're needled, and lying down is safer than sitting up." That was from the book, and he recited it word for word; then he added one thing the book didn't say: "Same when you needle patients later on."

Before the end of class, Shen Mang shuffled up to the lectern on his crutches, rolled up his trouser leg, and showed an ankle swollen like a risen steamed bun: "Teacher Yan, if you put a couple of needles in here, could I play on Saturday?"

Teacher Yan looked at it a while and didn't answer straight away. He turned round and wrote a line on the blackboard: "[What is called a node is where the spirit qi travels, goes out and comes in; it is not skin, flesh, sinew or bone.]{典=lingshu-jiuzhen}" When he'd written it he tapped it with the chalk and read it out: "The first chapter of the *Lingshu*. 'Node' is what later came to be called a point. The ancients said it isn't skin, flesh, sinew or bone; it's the place where spirit qi goes out and comes in. What spirit qi is, the book doesn't say, and I won't say it for the book."

Shen Mang's fingers were still pressing his calf. The book said it wasn't flesh; under his fingers it was flesh, plain as anything.

"Needling does help with some kinds of pain," said Teacher Yan, closing the textbook. "But people have compared real needles with fake ones that don't go in, and often the difference isn't big. Why it works, there are several explanations, and none of them is settled." He looked at his watch; ten minutes to the end of class. "Get up here. I'll needle it for them to see."

The whole class crowded round in a rush. Shen Mang lay on the lab bed with his trouser leg rolled to the knee. Teacher Yan didn't touch the ankle. He picked up his left hand first.

"It's the right foot that's sprained, so I needle the left hand first." He pressed with his thumb in the middle of the crease across the back of Shen Mang's wrist. "Yangchi. A point on the Hand Shaoyang meridian. The wrist answers to the ankle: this joint on the hand corresponds to that one on the foot. The old rule is 'left illness, treat the right': one needle far away first, and leave the painful place alone for now." Swab, one needle in, and Shen Mang hissed. "Sore?" "Sore. Pretty sore." "Leave it sore. Now turn your right ankle in slow circles. No turning fast. Stop if it hurts."

Shen Mang lay there with the swollen ankle propped up, turning it round and round. The first few turns were like twisting a rusty lock; by the seventh or eighth, the stiffness inside the lock eased a tiny bit. He felt it himself, and it probably showed on his face too. Teacher Yan nodded: "Keeping the needle in while you move, that's called the movement needling method. That's all the effect is, a tiny bit. It's not a miracle cure."

The needle came out. Only then did Teacher Yan bend over the ankle, measuring round the swelling with two fingers: "In front of and below the outer ankle bone, where the swelling's highest: that's the anterior talofibular ligament. Eight sprains in ten, that's the one torn. While it's swollen, we don't needle into it. We needle round it." He placed the needles as he talked, all shallow, a couple of twirls and stop: one in the hollow in front of and below the outer ankle bone, "Qiuxu"; one in the dip behind the outer ankle bone and in front of the Achilles tendon, "Kunlun"; one right under the outer ankle bone, "Shenmai"; one on the crease at the front of the ankle, between the two tendons, "Jiexi". "If we weren't racing the bell, there'd be one more three cun above the tip of the outer ankle bone, Xuanzhong. Four needles round the swelling, light hand, neither tonifying nor draining."

Shen Mang bared his teeth, sweat on his forehead. Old Cai said quietly from the crowd: "Busy, your ankle looks like a cactus right now."

Teacher Yan didn't look up: "A cactus doesn't hurt. He does."

During the few minutes the needles stayed in, he said to the ring of people round him: "Needles can bring down swelling, ease pain, help the blood in that area move a bit." He pulled the needles out and pressed cotton wool on. "They can't make this ligament grow back one day faster. Pain and injury are two separate accounts. That sinew of yours has to grow back by itself. Don't play in Saturday's game."

Shen Mang sat up on the bed and tried a circle with his ankle. It was a bit looser than before he got on the bed, and it hurt a bit less. It was the first time in his life Teacher Yan had needled him; the needling was real, the ankle really had loosened, and still what Teacher Yan said was the same old thing: your idea won't work.

---

That evening Ahe left her student card with the dorm auntie on the ground floor and came up to 617 on the sixth floor to bring bandages.

She put the bandages on the desk and looked at his ankle first: "The swelling's gone down a bit."

"Teacher Yan needled it."

"Needling can bring swelling down," said Ahe. "How many months did the doctor say?"

"Three. But he said it'd stop hurting in a week or two."

"Not hurting isn't the same as healed," said Ahe. "Ligaments grow slowly. Much slower than muscle." She lifted one foot, closed her eyes, and stood on one leg without the slightest wobble, like a post nailed into the concrete floor. "Everyone who's sprained an ankle has to practise this."

"Standing makes it grow?"

"Standing doesn't train the ligament. It trains the wires." Ahe opened her eyes. "Your ankle has a load of little messengers in it, telling your brain non-stop which way your foot's tilting and by how much. A sprain snaps a batch of those wires too. With the wires snapped, your brain doesn't know where your foot is, and that's why an ankle that's been sprained once is the easiest to sprain again. Stand with your eyes shut, and your brain can't see; it can only listen to the wires in your foot. Listen enough and it learns to listen again. Every day, stand on one leg with your eyes shut, until you can do a minute without wobbling."

He tried it once, holding the edge of the desk. The moment he shut his eyes, his whole body was a bamboo pole stuck in mud, both arms flailing, and on the third second his good foot slapped down flat on the floor.

"Three seconds," said Ahe.

Old Cai stuck his head out from the top bunk: "I was counting. Three seconds, not one more."

"The third round on Saturday," said Ahe, tucking her notebook under her arm. "Stand steady for thirty seconds and then we'll talk." She went to the door and looked back. "I've written a line in my notebook."

She didn't say what it was. Shen Mang didn't dare ask either.

---

Thursday, the nineteenth of November. At noon the injury list was posted in the team group chat. Under it, a row of "Got it"; Xing Bin replied "Noted," Big Liu replied with a thumbs-up. Shen Mang looked at it for ages, then replied "Got it" too.

He didn't want to sit at the side of the court for afternoon training and watch everyone run, everyone who passed asking "How's the foot?", and every time someone asked he'd have to smile. He went to the Rare Books Department on his crutches. He'd glanced at the power-cut notice at the library entrance, and all he'd taken in was: 9 p.m.

He asked Granny Ge to bring out the September case of *On Nourishing Life* and lay it open on the table; he wanted to look at those pages on breathing out again. Granny Ge had to go into town to the hospital at three today to collect her medicine, and as she looked for the book she reminded young Liu, the librarian minding the reading room: closes at half past four, lock up, don't forget.

There was one other person in the Rare Books Department.

Ma Xiaobao sat behind the table at the very back, six foot four crammed into an old wooden chair like a bear sitting in a teacup. In front of him lay open a thread-bound materia medica, and he gripped a pencil (only pencils were allowed in the Rare Books Department), copying stroke by stroke, sweating buckets.

"What are you looking at?" he said without looking up. "Teacher's punishing me. Made me come and copy."

His midterm report for History of Chinese Materia Medica had been generated by software, and the teacher had spotted it at a glance. The teacher didn't punish him any other way; just sent him to the Rare Books Department to copy out twenty medicines by hand from the rare editions, three times each, not one character wrong.

"Every medicine three times!" Ma Xiaobao weighed the pencil in his palm. "Thirty-six pharmacies in my family. Which medicine haven't I seen?"

At the table by the door Granny Ge pulled her reading glasses down and said unhurriedly: "Having seen something and knowing it are two different things. If you know the medicines in your family's pharmacies one by one, they won't cheat you one by one." With that she picked up her bag and left.

Ma Xiaobao went quiet and bent to his copying again. Two tables apart, neither paid the other any attention. The only sound in the reading room was the climate unit humming, even as someone chanting scripture next door. After a long time Ma Xiaobao suddenly said, without looking up: "How's the foot?"

"Nearly stopped hurting. Getting it properly better's going to take a few months."

Ma Xiaobao's pencil paused: "I sprained mine in Year Eleven. Back on court in three weeks. Then the same foot went twice more." He went on copying. "Don't do what I did."

It was the first thing Ma Xiaobao had said since Shen Mang had known him that wasn't scary.

At twenty past four he propped his bad foot on another chair, laid a book open on his chest, and fell asleep in the corner at the very back. The shelves hid him; looking in from the door, all you could see was a row of books. At half past four young Liu called "Closing now!" from the door, and nobody answered; she poked her head in and glanced round, switched off the lights, pulled the fire door shut, and then locked the iron gate outside with a *click*. The key she took home.

---

The cold woke him.

The reading room had gone half dark. Only the green exit sign was lit, and a little grey-blue daylight through the skylight under the ceiling. He took out his phone: ten past five, no signal, and no Wi-Fi either; they'd switched off the router along with everything else at closing. He limped to the door and pushed the fire door open; the iron gate outside was locked solid. He clung to the bars and shouted down the corridor a couple of times. Only the echo.

Behind him, a *whoosh* of flushing. The toilet door opened, and out came a six-foot-four person with earphones round his neck, who saw him and froze too.

"...What are you doing here?" they both said at once.

Ma Xiaobao had spent forty-odd minutes in the toilet with his earphones in (he said he'd been "sorting out his ideas"), one song after another, and hadn't heard anyone call closing at all.

The two of them stood at the iron gate for a while and went through every idea they could think of: no phone signal; shouting, nobody answered; the gate would rattle but not open; and at this end of the basement level, the Rare Books Department was the only room. Ma Xiaobao wasn't flustered. He switched the lights on, took his backpack out of the bag locker, found a protein bar, broke it and held out half: "Fine. Sit it out overnight. No big deal. When the library opens in the morning, the librarian's got to come and unlock." He glanced at the lights overhead and asked casually, "They don't switch the lights off here at night, do they?"

Shen Mang thought of the notice at the library entrance, of the few words he'd glanced at and not taken to heart.

"Nine o'clock," he said. "Power cut for the whole library."

The half protein bar in Ma Xiaobao's hand stopped in mid-air.

---

The hours before nine, the two of them actually spent not too badly.

Ma Xiaobao finished the first copy of all twenty medicines. Shen Mang limped round and round between the bookshelves; on his seventh lap he was told: "Stop going round, you're making me dizzy." So they talked across a table.

Ma Xiaobao started: "Hey, you come here every day. What are you actually looking for?"

Shen Mang's heart jumped. What he was looking for was secret manuals, and that he'd never say if they killed him. "It's cool in here," he said. "And quiet."

"It's November. Still cool?" Ma Xiaobao snorted, but didn't press it. He pushed his half-copied notebook across. "Then test me. I've been copying all afternoon and I don't know if any of it went in."

Shen Mang pointed at random: "Liquorice."

Ma Xiaobao snatched the notebook back and turned the page over: "Not that one."

"Why?"

"I've copied it most." He said it fast, one hand pressed on that page, pressed till his nails went white. Shen Mang didn't think anything of it and pointed at another; Ma Xiaobao recited two lines, got stuck, scratched his head for ages, swore at himself, and bent to his copying again.

Ma Xiaobao eyed the foot propped on the chair: "Every day at the side of the court you're doing the golden-rooster stand, eyes shut, wobbling about. Ahe teach you that?"

"Mm, she did. The wires in your foot snap, and you have to reconnect them."

"Nobody taught me that in Year Eleven," said Ma Xiaobao. "The coach just said, when it stops hurting, play." He tapped the pencil on the table twice. "Which is why that foot of mine still knows when it's going to rain."

Later he talked about his grandfather, who in the nineties had a medicine stall at the wholesale market in the western suburbs, his hands cracked from the cold in winter, wrapped round and round with tape; a little steelyard, and a qian was a qian. He talked about his family's thirty-six shops, his dad going round them one by one, never home all year. Shen Mang had nothing to say, so he listened. Talking, they both fell silent.

At ten to nine Ma Xiaobao started checking the time, again and again. At five to nine he went to the bag locker and tipped everything out of his backpack onto the top of the locker, looking for a power bank, and didn't find one. Among the things that came out were two little bottles, one white, one brown, covered in foreign writing; he swept them back into the backpack in one go. His phone had nine per cent left. Shen Mang's had died that afternoon and switched itself off.

At nine on the dot the reading room went *mmm*, the climate unit stopped, and the lights went out.

The dark didn't come all at once. The green exit sign was still lit, running on its battery inside, one small green glowing patch, turning both their faces green. In that thin green light Shen Mang saw Ma Xiaobao sitting in his chair, gripping his knees with both hands, not moving, eyes fixed on the green sign as if afraid it would run off if he blinked.

"How long will that stay on?" Ma Xiaobao asked. His voice was ordinary, but ordinary with a bit of effort.

"No idea. An hour or two?"

At twenty past ten Ma Xiaobao said he was going to the toilet. He went with his phone torch on and shut the door behind him. At half past ten the green sign flickered twice and went out.

The basement had no windows; eyes open or shut, it was the same. The line of phone light under the door was gone too: Ma Xiaobao's phone had died as well. Shen Mang's back went cold first. This dark had weight; it pressed in layer by layer, pressed until his chest was tight and his palms were all sweat. He was scared too. After a few seconds of silence, a voice came from behind the toilet door, so hoarse it didn't sound like Ma Xiaobao's: "...Shen Mang?"

Then panting, breath chasing breath, faster and faster: "My hands have gone numb... my mouth's gone numb... am I dying..."

Six-foot-four Ma Xiaobao, shut up in the dark, was terrified. The more scared he was the more he panted, the more he panted the more numb his hands and mouth went, the more numb they went the more scared he was. He couldn't stop panting.

Shen Mang's own fear was still there. But behind that door was someone more scared than he was.

---

Shen Mang stood up. His crutch had slid under the table; he felt around twice and didn't find it. From here to the toilet he had to get round two rows of shelves and a table, with all Ma Xiaobao's tipped-out stuff on the floor in between.

That right foot had been sprained five days ago.

Every day these last few days he'd stood on one leg in the dorm with his eyes shut, holding the edge of the desk, while Old Cai counted from the top bunk: three seconds, six seconds, eleven seconds. This morning, fifteen. Eyes shut, the brain can't see, and can only listen to the wires in the foot.

Now, eyes shut or open, it made no difference.

Holding the edge of the table, he took the first step. His right foot came down and the ankle tilted outwards a little. He felt it. The wire reported that tilt, the leg drew itself back, and he was steady. Second step. On the third, the top of his foot kicked Ma Xiaobao's flask, and it rolled away clattering. Fourth step, his hand found the cold metal edge of a bookshelf. He edged along the shelf, a step, a step, counting in his head. From the toilet end, the panting got faster and faster, like someone drowning in the dark.

Ninth step, he touched the corner of the table. Tenth, he went round it. Eleventh, his hand found the handle of the toilet door.

He stood steady. His right foot was on the ground, the ankle aching in waves, but it hadn't tilted, hadn't given. These eleven steps he hadn't walked with his eyes: eyes were no use at all down here, and there were no eyes watching him either. What he'd walked on was the wires in his foot. In the dark he split into a grin and shouted without a sound:

Done it!

He pushed the door open, and the panting was right at his feet. Ma Xiaobao was huddled between the toilet and the wall, hugging his knees. Not that anything could be seen: Shen Mang crouched down feeling along the wall, and could hear him shaking so hard his teeth were knocking. Something rolled up against his hand, a little bottle with its cap off and something rattling inside. Shen Mang didn't touch him: "Brother Xiaobao. Breathe with me. Out longer than in." He breathed in for four counts, then out for six, breathing out so it could be heard in the dark. Ma Xiaobao couldn't keep up; halfway through breathing out he'd snatch another breath in. Shen Mang didn't hurry him, led him one breath at a time, like leading a child just learning to walk. Ten breaths, twenty. After thirty-odd, Ma Xiaobao said hoarsely: "...My hands aren't numb any more. I thought I wasn't getting enough air, so I kept breathing harder."

"You were breathing too much. You blew all the carbon dioxide off, that's why your hands went numb," said Shen Mang. "Breathe out slower and it builds back up."

He found Ma Xiaobao's wrist and, the way Ahe had taught him, laid two fingers on it and counted. The hands weren't numb now, but the heart was still racing, too fast to count; and racing along, every so often it skipped, like missing a stair going down, and then raced on. He got hold of the little bottle. He couldn't see the writing on it, but he knew the size: the white one, tipped out on top of the locker at five to nine. He held it a while, then put it back by Ma Xiaobao's foot.

The two of them just sat like that, one inside the door and one outside, backs against the door frame. Later, in the dark, Ma Xiaobao said that when he was little, playing hide-and-seek, he'd climbed into a big wooden cabinet of liquorice in his grandfather's warehouse, and the cabinet door had latched from outside. It was New Year, the warehouse was locked, everyone had gone home, and he'd spent the night in there. "Next morning when my grandad opened the cabinet, I smelled of liquorice all over. My dad said, it's nothing."

Shen Mang thought of the "liquorice" page, and the fingernails pressed white.

After a long time Ma Xiaobao said: "What happened today—"

"What happened today?" said Shen Mang. "It's this dark. I didn't see a thing."

In the dark, Ma Xiaobao let out a snort of a laugh, his nose thick. Shen Mang didn't say anything more. What he'd said he hadn't seen wasn't only Ma Xiaobao being scared of the dark. It was also that white bottle.

---

At six the lights came on. The climate unit started up again, *mmm*. Shen Mang, leaning against the door frame, was woken by the glare, and saw Ma Xiaobao curled up asleep on the floor, six foot four, sleeping like a prawn. The white bottle on the floor was gone.

On the table the case of *On Nourishing Life* still lay open; he'd fallen asleep yesterday afternoon and nobody had put it away. In the margin of the page on breathing out were a few lines in pencil, small and hurried. The top line: "Tried it. Breathe out longer than in, and the heart does slow." Breathe out longer than in and the heart slows down: what Ahe had taught him in October, someone had tried at this very table decades ago. He took the borrowing card out of the case and laid it alongside: the pencil in the margin and the water-blurred name he'd seen on the card in September were the same hand. Further on, the pencil came only here and there, and stopped altogether a bit more than halfway through; after that the pages were clean, as if one day the writer had closed the book and walked out and never come back.

At twenty past six, keys jangled in the corridor. A security guard with a torch saw two people standing inside the iron gate and nearly dropped the torch.

---

Saturday, the twenty-first of November. Third round of the group stage. On the bench was a row of paper cups, mouths down, numbers written on the bottoms. Ahe's rule: whoever had been on court turned their cup over when they came off for a drink. Shen Mang sat in the stands on his crutches and counted twice: eleven cups in all. His, the "0", hadn't been put out.

Acupuncture and Tuina won, top of Group A, which meant the last four, which meant there'd still be an Acupuncture and Tuina team next year. Behind the scorer's table Ahe shut her notebook and pressed both hands on it, pressed for quite a while, before she opened it again. On the next half-court the Chinese Pharmacy school won too, top of Group B. As the crowd broke up, Ma Xiaobao wandered over and tapped Shen Mang's crutch with his shaker bottle: "Number Zero. A team has to register twelve. One short and you can't enter. You're just there to make up the numbers." Neither of them had mentioned the night in the Rare Books Department. Ma Xiaobao was even more offhand with him than before, as if to make up for that night.

Not one word of it was wrong, which was why it hurt so much. The Acupuncture and Tuina entry form: at twenty to midnight on the night the list was due, Ahe had been one short, and only then put his ID number on it. He gripped his crutch until his knuckles went white, and still had to smile. The thing that had burned in his chest when he grabbed that rebound got doused by those words, *make up the numbers*, and went out with a hiss.

But when the hurt had passed, something in him quietly eased: someone making up the numbers, nobody watches; doesn't have to step up to the free-throw line; doesn't have hundreds of eyes all staring at once. That easing scared him, and he quickly pushed it back down, and told nobody.

That night Old Cai's phone dangled down from the top bunk, the screen stopped on a line: "The youth went into secluded cultivation for three days, and came out having broken through two realms in a row."

He stared at that line a long time. A cave he didn't have. Three days, he did.

He rolled over, held his phone up to his face, and said to it: "How do you build muscle fast?"

A voice from the phone answered him, unhurried, as if one more person were sitting in the dorm: "You can't do it fast. Muscle grows while you rest after training. When you're starting out, don't add more than a tenth on top of last week's load each week. And when you wake up in the morning, take your pulse lying down. If it's a good bit higher than usual, don't train that day. Rest."

The top bunk creaked, and Old Cai's head hung down upside down. Specs in the opposite bed sat up too: "Who's that? Who's talking?"

"It is," said Shen Mang, and held the phone up for them to see.

Old Cai reached down and scooped the phone up, squinted at the icon, and read out the name: "Linggen." He cleared his throat and put on a storyteller's voice: "Linggen, the spirit root: the root of all cultivation. A top-grade spirit root, a thousand li a day; a middle-grade spirit root, ten years to Foundation Establishment; a low-grade spirit root, a hundred years of seclusion and you come out still first layer of Qi Condensation, and you've gone through three masters." When he'd finished he turned the phone over and over. "Making a spirit root into an app, that's way too cool~"

Specs pushed up his glasses: "That model works here, in China?"

"It works. My uncle in Canada made it," said Shen Mang. "He gave me a share of his own model in the app."

Old Cai had already swiped open the home page and pulled down: Health, Budget, and a row of tiles. His finger stopped on "Budget": "Does it know how many boxes of instant noodles you've had this month?" Shen Mang reached up and snatched the phone back.

He pushed that page up out of the way, didn't close it, and lay the phone face down on his chest. A tenth a week: by the semi-final he couldn't even add one tenth.

The semi-final was on the twenty-eighth. Ahe had given him only one exercise: stand on one leg with your eyes shut. This week he'd gone from three seconds to twenty-something; Ahe said thirty seconds steady and the wires were reconnected. But what he wanted her to see couldn't just be one steady foot. One steady foot was still someone making up the numbers. So he gave himself a "three days' seclusion" too: seven in the evening, up to the gym on the second floor, train till it closed at ten; no jumping on the ankle, everything else trained. The card he'd got at Mid-Autumn hadn't expired; at that hour the gym held only a few postgrad-exam students walking on the treadmills, and nobody knew him. And one more rule: Ahe mustn't find out.

---

The first day, Monday the twenty-third of November. The gym faced north; at seven in the evening it was pitch dark beyond the windows, and the glass reflected a row of machines and himself.

He warmed up with the empty twenty-kilo bar, then went up to forty, squats, sets of ten. The first few sets went smoothly: the bar pressing on his shoulders, down into the squat, up to standing, and the legs of the person in the mirror looked the part. Twelve sets of squats: a hundred and twenty. Then bench press, thirty kilos, six sets. Back at National Day he'd blacked out on this bench once; this time he didn't hold his breath: in, brace, push, out, pushing by the book. Pull-ups, he managed three and then hung there stuck, so he put on a resistance band and carried on. Seated shoulder press, dumbbell curls, dips, each one until he couldn't lift. He kept count of his sets in his phone's notes app, each line longer than the last. The gym closed at ten and he was the last one out, his legs like two bags of wet sand; going down the stairs, he held the rail at every step.

But he felt full inside. He thought: now this is training. On the day of the semi-final he'd make her look up, just once. Not at someone making up the numbers.

The next morning when the alarm went, he tried to roll over and couldn't. His thighs were stiff as fried dough sticks left out overnight, like they'd snap if they bent; he couldn't squat on the toilet and had to hold the wall; he couldn't raise his arms above his head, so he got halfway through washing his hair and the suds dried behind his ears; he couldn't straighten his elbows, and every move pulled at his chest and back.

Going down to the canteen at noon (six floors, no lift), he went down backwards: face up, hands on the rail, backing down one step at a time like a crab afraid of falling. Specs followed behind watching for ages: "Busy, are you playing yourself in reverse?"

"I'm going against the flow," said Shen Mang. "All the masters come downstairs like this."

At team training that evening he sat on his crutches at the side of the court as usual, standing on one leg, writing letters in the air with his toes. Ahe came past with her notebook and stopped: "What's wrong with your legs? Ankles don't make you walk like that."

"Nothing. I'm fine."

"Going up steps, both your legs are buckling."

His brain spun fast: "The ankle brace is too tight."

Ahe looked down at his feet, looked a long while, said nothing, wrote a line in her notebook, and walked off. Shen Mang's back was soaked with sweat.

He did have an account in his head. When she said don't train, what she was worried about was the ankle; he hadn't jumped, hadn't used the ankle once. She'd taught him in October that muscle grows while you rest, train every other day: he remembered that. But he'd worked it out: every other day, he'd only get three sessions in a week. Not enough time. Besides, pain means it's growing. He didn't know where he'd read that, but remembering it now, every word sounded true. He went over this account in his head back and forth, and every item held up.

He went again that night. Squats still at forty kilos, sets of ten; by a hundred his legs were shaking. He gritted his teeth and counted set by set to three hundred, the last few sets ugly, his back rounding first; then the upper-body exercises all over again, and one round extra. The line in his notes app was a good bit longer than the first day's.

At six on the third morning he took his resting pulse lying down and, as agreed, sent it to Ahe: eighty-eight. Usually it was around seventy. He looked at it and was actually rather pleased: heart beating fast, must be strong blood and qi. His legs were jelly; going downstairs he had to rest twice.

She didn't reply during the day. He thought: no reply means it's fine.

At twenty to eight that evening, on his two-hundredth squat, his phone buzzed:

"88? Don't train today."

He stood holding his phone under the squat rack, the bar still on the rack. What she'd said was don't train today. She didn't know what he was training; she thought he was in the dorm, standing on one leg. If he stopped now, these three days would be two and a half; a two-and-a-half-day seclusion, you never saw that even in the novels. He thought again of that line in her notebook, whatever it said. He thought a while, and replied with three words:

"OK, I won't."

Those three words went off, and before the phone was even back in his pocket, something in him sank, like swallowing an egg with the shell still on. He didn't look at it. He shouldered the bar and did the remaining hundred. The sets in his notes for the three days got longer each day.

---

And so there was that toilet bowl at eleven that night.

He stood in the toilet for a minute, and in the end couldn't keep it in. He came out and told his roommates: "My pee is cola-coloured."

Old Cai stuck half his head out from the top bunk: "Did you drink cola tonight?"

"One can. But the colour of this lot is darker than the can I drank."

Specs was already searching, and his face changed bit by bit: "I looked it up. Legs too sore to touch, no strength, cola-coloured urine: rhabdomyolysis. Untrained muscle, load piled on suddenly, especially the lowering, squatting-down part, and the muscle fibres tear one by one (you've torn several big muscles all over your body at once), and the myoglobin inside leaks into your blood; your kidneys filter it out, that's why the urine's cola-coloured, and if they filter too much, the kidneys block. Hospital, now." Old Cai, very solemn: "Qi deviation. He's gone wrong in his cultivation."

Specs rang Ahe while he put his shoes on. Shen Mang lunged for the phone, his legs gave, and he missed.

At twenty to midnight the duty doctor at the campus clinic took one look at his urine cup and, without a word, called a car to send him to the city hospital. Specs sat with him in the back seat; Old Cai came too, hugging his own pillow (in case someone had to sit up all night, he said). The car drove south along the Yi River, the lights on the bank sliding back one by one. Shen Mang leaned on the window, his legs throbbing, and what he was thinking about was something else: now she's going to find out.

One in the morning, A&E. A drip in his arm, a bag of saline overhead falling drop by drop. The doctor read out the test results: "Creatine kinase, forty-two thousand." Shen Mang asked if that was high. The doctor said: "A normal person's is one or two hundred."

The door at the end of the corridor opened. Ahe had come: a long down coat, a strip of pyjama trousers showing beneath it, trainers with no socks, her ponytail crooked. She went up to the trolley bed, looked at the bag of saline, looked at the sheet of results, looked a long time. Then she took out her notebook, wrote a line, and closed it. Not a word.

Shen Mang would rather she'd shouted at him. He said quietly: "Ahe..."

She lifted her eyes to him. He swallowed the rest. The egg with the shell still on, in his chest, only now really dug into him.

A bed wasn't free until four. All that time she sat on the bench in the corridor, wrapped in her down coat, not speaking, not leaving. Old Cai fell asleep beside her hugging his pillow, his head nodding and nodding. As the trolley went into the lift, Shen Mang saw through the gap in the doors that she'd stood up; she watched the lift doors close, and only then turned away. She had class the next morning.

The doctor beside him was writing up the notes and didn't look up: "Admitted, three days on a drip, drink lots of water. For two weeks after discharge, no strenuous exercise."

"The semi-final's on the twenty-eighth."

"On the twenty-eighth, you'll still be lying here."

---

It was a three-bed ward. Bed 17, by the door.

Early on the twenty-sixth, Specs came, and took out of his pocket a dog-eared copy of the *Baopuzi: The Inner Chapters*: "Old Cai says you've gone wrong in your cultivation. I went back and looked. Ge Hong didn't have that phrase in his day, but he's got a line that's more accurate: '[To force up what your strength cannot bear: that is injury.]{典=baopu-jiyan-shang}'"

"But I could lift it. Three hundred of them."

Specs put his finger on the word *force*: "What you could lift was each single one. This chapter counts up a dozen kinds of injury, and the last line is that injuries pile up until there's nothing left. Injury isn't done by one particular rep. It's saved up day by day."

In the afternoon Ahe came after class. She sat at the foot of the bed, pulled an infusion chart from the bedhead, turned it over, and before saying anything drew a horizontal line.

"This is where you started." From the line she drew a curve going down. "After training, you're worse off than before: tiny tears in the muscle, sugar used up, you're tired. Training is digging a pit." The curve climbed slowly back up, past the line, a little bit higher. "Rest well, eat well, sleep well, and your body fills the pit back in, and fills in a bit extra, in case it happens again. That bit is what you've actually grown." She put a dot at the top of that bit. "Next time, train from here. A bit at a time, upwards."

She drew another: down a curve, and before it climbed back to the line, down again; and down, and down, like three steps going down.

"You didn't fill in one pit, and then you dug two more days down." She pointed at the eighty-eight in her notebook. "Your morning pulse a good bit higher is your body telling you: you haven't paid back yesterday's account yet. Coach Lei's run the team twenty years and he says one thing: add load, a tenth a week at most. You added double in a day."

Shen Mang looked at the three steps and didn't make a sound for a long time. Suddenly he understood what that eighty-eight was. That morning he'd looked at it and been pleased, taken it for strong blood and qi; it was his body shouting stop. His body had shouted, and she'd shouted too ("Don't train today"); both of them had shouted, and he'd replied "OK, I won't," shouldered the bar, and done another hundred squats.

Those hundred he hadn't had to do. But he'd done them.

"This line's a rough drawing," said Ahe, folding the infusion chart and slipping it into her notebook. "People in our line of work still argue about how accurate it is. For keeping you in line, it's good enough."

He took out his phone. Linggen was still open, stopped on the page from the night of the twenty-first, the lines it had answered him with, not a word missing: no more than a tenth; morning pulse a good bit higher, rest that day. Coach Lei's line after twenty years with the team, it had said that very night. It had said it, he'd heard it, and having heard it he'd gone and squatted anyway.

Ahe glanced at the screen and said nothing; she only asked: "These three nights, how many hours did you sleep?"

He couldn't answer. He swiped down, to Linggen's health page, the sleep his wristband had recorded: three bars, each shorter than the last, and the one for the twenty-fourth broken in two places in the middle. His legs had hurt; turning over had woken him.

Ahe gave it just one look: "I've written that down too."

In the evening Old Cai and Specs came. Specs carried a stack of paper, his lecture notes from the last two days, copied out again for him in neat print, down to Teacher Yan's blackboard drawings. Old Cai came in hugging something, and all three beds in the ward turned to look: the foot bath his grandma had posted him, the heated kind. "My grandma says people in hospital get cold feet." He shoved the tub under the bed. "The nurses won't let you plug it in. Use cold water." Shen Mang lay in bed laughing, laughing till the needle in his arm wobbled. In the team group chat, Xing Bin sent him two words: "Get well." Big Liu followed with a thumbs-up, exactly like the one under the injury list. He laid the phone face down on his chest and kept it there a long time.

The evening of the twenty-eighth, the semi-final. He lay in the hospital bed watching the livestream on his phone, the needle in his left hand, the phone held up in his right, the sound turned right down. Acupuncture and Tuina against Pharmacy. In the third quarter Xing Bin got cramp and was carried off, and every sub had been on: Shen Mang stared at the phone and his first thought was: if I were there, it'd be my turn. The second thought followed, faster than the first: if I were there, would I even make it on? In the fourth quarter Xing Bin came back on rubbing his calf and opened the gap with a mid-range shot. Acupuncture and Tuina won by four. He let out a "Yes!" in bed, and a nurse stuck her head round the door: "Bed Seventeen!" He ducked back under the covers. The camera panned across the bench: twelve paper cups, and his "0" had been put out too, upside down.

Twelve in all. He wasn't there, and they'd still counted him in. Lying in Bed 17, his chest suddenly swelled full, and his eyes went hot with it; he quickly held the phone over his face, because the next bed was watching.

In the other game, Chinese Pharmacy beat Chinese Medicine by twenty, and Ma Xiaobao scored twenty-eight by himself. After it finished, Ma Xiaobao sent him a photo of the scoreboard: "Number-maker-upper, see you in the final. Lying down doesn't count."

---

Discharged on the morning of the twenty-ninth. He went and paid the bill himself: student medical insurance covered most of it, and the four hundred and something left took his whole November living allowance down to the bottom. At home, he didn't mention a word. Dad's leg was still propped up at home; this month, the family couldn't take one more bill.

Ahe was waiting at the hospital entrance, holding his crutches. He'd left them in 617 the night he was admitted; she'd left her card with the dorm auntie to fetch them. She handed him the crutches, then handed him a sheet of paper with three points on it: morning pulse back below seventy before you touch a ball; once you touch a ball, free throws only; blood test recheck on the sixth of December.

"Free throws count as training?"

"Free throws don't dig a pit."

The two of them stood at the hospital entrance waiting for a car, waited quite a while, and neither spoke. Shen Mang couldn't hold it in: "Ahe, are you still angry?"

Ahe looked across the road: "You training, I'm not angry about. Training till you broke yourself, I'm not angry about either. Everyone has a first time." She paused. "What I'm angry about is that day I told you not to train, and you told me OK."

He opened his mouth, and nothing came out.

"Your numbers, I write down every day," said Ahe. "The numbers are ones you report yourself. If you report the truth and then don't listen, then what is it I'm writing in this notebook?"

The car came. She got in first and didn't look back. Shen Mang got in after her, and all the way he turned those few sentences over and over, and remembered them better than anything the doctor had said.

---

His morning pulse for the first two days after discharge was seventy-six, seventy-two: still above seventy. He wasn't allowed to do anything, only eat, sleep, go to class, lie down; it was the first time he'd learned that resting takes gritted teeth too. At six on the morning of the second of December, 617 still dark, he laid two fingers on his wrist and counted twice against the stopwatch on his phone: sixty-nine. He shot up, banged his head, *thunk*, on the slats of the top bunk, and clutching his head with a grin he said: "Out of seclusion."

Old Cai on the top bunk, woken by the bang, asked groggily: "...What time is it?"

He sent sixty-nine to Ahe. Ahe replied: "Written down. 69."

The afternoon of the seventh of December, the outdoor court, a north wind blowing the net out sideways. He took off his gloves, his ten fingers frozen like ten carrots: two out of ten. At the side of the court Ahe closed her notebook: "Two weeks' rest, your hands are rusty. Rust comes fast, and goes fast too. Two hundred a day, then go back and soak your feet." Outside the wire fence, Ma Xiaobao went past with his kit bag, stopped and watched him take three: one clanged off the rim, one went wide, one didn't even touch the rim. "Number Zero. Is that a free throw or a wish?" "It's a wish." Ma Xiaobao laughed once, went a dozen steps, looked back once more, and said nothing.

That night, the campus confessions wall put up a prediction post for the final: the two teams' twenty-four names, each followed by a percentage. He scrolled to the last line:

"No. 0 Shen Mang (Acupuncture and Tuina) Chance of playing: 1%"

The top comment underneath: "That 1% is so he doesn't cry."

He stared at that "1%" for a long time, and thought it was quite a good number, actually: one time in a hundred meant there was still one time. He screenshotted that line and set it as his phone's lock screen. Every morning after that, lying there taking his pulse, the moment he lit up the screen, that was the first thing he saw.
