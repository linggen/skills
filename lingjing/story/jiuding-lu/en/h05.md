# Chapter Four: Twenty-Three Kicks Taken in the Mud / A Nine-Line Pill Kept in a Bottle

Shen Xiaoman's days in the outer sect of Sunken Cauldron Mountain began with paying up: the money wasn't even warm in his hand before someone reached out to collect it. The tenth day of the ninth month, not yet properly light. The outer sect's few dozen grey-tiled houses were stacked up the east slope tier on tier, and over the ridges of their tiles floated a white haze. It wasn't kitchen smoke. It was the mountain's spirit qi, which sank at night and was driven by the morning wind to flow slowly along the roof ridges. A long line had already formed at the door of the dining hall. A hundred or so new disciples in the grey robes they had drawn only last night; the robes were cut to fit the big ones, so on the small ones the hems dragged over their feet, and from a distance they looked like a flock of grey ducklings just out of the nest.

At the door of the dining hall stood an elm table, and behind it sat the outer sect steward, [Zhou Heng]{注=zhouheng}. Zhou Heng was a tall, thin man of thirty or so with a careworn face. Spread open in front of him was a ledger thick enough to use as a pillow. He called a name, handed out a jujube-wood waist token and three spirit stones. The spirit stones were the size of pigeon eggs, grey-skinned, but a faint light showed through from inside them, like a spark buried in snow. For each person he gave to, Zhou Heng looked up at them once and said, "Promising!" By somewhere past the thirtieth, the word was coming out like a sutra. When it was Xiaoman's turn, he looked at the words "five-element mixed spirit root" in the register, studied Xiaoman for a good while, and said, "...Promising." Without the exclamation mark.

Inside the dining hall door, not ten steps further on, was another table. Behind it sat Ma Xiaobao, with a small gilt-painted wooden box in front of him. On the box was pasted a sheet of red paper with two big characters: **Common Fund**. Behind him stood two disciples three years in, arms folded, like the two clay guardians at a temple gate. The name-calling steward (the one who had nodded to the man in white on the landing, who had added a stroke for Ma Xiaobao in the main hall) was Ma San's cousin, Ma Xiaobao's uncle; overnight, Ma Xiaobao had become "Senior Brother Ma". Zhou Heng glanced at the box and lowered his head to go on handing out. The register was in the name-calling steward's hands. [Sun Ergou]{注=sunergou}, ahead of Xiaoman in the line, still carrying the bundle bigger than he was on his back, held his three spirit stones and looked at them for a long time, then picked out the smallest and, shaking, put it in the box. He was just about to walk on when one of the clay guardians behind stuck out a leg and hooked his heel. Bundle and all, he pitched forward and stumbled three or four steps, lurching, like a reed bent by the wind, bent right down to the dirt, and then sprang back. He didn't fall.

Xiaoman picked one neither big nor small and put it in the box. Ma Xiaobao narrowed his eyes. "Just like that?" Xiaoman said, "It's the rule. Thanks for your trouble, Brother Xiaobao." Ma Xiaobao said, "Call me Senior Brother Ma." Xiaoman said, "Thanks for your trouble, Senior Brother Ma." Ma Xiaobao lifted his chin, satisfied, and an old wooden token at his waist swung. On one side were carved two characters: Night Watch. It was a job nobody in the outer sect would take, and his uncle had handed it to him of all people. Xiaoman was standing close, and a smell suddenly got into his nose: not the sweetness of osmanthus cake, but a green, bitter, grassy rankness, cool, and when he breathed it in, the dry well down in his belly started as if splashed with cold water. That was what Ma Xiaobao brought back on him after a night on watch.

Suddenly someone behind him tapped his shoulder.

Xiaoman turned and saw a girl with two little plaits. Her grey robe was a size bigger than his, the sleeves rolled up three times, the hem gathered with a straw rope at the waist; under her collar showed a line of green. A red nose, frozen like a radish. In her hand she held a little notebook the size of her palm, and a stick of charcoal was tucked behind her ear.

Xiaoman said, "...Ahe. You've been avoiding me a whole day."

Ahe said, "Mm. I was avoiding you."

"How did you get down here? The Abbot's courtyard is at the top of the mountain."

"I walked down," Ahe said. "On the steps, I was the one who rushed past you. The Luo Writing, I had it done half a stick of incense before you." She paused, and said very seriously, "In an exam you don't know anybody. Knowing you would've been a distraction." Xiaoman said, "And now?" Ahe said, "Now the exam's over."

Xiaoman looked at the line of green at her collar, then at the grey robe she had on: outer sect. "What you're wearing..." Ahe tugged her collar up. "Borrowed. Green stands out." She glanced into the dining hall. The name-calling steward sat behind Ma Xiaobao with a tea bowl, his eyes going over the new disciples' faces one by one.

Xiaoman asked, "Why didn't you come with me?"

Ahe said, "My ma said if you go on your own you'll die. If I go on my own I'll die too. If we go together, two die, and that's a bad deal. So we go separately." Xiaoman thought about it, and felt that though it wasn't nice to hear, the sums were clear enough. Ahe licked her charcoal, wrote something else down, and said, "Mixed spirit root."

Xiaoman said, "You're writing that down too?"

Ahe said, "I'm keeping it. The day you're not one any more, we can check the accounts." She turned and went off up the mountain.

Xiaoman watched her two little plaits bob away into the distance and couldn't have said what he felt. On the day he left home, only his mother had been standing under the old locust tree, and he'd thought the girl was sleeping in. In fact she had left a day earlier, by another road. And the road she was on now was another road still: up to the top of the mountain.

Those few days, Xiaoman asked everyone he met the same thing: how far was Mount Taihua? Sun Ergou didn't know. An old disciple in the lecture hall heard him and laughed. "West. Two thousand li or so, in Yong Province. The Taiyi Palace on Mount Taihua. What do you want with it?" Xiaoman didn't say what. Two thousand li or so. What he had told Shidan was, not far.

---

The lecture hall stood east of the main hall, past a stand of old cypresses. A big building three bays wide, grey brick to the roof, grey tiles, its eave corners lifted higher than any other building's, a stone beast crouched on each of the four, their faces worn away by rain. In the middle of the roof ridge sat a little bronze cauldron, palm-sized, mottled with verdigris. Five steps up, worn by three hundred years of shoe soles into a groove down the middle that held water when it rained. Two doors of old elm, unpainted, with a threshold so high you had to lift your leg. Over the doors hung a board, black ground, three characters: Lecture Hall, the gold lacquer flaked down to half.

On the left-hand door hung a whitewashed wooden board: the timetable. The days weren't fixed; the stewards' office changed it in charcoal every few days. This month it said:

**Entry Classes: 3rd, 9th, 16th, 22nd, 28th, hour of the Dragon. New disciples must attend. No absence permitted.**

**Advanced Classes: 11th, 25th, hour of the Goat. Open to fourth layer of Qi Condensation and above.**

**Foundation Classes: one lecture this season, date to be added. Open to seventh layer of Qi Condensation and above.**

Below that was a line in small writing: latecomers stand in the corridor to listen. The new disciples crowded in front of the board to read it. Someone reached "fourth layer and above" and turned to ask, "Is there anybody in this whole room who's on the fourth layer?" Nobody answered.

On the day of the first lecture, the bell had already rung and the new disciples in the hall were still whispering when someone said "Huh?" and pointed out of the window. The cloud halfway up the back of the mountain was being parted by something inside it: a streak of green light came slanting down, not fast, like a leaf drifting down on the wind. As it came close they could see it was a man standing on a sword, his grey robe puffed out by the wind, his two big sleeves streaming behind him, the corners of his robe flapping, the tuft of beard on his chin streaming too. All round him was wrapped a faint white haze, like a wisp of cloud torn off the mountainside, following him. He skimmed over three roof ridges, and of the row of sparrows resting on them not one took fright. Everyone in the hall crowded to the windows; those at the back stood on stools, and Sun Ergou, on tiptoe, rested his chin on Xiaoman's shoulder.

The green light stopped at the door of the lecture hall and scattered. He stepped down from the sword, and his feet touched the ground without a sound; only then did the puffed-out robe slowly fall still. The white haze seeping from between the bricks, which had been flowing lazily along the ground, now all surged to his feet together and swirled once round his shoes like a pack of dogs that had seen their master. He crooked two fingers back towards himself, and the sword gave a soft ring and slid back of its own accord into the scabbard on his back; he walked in through the door with his hands behind him. The whole hall fell silent at once. The few sitting by the door felt the hairs on the backs of their necks stand up first, like opening a door in winter into a blast of cold air. Except it wasn't cold. It was all the spirit qi in the room going to him. Someone, no one knew who, stood up first, and then the whole room stood, stool legs scraping on the brick floor in a long creaking racket. Sun Ergou's mouth hung open for a long while, and at last he said softly, "...An immortal." A girl in the front row held both hands clasped to her chest. Ma Xiaobao in the first row had, without noticing, sat up dead straight.

Sun Ergou held it in for a long while and in the end asked it out loud: "Master Chu, when will we be like you, become immortals, and fly?" Master Chu took a ruler from the lecture table and tapped it twice on his palm, and his goatee twitched. "Become immortals? That's a long way off. But after Foundation Establishment you can ride a sword through the air, and that's not hard. You only need to come to class on time and practise hard when you go back." Every eye in the hall lit up.

This was [Master Chu]{注=chuxiansheng}. They said he was fifty or so, but he looked no more than thirty-odd, without a single line in his face, only a goatee tuft under his chin. He was lean and wiry, and had been lecturing in the lecture hall for thirty years; in this whole courtyard he was the only one who had reached Foundation Establishment. Everyone else had a layer of dust on them; on that grey robe of his, dust wouldn't even settle. Xiaoman, in the back row, stole a look at his own cuffs, then at that grey robe. Standing on a sword, coming down out of the clouds: he had only one thought left. In this life, I'm going to fly like that, just once.

Under the floor of the lecture hall ran a thin vein of spirit qi, and morning and evening a little white haze seeped up between the bricks and flowed along the ground. By the front few rows it was still knee-high; by the back it was only a shadow like a thin frost. The front few rows were where the true spirit roots sat; the back few were where the false spirit roots and mixed spirit roots sat. Nobody had ever made it a rule, but everybody sat that way. Xiaoman sat in the back row with Sun Ergou on his left; Ma Xiaobao, on the strength of his letter, sat in the first row.

Master Chu, hands behind his back, wagged his head as he lectured. "Between heaven and earth, spirit qi is everywhere. Ordinary people cannot take it in. Cultivators can. Why? Because of the spirit root. One kind: a heavenly spirit root. Two kinds: a true spirit root. Three kinds: a weak spirit root. Four kinds: a false spirit root..." His goatee twitched, and his gaze came to rest squarely on Xiaoman. "All five kinds, what you call a mixed spirit root: leaks on every side." The whole hall burst out laughing, and someone beat time on the desk with his fingers: "Leaky ladle! Leaky ladle!" Master Chu called out, "Shen Xiaoman. Tell me, can a leaky ladle draw water?"

Xiaoman said, "It can." The whole hall was taken aback. Master Chu was taken aback too, his goatee stuck in mid-twitch, and only after a long moment said, "...How?" Xiaoman said, "Draw it more times."

The whole hall laughed again, only this time the laughter went a slightly different way from before. The front few rows turned round to look at him, the way you look at a fool talking nonsense; the mixed roots in the back few rows didn't turn round. They kept their heads down, and their shoulders shook. Xiaoman sat down, and from the wooden tablet at his chest a sleepy, impatient voice said in his ear, "...Who's making a racket?" He bent his head. "The master says mixed spirit roots are leaky ladles." The voice snorted. "He knows nothing."

Master Chu wasn't annoyed, though. Those four words, every three years when the gate opened, he always heard once. He rapped the ruler on the lecture table. "Draw it more times. Good. In the *Liezi*{典=liezi-yugong} there's a Foolish Old Man, nearly ninety, who was tired of two great mountains blocking his door and set about carrying the earth away basket by basket with his sons and grandsons. The Wise Old Man next door laughed at him, and he said: when I die, there'll be my sons; my sons will have grandsons, sons and grandsons without end, and the mountains won't grow any taller. And then what? The Emperor of Heaven was moved by his sincerity and sent two giants to carry the mountains away on their backs." He pointed the ruler at the back row. "Remember this, all of you: the mountains were carried off by gods. The Foolish Old Man dug his whole life, and how many baskets of earth did he get down? Shen Xiaoman, have you got sons and grandsons at home to dig for you? Have you got a god?"

The front few rows laughed again. Xiaoman opened his mouth. A moment ago the tablet had said "He knows nothing." The words were on the tip of his tongue, and one word held them down: hide. He said, "...No."

Master Chu said, "Then remember: a leaky ladle is a leaky ladle. Sit down."

This time nobody in the back rows bent their heads and laughed.

---

The seventeenth of the ninth month, his ninth day through the gate. That night the moon was just past full, but a thin layer of cloud had come up across the sky and the moon hid behind it, showing only a fuzzy white glow. At the foot of the back courtyard wall was a woodpile, pine, smelling of resin, no different from the one behind his house at home. Xiaoman climbed up as usual and sat cross-legged, took the wooden tablet out from his collar and laid it against his chest, then took the goose feather from his coat and held it under his nose. This was the method his mother had taught him at home: let a breath out thin and long, count heartbeats along with it, and only when you reached a hundred and twenty with the feather not stirring had you passed this gate. The night before he left home he had counted to ninety-six: twenty-four short. He shut his eyes.

With the first breath in, he felt something different.

The breath was cool and clear, like water just drawn from the well behind his house. It went in through his nose and flowed down his throat, all the way into his belly. Usually the breath flowed as far as his navel and scattered, like a ladle of water thrown on sand, soaking away without a trace. This time it didn't scatter. It kept flowing down, past his chest, past his navel, into a place three inches below his navel that he had never known he had. He couldn't see what the place looked like; he could only feel it: it was dry.

Dry as a paddy field in a year of drought, ice-cold, cracked open line after line. The cool breath soaked into it, and the place hissed like red-hot iron quenched in water. He jumped and almost opened his eyes, but from the wooden tablet at his chest he heard a very, very faint voice say, "...Don't move." So he didn't move.

The second breath soaked in, and a patch of the parched field grew damp. The third, a big patch. The fourth, the fifth, the tenth, like a soaking rain falling into cracked earth, drinking its fill little by little, and when it had drunk about half its fill, down at the bottom it suddenly **grew warm**. First a little warmth, as if someone had lit a lamp there. At first the lamp was only the size of a bean, flickering, exactly like the oil lamp with the trimmed-down wick in the kitchen at home; it burned and burned, brighter and brighter, and the warmth spread out ring after ring, out to his waist, out to his legs, out to his fingertips. Before he even had time to be afraid, the warmth began to move on its own.

It rushed out of his lower belly, sank down, went round to the back and, starting at the very bottom joint of his spine, burrowed upwards joint by joint like a little snake made of fire. It burrowed as far as the small of his back and ran into a gate. He could feel it was a gate, hard and cold, set across his spine. But when the little snake ran into it, the gate was open, and its hinges still had a few rough burrs on them as though they had just been chiselled. The little snake twisted and slipped through. It burrowed up to the middle of his back: another gate. Open too. It burrowed up to the back of his head: the third gate. This gate was only half open.

The little snake hit it, hit it once, didn't get through, and shrank back. Xiaoman felt as if someone had struck the back of his head with a wooden mallet. Stars burst in front of his eyes. The warmth went spinning under the back of his skull and hit, and hit, and every time it hit, his head ached, ached until his back teeth hurt. He clenched his teeth, but somehow, without knowing why, he thought of the way his father drew a bow: watch the wind first, then the deer. Draw it full, and don't be in a hurry to let go. Wait for the wind to catch its breath.

So he stopped hitting. He let the warmth stay behind the gate, stay there, while a breath came in, sank slowly down, and slowly went out again. In, sink, out. One breath, two, three. When the ninth breath had gone all the way out, the ache in the back of his head suddenly paused, like the wind dropping for an instant, and he drew a breath all the way down, and the warmth, riding that breath, hit the gate head-on.

There was a roar inside his head, and everything went white in front of his eyes, like looking straight up at the sun on a clear day. The warmth broke the gate open, rushed up to the crown of his head, turned once there, rolled over, and came down by way of his forehead, the bridge of his nose, his throat, his chest, pouring down like a channel with its sluice thrown open, back to below his navel, where it settled and stayed. A full circle, exactly. He opened his eyes. The goose feather under his nose hadn't stirred. Only then did he remember that he hadn't managed to count a single one of those breaths. But he knew that every one of them had gone well past a hundred and twenty.

The sky still had that thin cloud across it and the woodpile was still the woodpile, but everything he looked out at was different. Every crack between the bricks of the courtyard wall, he could see clearly; the lines on the wings of the cricket at the foot of the wall, he could see clearly; three houses away, Sun Ergou was mumbling in his sleep, "Ma, I don't want bran," and he could hear every word. He sat on the woodpile not daring to breathe, afraid that one breath would make it all go away again.

A little half-transparent silver fox crept out of the wooden tablet and crouched on his knee. It yawned, eyelids drooping, looking as though it hadn't had nearly enough sleep, and said, "...Through?"

Xiaoman stammered, "Th-through! Yinyue, in one night I... I..." It said, "I know. This queen worked it out." Xiaoman was taken aback. "...Worked it out?" Yinyue yawned again. "You think you sat out this year for nothing?"

"Those three gates on your back, if you hit them yourself, you could hit them until you're thirty and they wouldn't open," Yinyue said. "So this queen slept in your heart, and every night, while you were breathing out and in, chiselled away at them for you, a bit at a time." It raised a paw and touched him once at the small of his back, once in the middle of his back, once at the back of his head. "[Tailbone, Spine Pass, Jade Pillow]{注=三关}. This queen chiselled two and a half. The last half you have to break yourself. If this queen broke it for you, you'd never know as long as you lived how a gate opens." Xiaoman's mouth hung open, and it was a long while before he squeezed out, "...So what am I now?"

Yinyue said, "[Refining essence into true qi]{典=zhonglv-sishi}. Down among mortals they call it the first layer of Qi Condensation."

He thought of that winter at home, sitting on the woodpile every night breathing out and in, and nothing there, only a dry well below his belly. He thought of how every night, when he'd finished breathing, he held the tablet in his palm and gave his report, and when he finished the tablet was always warm. He had thought it was his palm warming it; but there had been plenty of nights when he never touched it at all, and it lay against his chest and was warm all the same. Who had warmed it? It was her. Three hundred-odd nights on the woodpile, he'd thought he was nothing but a dry well; all along someone had been at the bottom of the well, digging for him. Master Chu was right: the mountains were carried off by gods. But even gods have to see first that someone is digging. After a good while he said, "...So I'm not a genius."

Yinyue said, "You're a leaky ladle. This queen is the tinker who mends it."

Xiaoman snorted out a laugh, but halfway through the laugh he couldn't go on. Only now did he see it properly. It was thinner than on that night last autumn when it had crept into the tablet. Tonight the moon was hidden behind the cloud and let down only a fuzzy white glow, and even that little light went through it; through its body he could see the woodpile behind it, see the pine logs in it one by one. Its single tail hung down over his knee, not even its tip curling up, and the eight stumps at the root of it were darker than last year. He said, "...And you? You chiselled for a year, you..." Yinyue turned its face away and said, "This queen is a queen of demons. What's chiselling a few gates?" It looked out past the courtyard wall at the black mass of the mountain, and after a while said quietly, "...Just need to sleep a few more days."

It yawned again, a long one this time, and when it had finished its eyes were already half shut. It said, "But you were three days quicker than this queen worked out." Xiaoman asked, "Is three days quicker a good thing?" It looked at him, looked a good while, and said, "It's a good thing. This queen's business: keep it well hidden for this queen."

Before the words had died away, it was gone. He said quietly, "...Sleep a few more days. I won't make a noise."

---

Xiaoman jumped down from the woodpile. Jumped, they'd say; in fact he floated. His feet touched the ground without a sound, not even startling the cricket at the foot of the wall. The courtyard wall was more than ten feet high; he stepped back two paces, sprang forward, set his hands on the top of the wall, and was over. Outside the wall was the back mountain, and he charged up it without stopping for breath. A ravine that took half a li to go round in the daytime he cleared with one push of his toes, and only when he had landed did he look back: the ravine was a good ten feet across.

At the top of the cliff stood a big bluestone, a head taller than he was, its face covered in moss. He clenched his fist and coaxed the warmth that circled inside him out along his arm, bit by bit. It took three tries before it crept lazily up his arm, crept into his fist, hot. He threw a punch. There was a crack; a slab of skin broke off the bluestone and fell at his feet, moss and all. His fist wasn't even scraped.

He wanted to laugh. He opened his mouth to let out a shout at the whole of Sunken Cauldron Mountain, and as it reached his throat he bit down hard on his own fist. Biting his fist, squatting behind the bluestone with its broken skin, he laughed until he shook all over, laughed till the tears came, and didn't make a single sound. And as he laughed he recited, in his head, the word of the one in the tablet: "Hide. Hide, hide, hide..."

Going down the mountain he didn't dare run. He walked, step by step, and went the long way round the ravine on purpose, half a li, crossing on the stones, and when he stepped on a slippery one he made a point of stumbling. There wasn't a soul around. Halfway down, as the first fish-belly pale showed at the edge of the sky, under the old pine beside the stone steps at the gate, a bamboo broom stood leaning against the roots, and Elder Qu was holding up a long thin bamboo pole with a dab of birdlime on its tip. He touched it into the pine branches and brought down a cold-season cicada, touched again, and brought down another. Xiaoman kept his head down and tried to slip past behind him. Elder Qu didn't lift his eyelids. "That stone across the ravine is very slippery."

The sweat on Xiaoman's back all went cold at once.

For the next few nights, he could never get that circle to run round again. As soon as he sat on the woodpile his mind wouldn't keep still: he thought of the green, bitter, grassy smell on Ma Xiaobao, and the warmth leaked out; he thought of his mother, and it leaked again. On the twenty-second of the ninth month, the daughter-in-law of the fat woman who ran the kitchen brought radishes up the mountain with her grandson on her back, not yet a year old. She put him down in an empty radish basket, and he fell asleep there by the stove door. When the child breathed, his chest didn't move at all; only his belly rose, and fell, and the fall was longer than the rise. A fly landed on his face and he didn't wake; he just went on, rise, fall. That night on the woodpile Xiaoman copied the child: he didn't try to pull the warmth along, and when a thought came he didn't chase it off; he let it land and lift off again like that fly. And the warmth stopped leaking.

Two days later, Master Chu saw him in the lecture hall watching Elder Qu catching cicadas outside the window, and sneered, "Watching the sweeper? He thinks he's the hunchback in the *Zhuangzi*{典=zhuangzi-dasheng} who catches cicadas: 'His will undivided, it gathers into spirit.' Fifty years of catching cicadas, and what has he gathered? Three hundred steps!"

Once the warmth ran round properly, he noticed something else: the spirit qi on this mountain was like the pot of porridge his family boiled up in the twelfth month, little rice and lots of water; scoop up a ladle and you had to hunt for grains at the bottom. There was only one place that was different. Every day when he carried water he had to pass outside the low stone wall halfway up the back of the mountain, and inside the wall was the herb garden, bed after bed of spirit herbs: that was the green, bitter smell on Ma Xiaobao. Through just a wall, the grains in one breath were worth ten breaths on the woodpile. But the herb garden was Zhou Heng's, and the only thing that could get you in at night was the night-watch token at Ma Xiaobao's waist.

At the end of the ninth month Master Chu took the roll. He didn't need to lift his eyelids to look at people. He pointed with his ruler from the first row to the last, and for each one he pointed at, he called it: "First layer. Nothing. Nothing. First layer..." When he'd finished he laid the ruler on the lecture table. "Those who've drawn in qi needn't celebrate. The first ladle of water, even a leaky ladle brings up." In the back row he pointed at Xiaoman: "First layer." He pointed at Sun Ergou: "Nothing." Sun Ergou buried his face in his sleeve. In the front few rows, not even Ma Xiaobao turned round.

That evening, when Xiaoman came back with the water, Ahe was squatting by the well waiting for him, the line of green showing under her grey robe as usual. She didn't look up. "What layer?" Xiaoman said, "First." She made a note in her notebook, closed it, and only then said, "Zhou He. Second layer."

For a long while Xiaoman couldn't say anything. At last he said, "...How many days did it take you to draw in qi?"

Ahe said, "Three."

"And the second layer?"

"Another half month." She closed the notebook. "Master's scroll, its first line is 'Observe the way of heaven, hold to the workings of heaven.' I watched the grass for three days. However grass drinks water, that's how I drank." She paused. "It's not that I'm quick. It's that the scroll's quick. And Master's sleeve: every few days he takes a pill out of his sleeve for me, much better than what the outer sect hands out, and it's not in Zhou Heng's accounts. This second layer of mine, half is the scroll and half is that sleeve."

Xiaoman said, "Then you should be happy."

Ahe said, "Happy goes in the notebook." She glanced over towards the dining hall, where the name-calling steward was passing the door with his hands behind his back. "A heavenly spirit root, to some people, is a piece of fat meat. Fat meat, you don't lay out on show. Don't you go telling anyone that."

Xiaoman said, "What about me? First layer. Do I lay it out on show or not?"

Ahe said, "Show it all you like. Nobody steals a leaky ladle."

---

The first of the tenth month was spirit-stone day again. Behind Ma Xiaobao stood his little band of followers. The one at the very end was long and thin, a bit hunched, drooping like a scallion left three days in the sun. He never said a word, and everyone called him Old Wilt. Every ten days or so the Ma family sent loads up the mountain on a carrying pole, and in the loads, besides osmanthus cake, there were pills. Ma Xiaobao banged a porcelain bottle down on the table and shook one out to show the line: the size of a soybean, dusty grey, one line on it, with a ring of black specks along the line. When the stopper came out, a medicinal smell, scorched with something sweet in it, made everyone for three zhang in front and behind sniff. "Qi-Gathering Pill. From the best shop in the county town." Sun Ergou craned his neck and asked quietly, "Senior brother, the storytellers say an immortal's pill has nine lines. How come this one's only got one?" Zhou Heng, behind his table at the door of the dining hall, didn't look up. "The *Baopuzi*{典=baopu} says, 'A pill of nine turnings, take it and in three days you become immortal.' Nine turnings, nine lines: that's just what books say. In three hundred years since this abbey was founded, the best batch was three turnings, and it sits on the altar in the Founders' Hall, and nobody can bear to eat it." The line clicked their tongues.

---

On the eighth of the tenth month, Zhou Heng called Xiaoman into the stewards' office. The stewards' office was a narrow little room, one wall of it shelves, and on the shelves there wasn't a single book: from top to bottom they were stacked with ledgers. Zhou Heng didn't look up. He opened one. "The herb garden. Eighth month, three spirit herbs lost. Ninth month, four lost." Xiaoman said, "One more." Zhou Heng nodded heavily, and his careworn face crumpled into a dried tangerine. "You see it. **It's improving.** Tenth month, only seven days in: nine." He smoothed the ledger with his palm. "Twelve years I've had charge of it, and every year there's less than the year before. I can't bear to lose even one."

Half a head poked in round the door. It was Ma Xiaobao, and he shouted, "Senior Brother Zhou, I'll go! I'm the best at catching thieves!" Zhou Heng said, "You went in the ninth month. In the ninth month, four were lost. Before you went, three." The half a head went bright red at once and vanished, whoosh, and the four fingers still clutching the door frame slipped quietly away after it.

Just then Yinyue's voice sounded in his ear, sleepy as if it came up from the bottom of a well: "...One more each month, and this month, nine in seven days. A thief steals by how much he can sell. This one steals by how big its belly is. It's not improving. It's growing. ...Take a radish." And she went back to sleep. Zhou Heng looked at him suspiciously. "Junior brother, who are you talking to?" Xiaoman said, "To... my Dao heart." Zhou Heng was struck with respect, his eyes shone, and he slapped the ledger. "Good. Promising!"

He took a wooden token out of a drawer (at the end of the ninth month he'd taken it back off Ma Xiaobao's waist himself) and held it out. "The herb garden's night-watch token. Night watch has no perks, so nobody takes it. The register says your family are hunters. Do you know your tracks?" Xiaoman said, "A few." The token was ebony, as long as a palm, carved "Night Watch" on one side and a little cauldron on the other, the cauldron's mouth inlaid with a ring of fluorite dust that gave off a faint green glow of its own in the dark, not enough to light three steps. Zhou Heng also broke a piece off half a candle for him. "There's a night-watch book hanging at the garden gate. One night on watch, one handprint."

Ma Xiaobao's half a head poked in again, and this time it stayed. "Senior Brother Zhou, what perks are there for the night watch?" Zhou Heng said, "The outer sect's poor. There are no perks. The perk is in the twelfth month: the sect's Grand Contest, inner and outer sects competing together, and the champion gets a Foundation Pill, from the Abbot's own hand. In the whole abbey, in a whole year, there's only that one." Ma Xiaobao's eyes lit up. "How many lines?" Zhou Heng said, "One." He turned the ledger back to the herb-garden page and said to Xiaoman, "You've got till the end of the month. When the accounts are done at month's end, if the herb garden is still losing things, this token goes to Senior Brother Ma."

The half a head at the door grinned at Xiaoman. "Junior Brother Shen, watch well. Your senior brother's waiting."

When Xiaoman came out of the stewards' office, Ahe was standing at the foot of the wall with a small hoe on her shoulder and mud all over her trouser legs. Since the first of the tenth month, on one word from the Abbot, the herb garden had been hers to look after by day: a heavenly wood root, practising on the herb garden.

She said, "Nine lost in seven days. That number of Zhou Heng's, I'm the one who reported it." She looked at the token in his hand too. "By day it's mine. By night, it's yours now?" Xiaoman nodded. She took a little wax-sealed bamboo tube from her coat and shook it. It was empty; it made no sound. "For seeds. The hillside fields get thinner every year. I want to get hold of some seeds that'll live and try them. I've kept a foot of ground in the east corner, a bamboo stake at each corner, a red thread tied round. By day I watch it. By night you're on watch, so watch it for me while you're at it."

Xiaoman said, "Watching a foot of ground, do I get paid?"

Ahe said, "You do. It's written down."

---

The radish he begged from the kitchen. Xiaoman went to help in the kitchen at dusk and peeled radishes all afternoon, and when he'd finished he held one up and said honestly to the fat woman, "Auntie, I'd like a radish." The fat woman held the big ladle in that kitchen, and was so fat she had to turn sideways to get through the stove-room door. She looked him over. "What do you want a radish for?" Xiaoman said, "Practice." The flesh of the fat woman's face twitched and twitched, and she picked the biggest one out of the basket and stuffed it into his arms with the other. "Have two. Take them back and practise twice."

The hour of the Rat, the herb garden. The herb garden was halfway up the back mountain, with cliffs on three sides and on the fourth that low stone wall. Bed after bed of spirit herbs grew in the garden, their leaves giving off a faint ghostly light in the night, like a gardenful of unlit lanterns. Between the beds lay a thin mist no higher than his ankles; as he walked through, the mist drew aside and closed again behind him. The green bitter smell was so thick here it turned sweet; one breath of it and the root of his tongue stayed cool a long while. Xiaoman drew a breath. The grains of rice in that breath were thicker than he'd imagined. He was just about to shut his eyes when a lantern lit up outside the wall.

Behind the lantern was Ma Xiaobao, with two followers behind him, each carrying a food box. Ma Xiaobao shouted from the top of the wall, "Junior Brother Shen! Your senior brother's come to keep you company on watch!" The three of them climbed over. Ma Xiaobao first lifted the night-watch book hanging by the garden gate, licked the worn-down brush tied to it, and in the square for the eighth wrote a line in big characters: **Ma Xiaobao kept watch with him. Hour of the Rat to hour of the Ox.** He stepped back and admired it for a while, very pleased. The three of them sat down on the stone steps at the garden gate, set the lantern in the middle and opened the food boxes, and the sweet smell of osmanthus cake and the salty smell of cured meat drifted on the wind all over the garden. Xiaoman said, "Brother Xiaobao, a night watch needs quiet." Ma Xiaobao said, "Quiet for what? If the thief hears there's people, he won't dare come. That's called beating the grass to scare the snake." The three of them ate half the night and laughed half the night. Partway through, one of the followers said he was going to relieve himself and went off into the garden carrying a food box, and was gone a good while before he came back. Once the hour of the Ox had passed, they dusted off their backsides and left, abandoning the lantern and an empty food box on the steps; none of them thought to take them.

Near dawn, Xiaoman went over the garden spot by spot, and at the far west end one spirit herb was gone, right down to the root.

That morning Xiaoman came back to the herb garden and squatted by the stump of that herb. His father couldn't read, but he knew every hoofprint and pawprint in the mountains. The cut was clean: bitten off in one go; the thing had good teeth. On the muddy ground were a few small pawprints, cat-sized but deeper than a cat's; the thing was heavy. Along the way the pawprints went, a few fine lines dragged through the mud, running alongside the prints, one beside the next, none crossing the other. One tail dragging on the ground could only drag one line. He counted them: five. On a stone at the corner of the wall hung a tuft of fur, red, deep red, redder than Ma Xiaobao's fox-fur jacket.

The pawprints came from the foot of the cliff at the west end and went back to the foot of the cliff. Last night the lantern had been set at the east end, and the wind came from the east, and osmanthus cake, cured meat and the sweat of three people had all drifted west on it in one go. The thing could smell people, so it went round to the end where there weren't any, ate, and left.

The second night, Xiaoman lit the lantern the Ma boys had left behind and set it, along with the food box that still had cake crumbs in it, at the east end of the garden. He himself went round to the west end, to the foot of the cliff where the wind blew into the garden, and squatted down behind a clump of spirit herbs half as tall as a man. The east end had a light and the smell of cake; tonight the thing would only come to the empty west end: come right into his arms. He held a radish in his hand and squatted without moving, like the stump of a tree under an old pine. His will undivided. However big heaven and earth were, all he knew was the wind on his face.

Halfway through the hour of the Ox, at the far end of the herb garden, beside the most valuable plant of all, the "purple jade ginseng" (its leaves so purple they were nearly black, silver threads running through their veins), something was crouching. It was the size of a cat, with a coat of red fur that the faint light of the spirit-herb leaves made shine bright red, and trailing behind it were five tails; on its head grew a single horn, short and sharp, like a bamboo shoot that hadn't finished growing. It held a spirit herb in its two front paws and was eating it, crunch, crunch, and as it ate it made a sound, tock, tock, like someone knocking two little stones together. From the tablet, a voice so sleepy it could barely be heard said in disgust, "...A [zheng]{注=zheng} from Mount Zhang'e. Five tails and one horn, and its voice is like stones being struck. It's all in the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*{典=shanhai-zheng}... You mortals, don't you read?"

It pawed over to a second herb and stopped, its nose twitching twice towards the west. The wind had turned, and it had his scent. It stared at the clump of herbs where he was hiding and crouched low, all five tails standing up together, tock, tock, tock in its mouth, fast and hard. Xiaoman's chest tightened, so he let his belly drop, a long drop, and pressed the tightness down. Only then did he slowly stand up, both hands open, letting it see there was nothing in them, except a radish. He took three steps, bent down, laid the radish gently on the ground in front of it, stepped back and squatted down again.

It stopped eating and looked at the radish, then at him. That look, Xiaoman remembered until he was old. The first layer of it was "Who do you think you're insulting?" The second was "This beast eats spirit herbs." The third, the third he didn't understand at the time; he understood only later. That layer was: "...This is for me?" It stared at the radish for a long, long time. Then it ate the radish.

The way it ate the radish wasn't the way it ate spirit herbs. With spirit herbs it hugged them and gnawed, a bite, a look all round, as if afraid someone would snatch them away. The radish it ate lying down on the ground, both front paws holding it, bite by bite, very slowly, and it even ate the leaves. When it had finished it gave a long, loud burp, toppled over on the spot, wrapped its five tails round itself and went to sleep. It didn't run. In front of a stranger.

Xiaoman squatted there not daring to move. This thing had been stealing spirit herbs for a year, and the more it stole the hungrier it got; a radish, not worth a copper, given to it by somebody, and it was full. He thought of Ma Xiaobao's hand that went on feeling around in the food box even when he was asleep, and his chest went a little cold.

Early next morning he walked into the stewards' office carrying a ball of red fur that was snoring away. Zhou Heng looked at the zheng for a long time, opened the ledger, tapped his finger on the herb-garden page, thought a long while, and only then said, "Herb garden: first to ninth of the tenth month, ten. Entered: one spirit beast." He shut the ledger and let out a long breath, and a smile appeared on his face. Xiaoman had known him a month, and this was the first time he'd seen him smile. He said, "The accounts balance. At month's end, if the herb garden hasn't lost another one, this token is yours from now on. Every month the garden pulls up the dead stalks, and every year they get thrown out. From now on they get thrown to you." Xiaoman said, "...Isn't that rubbish?" Zhou Heng said, "Rubbish goes in the accounts too."

That evening the whole outer sect saw the ball of red fur in his arms. Ma Xiaobao, sitting at the far end of the dining hall, stared at the red with his eyes as wide as two copper coins, chewing and chewing until he forgot to move his mouth, and half a piece of osmanthus cake fell out of the corner of it, plop, into his soup bowl.

---

On the eleventh of the tenth month, at the hour of the Rooster handover, Ahe finished reporting the day's numbers and bent her head to slap the mud off her trouser legs, barely moving her mouth. "Ma Xiaobao's been asking about your animal. Where it sleeps, where it is at night. Yesterday he asked the kitchen, today he asked the gatekeeper, and the gatekeeper took a piece of osmanthus cake off him." She said it very fast, as if afraid that saying it slowly would cost her an extra share of interest. "Be careful."

Xiaoman didn't hide. At night the little zheng would only sleep in the heap of dry straw behind the woodshed; he could hide past the eleventh but not past the end of the month. He had made up his mind about only one thing: when they came, what to do. That night he took off the night-watch token and pushed it into a crack at the very bottom of the woodpile. A person could take a beating; this one thing, nobody was going to get their hands on.

The night of the twelfth of the tenth month, three days short of full moon. Shen Xiaoman squatted behind the woodshed feeding the little zheng radish. Stroking its single horn, he said in a low voice, "Listen. If anybody comes in a bit, don't bite. If you bite somebody, they'll beat you. If they beat you, I'll have to hit back. If I hit back, I can't stay hidden." The little zheng, half a radish in its mouth, may or may not have understood. Just then, behind him, the woodshed door creaked open.

Ma Xiaobao walked out of the woodshed with bits of straw stuck all over him. Behind him, one after another, came five more. The six of them lined up in the moonlight and blocked off the palm-sized bit of open ground behind the woodshed completely. Ma Xiaobao said, "Shen Xiaoman. I hear you've taken in an animal. Give it to me." Xiaoman nudged the little zheng behind him. "It doesn't take to strangers." Ma Xiaobao said, "I don't care whether it takes to me. As long as I take it." He waved his hand, and the five closed in.

In the time it took the five to close in, Xiaoman had gone over the accounts once. Five followers, all old disciples three years in, two at the third layer of Qi Condensation, three at the second. Ma Xiaobao at the first, force-fed with pills, as soft as a cake left soaking in water. He was first layer himself, and on the bluestone up the back mountain, one punch had only knocked off a bit of skin. Could he win? He couldn't. Going all out for Ma Xiaobao alone, that he could manage: grab him, slam one fist into that soaked cake. But once he'd done it, the five wouldn't just be beating him any more. The ball of red fur in his coat would get dragged off, and the tablet at his chest would get dragged out too, so they could see what kind of wretched tablet got hot in a fight. He could beat one Ma Xiaobao. He couldn't beat what stood behind Ma Xiaobao.

He scooped the little zheng up and stuffed it into his coat, wrapped the front of his clothes round it, crouched down and wrapped both arms round his head. Fists and feet came down at once like rain.

The first was Ma Xiaobao's kick, to his back, and having kicked he stepped back and moved his osmanthus cake to his other hand. The second to the fifth were from the two third-layer ones, one kicking his left ribs, one his right. The one kicking his left ribs had a patch of black cloth on the toe of his shoe. Xiaoman counted in his head: two, three, four, five. After that it got muddled. He curled up into a ball and gave them his back, his backside, his two arms, one thing after another. Only the red ball of fur in his coat, struggling for all it was worth and going tock tock, and the tablet at his chest, he couldn't give them. At twelve, Ma Xiaobao pushed into the crowd and slammed a fist into his left eye socket. Everything went black, then bright, and the world on his left was only half there.

Ma Xiaobao crouched down and felt all over him, at his waist, up his sleeves. "Where is it? The night-watch token?" He found nothing and thrust a hand into Xiaoman's coat, and the little zheng bit him on the web of his thumb. Ma Xiaobao yelped, ow, and snatched his hand back, shook the blood off it, and pounded him twice on the back. At nineteen, the black cloth came again, a stamp to his left side under the ribs, and there was a crack. He heard it. It came from inside his own body.

At twenty, the blow didn't come. Of the five, a thin one who had been standing at the very edge the whole time lifted his foot and put it down again, and said, very low, "...That's enough."

It was Old Wilt, the one behind Ma Xiaobao who never said anything. All Xiaoman knew about him was that he was the son of one of the Ma family's tenants. Ma Xiaobao turned his head and gave him a look. Old Wilt lowered his head, but that foot didn't lift again.

Twenty-one. Twenty-two. The twenty-third was Ma Xiaobao's kick again. He stepped up, looked at the ball crouched on the ground, and gave a last kick, neither hard nor soft, to Xiaoman's shoulder, as though stamping a seal on the whole business.

As he kicked, Xiaoman, through the gap between his arms, with the right eye he could still open, happened to look at Ma Xiaobao. He'd thought that when Ma Xiaobao kicked someone, his face would be gloating. But Ma Xiaobao's face, in the moonlight, was screwed up, and his eyes were still searching back and forth over Xiaoman's coat and waist, like someone who had turned out every chest and not found the thing. At the time Xiaoman didn't understand what that meant. He just remembered it.

Ma Xiaobao, breathing hard, spat. "An animal that won't own its master, what use is it to me? Keep it and feed it yourself." He walked two steps, then turned back. "Month's end, the accounts. The half month you're lying on your bed, if anything goes missing from the herb garden at night..." He didn't finish. He left with the five. Old Wilt walked last and didn't look back.

Behind the woodshed there was only one person and one zheng again. Slowly, Shen Xiaoman lowered the arms he had wrapped round his head, tipped over, and lay flat on his back in the mud. The mud was cold and wet. There was a taste of blood in his mouth; he had bitten his tongue. His left eye was swollen shut, and at his ribs every breath hurt; breathe deep, and it hurt till everything went white. He tried a few times and found a gap neither deep nor shallow, and breathed by that gap, breath after breath.

Lying there, he finished Ma Xiaobao's half-said words in his head. If he couldn't get up, things would naturally go missing from the herb garden: the zheng had stopped stealing, so some other "zheng" would do it. At the end of the month Zhou Heng would open the ledger, and the night-watch token and the dead stalks would go back into the Ma family's hands one after the other. Hit back, and he couldn't stay hidden; don't hit back, lie there half a month, and the herb garden lost things.

The little zheng crept out of his coat and shook out its bristling fur, climbed onto his chest, lowered its head, and nudged his face with its single horn, gently, again and again. The horn pressed on his swollen eye socket and the pain made him suck in air. He didn't pull away. And then the tablet at his chest began to burn.

---

This time it burned a long, long while, so long that even the little zheng sensed something was wrong, climbed down off his chest and crouched to one side to watch. Yinyue slowly crept out of the tablet. It was thinner again than that other night; the moonlight came through it, and he could see the moon behind it. It lowered its head and looked at the eye swollen up like a steamed bun, and kept looking until the moon had moved a finger's width behind it.

Usually its tongue was so sharp: he was noisy, he was stupid, he was a leaky ladle. Now it didn't say a single word. Then it opened its mouth and, ptoo, spat something out. It landed on his chest with a ding, not like bronze, more like a bell struck once in a mountain temple very far away, humming in his chest a good while.

It was a palm-sized [little bronze thing]{注=danlu}, a round belly on three legs, two ears standing up from the rim, all over a layer of green verdigris with a dull gold showing through underneath, like the incense burner on the altar table in the village ancestral hall, squeezed a hundred times smaller. On its mouth sat a lid, and on the lid crouched a little beast, which yawned, opened one eye, glanced at him, and shut it again. Yinyue said, "From this queen's belly. Swallowed who knows what year, and it's been eating this queen's spirit qi for years. Look after it for this queen. It eats anything; feed it, and it spits something out. What it spits out depends on your luck: nine times in ten, junk. Feed it mortal things and mostly you get mortal things; if you want something immortal out, you have to feed it something immortal. Each time it opens, it takes a mouthful from you." Xiaoman cried, "Yinyue?"

"Next time," she said, "hit back."

As the words fell, she was gone, like a wisp of smoke in a stove.

Xiaoman clutched the tablet in his palm and called "Yinyue" twice. No answer. The little curled-up fox was still there, but of its nine tails, the one that used to glow was dark now. He stared at it until his palm was all sweat, and only then saw that tail, very, very slowly, glow once, and go dark again.

Still there.

Only then did he let out a long breath, too hard, and his ribs hurt so much he doubled over. Next time, hit back. She wasn't telling him to hit back today. She was saying: today, this is how it is; next time, hit back. He put those words away carefully in his heart.

Tonight was the first night of that half month Ma Xiaobao had talked about.

He got up holding on to the woodpile, and at his ribs there was a crack, and he went down on his knees again. Broken, at least one. He felt out the night-watch token from the bottom of the woodpile, hung it back on his waist, and tucked the little zheng and the little bronze burner into his coat together. There was no getting over the wall now, but at the foot of the wall was a drainage gap, and he got down on his belly and crawled out bit by bit, and crawled up the little path on the back mountain. Every ten feet, he rested three times; and while he rested he didn't dare breathe deep, only by that gap neither deep nor shallow, one breath, one breath. The moon moved from one side of the sky to the other before he had crawled to the herb-garden gate. He lifted the night-watch book hanging by the gate and, with a hand that was mud or blood, he couldn't tell which, pressed it into the square for the twelfth night, and there was a dark red handprint.

He sat like that, his back against the garden gate, until dawn.

Near dawn there were footsteps on the path, one step, a stop. It was Sun Ergou, holding a cloth soaked in well water. Sun Ergou's place on the long bed was by the little window, and from that window you could see the open ground behind the woodshed as clear as day. He crouched down and pressed the cloth to Xiaoman's swollen left eye, and after a long while said quietly, "...I didn't come out." Xiaoman said, "I know." Sun Ergou said, "I was scared." Xiaoman said, "So was I. Being scared's nothing to be ashamed of." Sun Ergou kept his head down, and his ears slowly went red. He said, "When I'm... steady enough." The second half, he didn't say.

---

On the thirteenth of the tenth month Xiaoman went to the dining hall propped up by Sun Ergou. He thought: if I don't go, it'll look even more like something's happened. The whole dining hall stole looks at him, and when they'd looked, stole looks at Ma Xiaobao. Everybody knew what had happened. Nobody said a thing. Ma Xiaobao passed his table with an empty bowl and poked him in the shoulder with a finger, right on the spot where the twenty-third had landed. "Junior brother, that was a nasty fall. You lie down and rest. Your senior brother will see to the herb garden."

Ahe came down from the mountain. She had gone into the herb garden at the hour of the Rabbit and seen the square for the twelfth night in the night-watch book. She sat down opposite him with her bowl and looked at his face first, from his left eye to the corner of his mouth, and said, "Who hit you?" Xiaoman said, "I fell." Ahe made a note in her notebook, shielding it with her hand, but he still glimpsed a circle. Ahe said, "The mud marks on your clothes. One mark, one blow. I counted. Twenty-three." Xiaoman was startled. She took a boiled egg from under her bowl, still warm, and put it in front of him. "Eat it while it's warm." Xiaoman said, "...Going in the accounts?" Ahe picked up her charcoal and set it to the paper, and it stayed there a long time; the tip of the charcoal pressed a little black dot into the paper that slowly spread into a ring. Then she wrote something, closed the notebook, and said, "The interest, this time... I won't count it yet." She picked up her bowl and left, and never once looked back.

Outside the dining hall, at the door of the dispensary, stood the Jishi Hall's carrying pole with its load of medicinal herbs, and behind it a barefoot errand boy. Xiaoman recognised him: the one under the eaves who had shouted "Then I'll be the one!" The boy took a letter out of his coat. On the envelope was his uncle's hand: For my nephew Shen Xiaoman, to be opened by him.

The letter wasn't long. All well at home, don't worry. Your ma's eyes have been strained a little by sewing at night, nothing serious. The night we buried Shidan, it was this boy who helped me dig. The last line was written smaller: **Your pa goes into Black Pine Ridge every day now. The Ma dogs don't go past the broken stone; he does. His knees are worse than last year. On grey days he can't get off the kang; on clear days he goes all the same. I can't talk him out of it.**

Xiaoman read the letter three times and tucked it into his coat, against the wooden tablet.

---

That day, at the hour of the Dragon, Xiaoman didn't go to the lecture hall. He squatted in the woodshed with the little bronze burner in front of him and talked to it. "What's your name?" The lid didn't move. He reeled off seven or eight titles out of the mouths of the storytellers in town, "Heaven-Quelling Divine Burner", "Nine-Turn Golden Pill Burner", "The One Burner of Heaven and Earth", longer and longer, louder and louder, and it couldn't even be bothered to stir. Since last night he'd had only a bowl of porridge and an egg, his stomach was rumbling, and in the end he swore at it: "You rice bucket, you're harder to please than I am." There was a click, the lid gave a little jump, and the small beast opened one eye and winked at him. What it had taken a fancy to was, of all things, "rice bucket".

Round its belly was carved a ring of pictures, so fine you had to squint: mountains, water, a winding river, and by the river a square city. He spanned it with his thumb and fingers and couldn't cover half its girth. But having carried it all the way last night and lifting it again this morning, he couldn't help feeling it was a little heavier than last night.

Three knocks at the door, tok tok tok. Flustered, he grabbed the little zheng and plonked it on top of the burner. The little zheng, still half asleep, wrapped its four paws round the burner, hugged it under its belly and covered it with its five tails, so that all that showed was a ball of red fur. Xiaoman pulled the door wide open and leaned against the frame, his body blocking half the room.

It was Zhou Heng. He held his ledger in his arms, looked at Xiaoman's left eye swollen to a slit, looked at the hand he was holding to his ribs, and said, "Junior Brother Ma came to the stewards' office first thing. Said you'd had a bad fall and can't get up, and for the next few nights he'll keep watch on the herb garden for you." He went on, "I went and looked at the night-watch book at the garden gate. Last night's square has a handprint." He didn't say what colour the handprint was. Xiaoman said, "I put it there. Tonight I'm keeping watch too." Zhou Heng said, "In that state." Xiaoman said, "I can do it." Zhou Heng shut his ledger. "At month's end I go by the accounts, not by faces." With that he went off, ledger in his arms.

Xiaoman thought for a long time before he worked it out: about last night, Zhou Heng knew. And Zhou Heng went by his accounts and nothing else.

---

He barred the door and dug the Rice Bucket out from under the little zheng's belly. Feed it, and it spits something out: that was all Yinyue had left him. He stared at the bronze belly, even forgetting the pain in his ribs, the way he used to stare as a small boy at the rattle-drum his father brought back from the county town.

The first thing he threw in was a stick of firewood. Crunch: his palm went cool, and a mouthful of the warmth in his dantian was sucked away. Click: out came a toothpick. Now he got into it: a pebble, half a steamed cornbread, a dead stalk of milkvetch, a dry leaf, a beef bone left from the kitchen's soup, one thing after another into it. For each thing in, one mouthful sucked away, one thing out, and everything out was junk. When he got to the sole of a straw sandal, it burped and spat it straight back out, all over his face. Finally he threw in the last two spirit stones he had (the two from the ninth month this leaky ladle of his had long since drained dry, mouthful by mouthful; these two were what was left from the first of the tenth after the Common Fund, and new ones weren't due until the first of the eleventh, more than half a month away). It crunched them loudly, crunch crunch: now that was a proper meal.

By the time he came to himself his dantian had long since run dry, everything went black, and his forehead hit the lid, thunk.

A row of things was already laid out on the ground: an even smaller pebble, a piece of rice crust hard enough to crack walnuts, a small clay jar. Lift the lid and out came a smell of beef tallow and Sichuan pepper; on the jar, in crooked characters, were four words, Beef Tallow Sweet Dew, and below them in small writing: Numbing, Hot, Fresh, Fragrant. The little zheng licked it once and ran round and round the room from the heat. In the middle of all this were two pills. One was grey with a tinge of red, no lines, wrinkled, bitter-smelling, wrapped in a scorched corner of straw paper, shrunk up beside the rice crust like a jujube stone nobody wanted. The other, the moment Xiaoman looked at it, he couldn't take his eyes off: the size of a longan, white and glowing all through, with a faint sheen of dawn colours in the white. It sat on a little square of red paper, shining by itself, like the pearl in the hand of a bodhisattva in a temple. There were a few little characters on the red paper; he didn't stop to read them. A fragrance went straight up his nose, sweet and cool, like the immortal peaches Granny Wu used to talk about. The little zheng's nose twitched and drool came pouring down a foot long, and its paw was already reaching out.

An immortal's pill. The Rice Bucket had brought out an immortal's pill.

Xiaoman was quicker than the zheng. He snatched it and, without thinking at all, popped it in his mouth.

The pill melted the instant it went in. Sweet, so sweet he broke into a grin. And once he'd grinned, he couldn't stop. He wanted to get up and go to the light by the window to see whether immortal qi was coming off him, but he'd taken only half a step when his knees gave and he pitched, thump, into the woodpile, still smiling; he got up, took another half step, and fell again.

A pointed nose poked out of his collar. Yinyue's voice was sticky with sleep; the nose twitched twice and she was suddenly awake. "...Where did this poison come from?"

Xiaoman, sprawled in the woodpile, couldn't stop grinning. "...Poison?"

"Yes." She looked at him, then at the little zheng with its foot of drool, pawed the little square of red paper on the woodpile over to her, and read: "Half-Step Grin-and-Tumble." She read the line of small writing below as well: "Take it and smile; flowers bloom at every step." She paused. "Flowers? Falls. Like you." She nudged the wrinkled one beside the rice crust with her nose. On the straw paper were three words, Stop-Laughing Pill, and below in small writing: Turn Back from the Sea of Bitterness. "Eat it. It's the antidote. One poison, one cure, out of the same pot." She added, "The pretty one's the poison, the ugly one's the cure. Remember that."

Grinning, Xiaoman stuffed the grey one in his mouth, and it was so bitter his grinning face screwed up into a knot. It took a cup of tea's time before the corners of his mouth would come down.

Yinyue glanced at the junk all over the floor and her eyes suddenly went fixed. Whoosh, she threw herself on the little clay jar and, hugging it, nosed at it. "...Beef tallow, Sichuan pepper, star anise: this is hotpot base! This queen's favourite!" She raised both front paws over her head. "Hotpot tonight! Yaaaay!"

Xiaoman's eyes went wide. He looked at her, at the jar, at the Rice Bucket, with an expression of utter disbelief. After a long moment he said slowly, "...Isn't that just what popped out of the hotpot you gave me? It shouldn't be called the Rice Bucket, it should be called the Hotpot. Let's cook in it tonight, it'll be great." When he'd said it, the last of that grin was still hanging on his mouth and his eyes were staring: he looked like someone who'd had a terrible shock and gone simple on the spot.

Yinyue leaned over and waved a paw in front of his eyes. "Hey. Did you only eat half the antidote? Hahahaha!"

Not letting go of the jar, she looked at the junk on the floor, then at the Rice Bucket. "Watch. This queen will start you off." She pulled a silver hair from the tip of her tail, baring her teeth at the sting, and dropped it in the Rice Bucket. The small beast on the lid opened one eye, looked at her, and turned its backside to face her. Click: out floated a sheet of yellow paper. Painted on it in cinnabar was a horse, big head, small body, four legs like four chopsticks stuck in a steamed bun, a circle dotted under each of its four hooves, its tail one curving hook to the edge of the paper. The cinnabar was still wet, and in the strokes there seemed to be a very fine thread of light, sliding down the horse's legs into the four circles under its hooves, and gone. Xiaoman held it in for a long time and finally came out with: "...What kind of hell-for-leather charm is this?" The words had barely left his mouth when he saw, in the corner of the charm, a line of vermilion characters the size of rice grains: Hell-for-Leather Charm. Below that was another line, smaller: One tread underfoot, and you're off at a gallop.

Every hair on Yinyue's face stood up in disgust. "One of this queen's hairs, for this. 'Off at a gallop.' Off where? Drawn like that, this queen could do better herself." Her paw curled up and she was asleep again. That tail had gone dark by another stretch. It didn't eat for nothing; touch it once and it took a mouthful from you.

That charm, "one tread underfoot": was it stuck to the sole of your foot, or trodden underfoot? And once you'd trodden, where did you go? He couldn't work it out. A worthless scrap of paper, but it had cost one hair from her tail, and he couldn't bring himself to throw it away. He folded it into a little square and tucked it into the lining of his belt, a pocket his mother had sewn in for coppers that had never once held any.

He was shoving all the junk under the woodpile when, last of all, a pill rolled out from under the rice crust, bright green, clear as a drop of dew that had set. It had lines on it. He counted them, and counted again: nine, all gold, one close against the next, like someone tying a sash round and round their waist. Carved in the gap between the lines were two characters the size of rice grains: Spring-Return. He brought it close and sniffed: a beany smell, with a little sweetness after. That was what the dead milkvetch stalk had bought.

He knew what one line looked like. His uncle had told him that in the glass jars on the counter at the Jishi Hall they kept Spring-Return Pills too, dull and dark, one line, a few black specks, five taels of silver apiece. The one in his hand had nine full lines.

He held the pill in his palm, his ribs twinging, twinging. He knew what this thing could do: swallow it, and tonight the broken bone would knit and the swelling would go down, and tonight, tomorrow night and every night after, he could stand in the herb garden on his own two feet and come through the month's-end accounts without a wobble. Ma Xiaobao's half-finished words would come to nothing.

He raised the pill to his mouth.

The letter in his coat dug into him. **His knees are worse than last year. On grey days he can't get off the kang; on clear days he goes all the same.** His father, dragging that leg, going past the broken stone alone, walking into Black Pine Ridge, all for a couple of rabbits.

The small beast on the lid of the burner had its eyes open, watching him.

He lowered his hand.

The fat woman in the kitchen had a little porcelain bottle that had held chilli oil; it was empty and she was going to throw it out. He went and begged it from the kitchen, washed it seven times at the well, and it still smelled of chilli. He put the pill in it, stoppered it tight, and tucked it into his coat, against the letter.

He couldn't send it down. Hand it to a barefoot boy, three days of mountain road: what if he pulled the stopper and had a sniff? Even if it got home: a mixed root one month through the gate who couldn't pay the Common Fund sending home a pill the Jishi Hall had never seen in its life, and Ma San tapping his short club on the door frame asking, Shen the Elder, your son up the mountain, whose house did he steal that from? He asked the boy to carry a spoken message to his uncle: Your boy is well, don't worry. Tell Pa not to go into Black Pine Ridge. And he took out three coppers (all he had on him was those three) and pressed them on the barefoot boy. The boy held the three coppers and looked at him a long while. "Your uncle said, he's already given me the three coppers." He pushed the money back at him, shouldered the pole and left. Xiaoman clutched the three coppers, his face hot for a long while.

That night, at the hour of the Rat, unable to get over the wall, he crawled out through the gap as before, crawled to the herb garden and pressed a handprint into the square for the thirteenth night in the night-watch book.

---

For the half month after that, Shen Xiaoman kept the night watch with a broken rib.

By day he went to the lecture hall as usual and carried water as usual, one load, three rests, not daring to change shoulders. In the dining hall Ma Xiaobao passed his table every day with an empty bowl, stopped every day, and poked that one spot with his finger. "Junior brother, any better?" At every poke everything went white in front of Xiaoman's eyes, and his mouth said, "Better, senior brother."

The handover was every day at the hour of the Rooster. Ahe came out of the garden with her hoe on her shoulder and gave him the day's numbers: which bed had put out new leaves, which plant had wilted, purple jade ginseng forty-one. At night he counted again, and at the hour of the Rabbit he gave the numbers back to her. For half a month the two of them never differed by a single plant. When she gave the numbers she didn't look at his face, only at the hand he held to his ribs; when she'd finished she took a boiled egg from her sleeve, set it on the stone step at the garden gate, shouldered her hoe and left.

At night he sat in the east corner of the herb garden beside Ahe's ring of red thread, in among the spirit herbs. His ribs hurt and he couldn't draw a breath all the way down, so he didn't draw it all the way down; he copied the child in the radish basket, belly rising, falling, shallow, the fall drawn long. The spirit qi the herbs breathed out at night was thick as the pot of porridge his mother boiled at New Year, and it settled breath by breath, and the ball of warmth down in his belly sank a hair's breadth lower each night. When he'd sat a long while, the leaves of the herbs nearest him all leaned his way, like sunflowers towards the sun. The little zheng crouched at his feet, facing the dark, not making a sound.

On the night of the fifteenth, there was a rustle on top of the wall, and a head appeared. Xiaoman didn't move. The person sat astride the wall, looking all round, and when he looked Xiaoman's way, he stopped.

It was Old Wilt.

Under the moon, the two of them looked at each other across half the garden for a long time. One sitting among the spirit herbs, his face still bruised; one astride the wall, holding an empty cloth sack. Old Wilt's lips moved, and nothing came out. Slowly he backed down off the wall; his foot slipped, there was a thud outside the wall, and then footsteps going away with a limp.

After it got light, Xiaoman found a piece of oiled paper in the grass at the foot of the wall, folded into a square, still smelling a little sweet, and on its corner was a red stamp: the stamp of that pastry shop in the county town. In the whole of the Sunken Cauldron Abbey, only one person could afford that shop's osmanthus cake. He tucked the oiled paper into the night-watch book.

---

On the twenty-second of the tenth month, just after dawn, the woodshed door was kicked open. Ma Xiaobao stood in the doorway, with Zhou Heng behind him, and behind him his followers; the one with black cloth patched on the toe of his shoe was there too. On the long bed they heard the noise, and a row of heads poked out of the window. Ma Xiaobao flung aside the heap of straw the little zheng slept in, and under the straw, plain as day, lay a purple jade ginseng, its leaves still fresh. "The most valuable thing in the herb garden," Ma Xiaobao said. "An animal's an animal; it can't stop stealing. The night watchman keeping a thief. Senior Brother Zhou, how will you write that up?"

Zhou Heng opened his ledger. "Purple jade ginseng, start of the month, forty-two." Xiaoman said, "I count every night. Forty-one." Ma Xiaobao clapped his hands. "Hear that! One short, and the night watchman doesn't report it!"

Xiaoman crouched down, picked up the ginseng and looked at the cut. On the ninth of the tenth month, the spirit herb chewed off at the west end of the garden had a level cut, but a ragged one: bitten off in one go, tooth marks line after line. This one's cut was smooth, a single slanting line, flat as a mirror. "Senior brother," he said, handing the ginseng to Zhou Heng, "a zheng gnaws with its teeth. This one was cut with a knife."

Ma Xiaobao said, "Cut with a knife? That makes it easier still." He turned round to the row of heads at the window and raised his voice. "The leaves are still fresh, it was cut last night. Last night there was only one person in the herb garden, the night watchman. Stealing what he's meant to guard, cutting it to feed his animal, and what the animal couldn't finish it hid in its nest. Senior Brother Zhou, whose should this token be?"

The row of heads at the window all looked at Xiaoman. Xiaoman opened his mouth. Last night there had been only him; that was true. And his "I count every night", said now, would only sound like covering for himself.

"It wasn't last night."

Ahe was standing outside the door, nobody knew since when, the little hoe on her shoulder, dew on her trouser legs. She had gone into the garden at the hour of the Rabbit, seen a group of people heading for the woodshed from the garden gate, and taken the night-watch book down off the gate on her way. She leaned the hoe against the door frame, opened her little notebook and said to Zhou Heng, "Senior Brother Zhou, the herb garden is mine by day. I count every day." She read it out page by page, flatly, as if reading someone else's accounts. "Eighth of the tenth month, hour of the Rooster, finished work, purple jade ginseng forty-two. Ninth, hour of the Dragon, started work, forty-one. Tenth, forty-one. Eleventh, forty-one... Yesterday, hour of the Rooster, forty-one." She turned the notebook round to show Zhou Heng. "None went last night. The one that's missing went between the hour of the Rooster on the eighth and the hour of the Dragon on the ninth."

Zhou Heng said, "The night of the eighth was Junior Brother Shen's first night on watch."

Ahe said, "In the night-watch book, that square has two names in it." She turned the book to the square for the eighth: one handprint, and beside it a line in big characters, the ink thick and black: **Ma Xiaobao kept watch with him. Hour of the Rat to hour of the Ox.**

It went quiet in the woodshed. Ma Xiaobao's face went red first, then white, then green, and sweat dripped from his forehead bead by bead. "I went to keep him company! The three of us, we just sat on the steps..."

Ahe said, "Mm. At the hour of the Dragon on the ninth, there was a food box left on the steps." From her notebook she shook out a leaf, pressed flat, so purple it was nearly black, a silver thread in its veins. "A bit of wet mud in the bottom of the box, and this. I picked it up, kept it in my notebook, and wrote it down." She held the leaf up against the ginseng in Zhou Heng's hand. One leaf was missing from the ginseng's stem, and the break matched exactly.

At the window someone drew out a long "Ohhh..." and quickly clapped a hand over his mouth. The follower with black cloth patched on his shoe shrank half a step behind the others. The little zheng crept out from Xiaoman's feet, twitched its nose twice at him, and went tock. Ma Xiaobao's mouth opened, shut, opened again, like a fish flung up on a bank. One more word, and who had gone into the garden with a food box that night would have to be named, one by one.

But Ahe closed her notebook, her voice still flat. "Maybe that night one of the senior brothers found it lying in the garden, was afraid it would get lost, kept it safe for the garden, and brought it back this morning. To the wrong nest." She looked at Ma Xiaobao. "Isn't that right, Senior Brother Ma?"

Ma Xiaobao glared at her, his eyeballs nearly popping out. After a long while he squeezed out through his teeth, "...That's how it was."

Zhou Heng looked at Ahe, looked at Ma Xiaobao, and tucked the ginseng into his sleeve. "Brought back. Not entered in the accounts." He left with his ledger in his arms. Ma Xiaobao flung his sleeve and left with his people, and passing the door he kicked over the water bucket beside it and soaked half of one of his own trouser legs. The row of heads drew back from the window, and nobody said it out loud.

Xiaoman waited until they had gone a long way off before he said, "You knew on the ninth?"

Ahe said, "I knew."

"Then why didn't you say?"

"On the ninth, saying it is telling tales. Today, saying it is checking the accounts." She tucked the leaf back into her notebook. "He brought the ginseng to the door himself. That's what gave me accounts to check."

"Then why did you let him down easy?"

Ahe put the hoe on her shoulder. "Show him up and he can't climb down. If he can't climb down, his uncle will have to come down."

Month's end was six days off.

---

On the night of the twenty-fifth of the tenth month, sitting among the spirit herbs, a breath settled and there was a thud down in his belly. The ball of warmth suddenly sank, sank deeper, like ground dry all winter where one swing of the mattock turns up another layer beneath; once it was broken open, what it could hold had suddenly doubled. Second layer of Qi Condensation.

He had sat it out himself, night after night, with a broken rib.

---

On the twenty-eighth of the tenth month, Zhou Heng did the month's-end accounts, and by the time he reached the herb-garden page it was already dusk. Xiaoman waited at the door of the stewards' office; Ma Xiaobao was there too, followed by the one with black cloth on his shoe, and Old Wilt, who stood outside the threshold. Zhou Heng read out, his finger on the ledger: "Tenth, entered one spirit beast. From the tenth to today, lost from the herb garden..." He looked up at the one in the doorway, then down again. "Not one thing lost."

Xiaoman drew out the oiled paper tucked in the night-watch book and laid it on the ledger. "On the night of the fifteenth, someone came up on the wall, took a look, and went back. At the foot of the wall, this was dropped." A red stamp on its corner. Outside the threshold, Old Wilt's face went white at once. Xiaoman didn't look at him.

Ma Xiaobao said, "A bit of oiled paper, who knows where it was picked up?" He stepped inside and pointed at Xiaoman. "Zero? That's because he ate them himself! Senior Brother Zhou, a leaky ladle, a month through the gate, sitting every night in a pile of spirit herbs with the leaves all leaning towards him: he's been stealing spirit herbs to eat, and he's eaten himself up a layer!"

Xiaoman said, "How does senior brother know the leaves lean towards me?"

The stewards' office went quiet. Ma Xiaobao stood with his mouth open, looked at Zhou Heng, looked at the roof beams, and it took a long while before he got his breath back. His voice went higher, if anything. "...Get him checked! Get Master Chu to come and see whether he's on the first layer or the second!"

That line was a real knife. A leaky ladle on the second layer in a month and a half: let that get around, and without Ma Xiaobao saying another word, the whole outer sect would fill in the rest for him.

Xiaoman didn't look at Ma Xiaobao. He said to Zhou Heng, "Senior brother, ask Master Chu to come and check."

Master Chu was fetched over from the lecture hall, his goatee twitching, very impatient. He didn't waste words. He clamped a hand round Xiaoman's wrist. A thread of cool spiritual power slipped in at his wrist and went probing inward along his meridians. Xiaoman shut his eyes. He didn't dodge it and he didn't press it down; he let it probe. Wherever Master Chu's spiritual power touched, it was as if an iron brush had been dragged across it; when it touched his ribs, the pain whited out everything in front of his eyes.

Master Chu probed for a good cup of tea's time before he let go, and frowned. "Second layer." Ma Xiaobao clapped his hands. "What did I say!" Master Chu ignored him and pressed on Xiaoman's ribs, and when he reached the one on the left, his hand stopped. Xiaoman made no sound; sweat dripped from his forehead bead by bead.

"This bone is still broken," Master Chu said. "Half a month." He turned to Ma Xiaobao. "The spirit herbs in that garden are the best thing there is for healing. If he'd eaten even one, this bone would have knitted long ago." Zhou Heng opened the night-watch book and pushed it in front of him: one handprint a night, from the tenth on, not a night missing. Master Chu turned two pages and said, "In a pile of spirit herbs the spirit qi is thick. A leaky ladle sitting in there leaks half and still keeps half. Sit there half a month, and you sit out a layer..." He snorted. "A leaky ladle is still a leaky ladle."

Ma Xiaobao was halfway through swallowing a mouthful of osmanthus cake, and it caught in his throat. He doubled over coughing and didn't cough out a single word.

Master Chu went to the door, then stopped, turned back, and took one more look at Xiaoman, at the hand he held to his ribs. In the end he asked nothing and left.

Zhou Heng shut the night-watch book, slid it under the ledger and said, "By the accounts, not by faces. Herb-garden night watch: Shen Xiaoman. Pay: dead stalks, one basket a month." From under the table he lifted a bamboo basket, and in it were this month's pulled-up dead stalks: insect-bitten, grown crooked, their spirit qi half gone, a small half basket of them.

Xiaoman carried the basket out of the stewards' office and stood holding on to the wall for a while: Master Chu's cup of tea with the iron brush had left his side aching in waves. The chilli-oil bottle in his coat dug through his clothes into that bone that hadn't healed.

At lamp-lighting time, Ma Xiaobao's uncle, the name-calling steward, opened the outer sect register and under "Shen Xiaoman" added four small characters: herb-garden night watch. When he'd added them, the tip of his brush paused on the "Man" of the name above. On the day of the name-calling, the ninth of the ninth month, his brush had been running dry, and one downstroke had broken off halfway. He dipped in a little ink, joined up the broken half, and didn't say a word.

---

That night, by the well in the outer sect's back courtyard, Ma Xiaobao squatted alone, washing his chin with well water. He had three roots, colour murky, and his father couldn't wait: in one month he had force-fed him three bottles of Qi-Gathering Pills, and fed out of him a first layer of Qi Condensation, and also fed three little black spots onto his chin. He scrubbed with soapberry, he rubbed with the coarse sand off the well's edge, rubbed his chin bright red, and the three black spots were still there. He took the porcelain bottle of Qi-Gathering Pills from his coat, turned it upside down and knocked it into his palm. Out came three: the last three left this month, one line each, a ring of black specks along the line, exactly like the ones on his chin.

He thought of last autumn at the Shens' door, making Shen Xiaoman bark like a dog, and Shen Xiaoman saying "I'm learning," and how he'd laughed about it for days. Thinking of it now, it wasn't funny at all. He thought of that broken bone of Shen Xiaoman's, of the oiled paper on Zhou Heng's ledger, of that one Foundation Pill in the twelfth month. When people in the lecture hall talked about Shen Xiaoman, they said "the one who took in the zheng", not "the leaky ladle". He thought: what makes him so special? Then he thought: he eats one mouthful, I eat three.

He swallowed the three pills in one go, without even water, dry, his neck jerking as they went down. The warmth rose, hot and heavy, burning his whole face red, burning the three black spots on his chin till they throbbed. He gritted his teeth and dug his nails into a crack in the stones of the well's edge and waited for it to pass. He stood up, wiped his mouth, took another piece of osmanthus cake from his sleeve and bit into it. Still hungry.

Early next morning, a new notice was pasted up on the outer sect's notice board, the ink not yet dry:

**The Sunken Cauldron Secret Realm opens once in ten years. First day of the eleventh month. Disciples of Qi Condensation, inner and outer sect alike, may enter.**

---

The night before the secret realm, Xiaoman squatted in the woodshed doing the accounts over the Rice Bucket. Ma Xiaobao was going in too. He had no sword, no charms, not a single spirit stone left, only a bellyful of spirit qi: the second layer he had sat into on the twenty-fifth of the tenth month, sat full these last few nights. Second layer, at its peak.

He pressed both hands on the bronze belly. "Give me something that'll keep me alive."

He thought: the more I feed it, the better what comes out ought to be. So he didn't stop. At thirty-odd mouthfuls his dantian ran dry, and it was still sucking, glug glug, and there was no holding it back. Suddenly there was a crack at the bottom of the vessel, and he went cold all over, like missing a step on the stairs: the layer he had sat out in the herb garden with a broken rib, it had sucked away, bottom and all. First layer of Qi Condensation.

Click. The lid sprang open.

Crouched in its belly was a little wooden animal, long neck, small head, a coat of curly wool, two blank eyes, like a sheep and also like a camel. Branded under its belly were four characters, crooked: **Wooden Grass-Mud Horse**; and below them a line in small writing: Knows its master. Fiery temper; keep away from flame. He lifted it out and set it on the ground. Its belly went tick, tick inside and gave off a smell like New Year firecrackers. It lowered its head, clip-clop, two steps, and butted him right in the shin.

Xiaoman yelped, ow, and hopped away on one leg clutching his shin. It chased him. He ran round the chopping block; it ran round the chopping block after him; the little zheng ran after it. Seven or eight times round, then Xiaoman grabbed a broken wicker basket and slammed it down over it, caught it, and sat on the basket panting, and as he panted he glared at the four characters under its belly and read them out one by one, as if cursing: "Wooden... Grass... Mud... Horse." He tied its four legs with straw rope and stuffed it into the bundle he'd be carrying tomorrow. At least it was something that moved.

He sat back down on the basket and felt his dantian over, this way and that, and however he felt it, there was only one layer left. His eyes went red.

Yinyue crept out and crouched on the rim of the basket. First, without looking at him, she unhurriedly smoothed the tuft of fur on her chest with a paw.

"What are you crying for?" She lifted her chin and aimed her nostrils at him. "Country boy. Never seen anything of the world. This wretched bucket: feed it firewood and of course it spits out toothpicks; feed it a shoe sole and of course it plasters your face. This queen told you: feed it something immortal and you get something immortal."

The tip of her tail curled up into a hook.

"This queen has one more. The very last one. A treasure of the immortal realm. From the Supreme Old Lord's own pill house, this queen..." She coughed. "...took it. A Nine-Turn Soul-Return Pill. The day its box was opened, every crane in the hall craned its neck."

Xiaoman: "...Took it?"

Yinyue: "Robbed it."

She stretched out her neck, hrrk, and a pill dropped into her paw: the size of a longan, nine gold lines winding round it one after another, turning slowly round the body of the pill by themselves, warm in the palm.

The musty smell of the woodshed, the smell of pine resin, the rank smell of the little zheng, whoosh, all gone at once.

A cricket frozen stiff in a crack in the wall went chirp, and came alive.

The little zheng's five tails shot up together, flick.

Xiaoman's mouth opened into a round you could have stuffed an egg into.

Yinyue weighed the pill on the tip of her claw a couple of times, eyelids half lowered, and slid a glance at his face. That glance plainly said: bumpkin, watch closely; if this queen doesn't show you a thing or two, you'll think this queen of demons is a fake.

"Watch."

A flick of the paw.

The pill drew a snow-white arc through the air, trailing a wisp of immortal qi behind it, and slow... ly... fell into the Rice Bucket's belly.

Crunch.

The bronze belly lit up.

First a thread of gold showed through under the verdigris; the thread became a patch, the patch became a glow, so bright that the shadows on all four walls of the woodshed shrank back together. A wisp of purple smoke crept out of the gap under the lid, and another, and another, circling the Rice Bucket, going round into one ring, then a second ring.

The small beast's eyes on the lid opened rounder and rounder, round as two bronze nails.

Then there was a sound inside.

Not chewing. Like a breath, in a very deep place, slowly being drawn in.

Xiaoman held his breath. Yinyue held hers too: her two front paws were still folded across her chest, her chin still raised, but her eyes had already turned and fixed on that bronze belly. The little zheng lay flat on the ground, its five tails pressed to the floor, not daring to move. The cricket in the crack in the wall had stopped chirping too.

The purple smoke went round and round. The gold light brightened and dimmed, as if breathing.

Nobody knew how long it went on. The candle wick formed a snuff, pop, and nobody trimmed it. It seemed like a stick of incense.

The purple smoke, wisp by wisp, drew back into the gap under the lid. The gold light, inch by inch, sank back under the verdigris.

Click.

Three heads leaned over together.

In the belly lay a pill. Dusty grey. One line. A ring of black specks along the line.

A Qi-Gathering Pill. Exactly the same as the bottle under Ma Xiaobao's pillow: the kind the shop in the county town sold by the bottle.

It was quiet in the woodshed for a long time.

Yinyue's folded paws slowly dropped.

Xiaoman's jaw hit his own chest, clunk.

The little zheng went over and sniffed it, sneezed, and walked away.

Then Yinyue burst out wailing. When a queen of demons cries she's no different from a three-year-old, and as she cried she flung herself at the Rice Bucket, clung to it with all four paws, and scratched at the bronze belly till it squealed, scratching white marks line after line. "Fake! Fake! You black-hearted pot! Give this queen back her Soul-Return Pill! When this queen was in heaven, a wretched pot like you wasn't fit to boil this queen's tea..." The small beast on the lid turned its backside to her, burped, and puffed out a cloud of black smoke, pff, right in her face. Silver fur, smoked grey.

Xiaoman had wanted to cry as well. But he looked at the grey pill, looked at the fox with her face all black with soot, and the corner of his mouth twitched, and pff, he burst out laughing, and the more he laughed the less he could stop, rolling off the basket and rolling about on the woodpile, gasping at the pain in his ribs, still laughing. He pinched his voice to copy her: "Watch... this... queen." And again: "Last time, 'this queen will start you off', and out came a hell-for-leather charm; this time, 'watch this queen', an immortal's pill robbed from the Supreme Old Lord, and back comes something they sell by the bottle in a shop in the county town." He laughed so hard he was slapping the floor. "I'd have done better throwing in a stick of firewood. At least a stick gets you a toothpick."

When he'd laughed enough, he swallowed the Qi-Gathering Pill. The door of the second layer pushed open again, and inside it was empty, bare, just as it had been when he first sat into it on the twenty-fifth. He hugged his knees and mumbled, "Second layer, peak... my second layer, peak..."

Yinyue wiped the soot from her face and at last found something to answer back to. "...The second layer of Qi Condensation has a peak?" She paused. "So a bumpkin's molehill calls itself a summit too." She crept back into the tablet and didn't come out again all night.

In the bundle, the wooden animal's belly went tick, tick, all night long.
