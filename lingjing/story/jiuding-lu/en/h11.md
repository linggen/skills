# Chapter Seven: Nine Squares on the Clifftop: He Learns the Step of Yu / One Pill in the Pill House: No Fire in It

On the ninth of the twelfth month, the second day of the Grand Contest, Shen Xiaoman rolled up his bedding and moved out of the outer sect's long common bunk at the Sunken Cauldron Abbey and into the inner sect.

There wasn't much to move: one quilt, one bundle, one bow, and a storage pouch at his waist. The outer sect's common bunk was a row of dozens of sleeping places, one against the next; if anyone turned over in the night, half the room woke with him. He had slept on it for three months, in the place nearest the door, with the wind from the crack pouring in all night. The inner sect's rooms were along a long corridor west of the main hall, called the West Gallery: one room, four beds. Proper beds, with real boards, not a door laid across two benches. There was a window in the wall, freshly papered, and at the foot of each bed a wooden chest with a lock on it. Two old inner-sect senior brothers lived in the room, one in his thirties, one past forty, both stuck at the ninth layer for years. When he came in, one of them lifted his eyelids; the other didn't even do that.

The bed by the window was empty, bright and warm. Xiaoman stood in the doorway looking at it for a while, then set his bedding down on the one by the door.

The first night, the room was so quiet he couldn't sleep. On the common bunk dozens of people snored, ground their teeth and talked in their sleep, and he was used to it; here there were only two, and they breathed evenly, one breath in, one breath out, frighteningly long. They were breathing out and in even asleep. He held the wooden tablet from his chest in his fist, both hands crossed over it, and didn't let go even in sleep, like an old hen sitting on an egg. On the day of the contest, when the Ma family's mirror shone on him, something had glowed under the hand he pressed to his chest. The mirror had cracked; the person at the other end of the mirror hadn't. From now on this piece of wood had to be kept closer than ever.

Early next morning, Zhou Heng issued him a new robe in the stewards' office. The outer sect's robes were grey and cut for big men; he had worn his for three months, the hem dragging on his feet, the cuffs frayed, and he had mended it twice himself with the needle and thread Mother gave him. The inner sect's were blue cloth Daoist robes, cut to fit. Zhou Heng measured his shoulders with a piece of string, then his legs, wrote two numbers in the ledger, and pulled one out of the cupboard, blue enough to shine, with a white border round the collar. Xiaoman changed into it and looked down at himself for a long time, then folded the grey robe into a neat square and pressed it to the bottom of the chest at the foot of his bed.

Zhou Heng opened the ledger again and read it out to him, the way he would read anybody's accounts: "Inner-sect allowance: ten spirit stones a month. Three Qi-Gathering Pills a month, one line. One candle a month. The inner-sect dining hall, with oil in the vegetables." He shut the ledger. "That's it."

Xiaoman did the sum in his head: ten spirit stones and three pills, three outer disciples' allowances stacked together, still less than his alone. He had fought his way into the inner sect half dead, and this was the prize. He held the cuff of the new robe and wanted to laugh, and was too embarrassed to. Zhou Heng seemed to see what he was adding up, and said, "The allowance doesn't grow with you. What you're short, you earn yourself: take a tablet at the Stewards' Hall, go down the mountain, fight demons." He didn't go on. "After New Year, go and look for yourself."

At noon that day he went into the inner-sect dining hall for the first time. The rice was the same coarse rice, the vegetable the same radish, except that a film of oil floated on the radish, shining. With his chopsticks he moved those few drops of oil one by one onto his rice, mixed them in, and ate very slowly.

---

During the days of the move he turned his storage pouch upside down and sorted through everything, and sorted out two pieces of junk: Zou Qingsong's red-copper gourd, its mouth open and its belly empty, and the seven or eight shards of the red-copper shield, a small jingling heap.

He slipped into the woodshed, opened the Rice Bucket and tipped the lot in, thinking nothing at all. Good riddance.

The Rice Bucket chewed for a long time. *Crunch*, *crunch*: it chewed a while, rested a while, like a toothless old man gnawing a frozen flatbread. Each time it rested it drew a breath from him, and by the fifth breath his sight was going dark in waves. At last the lid sprang open with a *click*, a red light shot out, and with a *thock* it stuck fast in the door, the hilt still humming. Xiaozheng dived headfirst into the woodpile.

He pulled the sword out. All it had left in the door was one crack, running straight down the grain to the bottom, clean as if planed. A sword, about three feet long, the blade dark red, the copper colour of the gourd and the shield, with not one fire pattern left on it. Along the spine ran a row of hammer marks, pitted and dented, like something a blacksmith's apprentice in town had botched. He put a thread of spiritual power into it, and a thin layer of light rose on the blade, like a skin of oil: a magic tool, the most ordinary kind. On the spine near the hilt, among the hammer marks, two crooked characters were cut: Matchstick. Below them, a line of small writing: The sword rises, the fire climbs; good for attack and defence.

---

On the fifteenth of the twelfth month, Elder Qu was waiting for him at the kitchen door.

That day Xiaoman came back carrying water, and Elder Qu stood there with his broom on his shoulder. He looked at the bow on Xiaoman's back, then at the new sword at his waist, and said, "Come with me." With that he shouldered the broom and walked off towards the back mountain. Xiaoman set down the buckets and followed.

On the clifftop there was still the same grass hut, the same bluestone, the same incense burner. Elder Qu didn't go into the hut. He took a handful of something from inside his robe and scattered it on the bluestone: nine chestnuts, roasted, the shells still scorched black. He said, "This, I know you can lay out."

Xiaoman squatted down and laid them out anyway, his hands quicker than his head. Nine on the head, one on the feet, three on the left, seven on the right, two and four the shoulders, six and eight the feet, five in the centre. The nine chestnuts were in three rows on the bluestone in the blink of an eye. He had been six when Mother laid out marked chestnuts for him under the oil lamp; these nine had no marks, and he didn't count, and one by one they all fell where they should.

Elder Qu waited till he was done, then started drawing on the bluestone with the broom handle. The handle was worn bald and left no ink, only faint white scratches on the stone: three lines across, three lines down, nine squares, the same size as the chestnuts' layout. He touched each of the outer eight squares once: "Kan, Kun, Zhen, Xun, Qian, Dui, Gen, Li." The middle square he didn't touch. "The centre palace stays empty."

Xiaoman said, "Master, why is the middle empty?" Elder Qu said, "Whichever square you stand in, that square is the centre palace."

"Eight words, and each word is one thing: water, mountain, thunder, wind; and earth, heaven, marsh, fire," said Elder Qu. "You needn't remember the words, only the things. Once you know the things, when you fight someone later, you'll be able to see which of them the person facing you is just then."

"This square is Kan, and Kan is water," said Elder Qu. "Water runs downhill; wherever there's a hollow, it pours in. Put your hand out to stop it and it goes round through the gaps in your fingers—" He untied the water gourd at his waist, pulled the stopper, poured a mouthful into his palm and flicked his fingers. A handful of drops flew at Xiaoman's face. Xiaoman said "Ah!" and threw up a hand; he stopped two or three, and his face still came away wet, ice-cold. Elder Qu said, "—you can't even dodge it. In a fight, if the man facing you is water, he won't meet you head on. He looks for your gaps and pours in. Wherever you're empty, that's where he'll be."

"This square is Gen, and Gen is mountain. A mountain has only one skill: it doesn't move." He turned the broom upside down, bald end first, planted it on the bluestone and let go. The broom simply stood there, not tilting, not falling. "Come and push it." Xiaoman went over and pushed with one hand; it didn't move. He pushed with two hands; it still didn't move. He pressed the spiritual power in his dantian down into his arms and pushed with everything he had, and the broom handle stood like a stump growing out of the stone, not stirring a hair. Inside his robe someone went "Hmph," and Xiaoman could tell it was a laugh. Elder Qu said, "Can't push it, can you? If your opponent has made himself a mountain, don't push him. You'll push till dark and he won't move. You have to go round."

He took the broom back and said, "This square is Zhen, and Zhen is thunder." As he said it he tapped the bluestone lightly with the broom handle. "*Dang*—" The sound wasn't loud, but it came back up from the bottom of the cliff, layer on layer, one after another, as though someone were beating a drum in the mountain's belly. Xiaoman's heart gave a jump, and his hand was already on his sword hilt. Elder Qu said, "You see? The heart jumps first, then the hand moves. That's thunder: it comes fast and frightens you first. The first move in a fight is often a clap of thunder. If it frightens you, you won't catch the moves after it."

He snapped a dry leaf off the broom head. "This square is Xun, and Xun is wind. Wind is soft, but it gets in everywhere: the crack of a door, the crack of a window, your cuffs." He held the leaf up in front of his eyes and let go. The leaf floated down. "Try cutting it with your sword." Xiaoman drew Matchstick and swung a cut across. Before the blade got there, the leaf was pushed aside by the wind the sword raised, spun and drifted three feet away, and landed outside the squares. He cut again, and again the leaf drifted off. He chased it for four or five cuts, didn't touch it once, and cut himself into a sweat.

Elder Qu said, "You can't cut the wind. However much strength you put in, that much it moves aside; your strength falls on nothing, and you're the one who gets tired." He picked the leaf up, held it up again and let go. As it floated down, he followed its path down slowly with the broom handle, and when the leaf reached the ground the handle reached the ground too, and the leaf landed right on its tip. "You have to go with it. Follow it."

Xiaoman said, "Master, of the eight words in the nine palaces, why teach Xun first?"

Elder Qu sat down at the edge of the bluestone and said, "Two reasons. You're from Xu Province, and Xun four is the Xu Province palace; that one you know." With the broom handle he drew three lines in the Xun square: the bottom one broken, the two above it whole. "The second: look at it. Empty below, solid above. The soft is underneath, the hard on top. The softest thing under heaven can get in anywhere."

He recited a line from a book, slowly, as if reading out an old list: "The *Daodejing*{典=laozi-zhirou} says: 'The softest thing under heaven gallops through the hardest. What has no substance enters where there is no gap.'" When he'd finished, he touched the leaf with the broom handle. "Water is like that, wind is like that. Xun is one word: enter."

The voice inside his robe spoke again, and this time it wasn't a hmph, it was words: "The one who rode the blue ox? Talks too much." Xiaoman pressed a hand on his chest. Elder Qu glanced at his chest and didn't ask.

Elder Qu stood up, walked into the middle one of the nine squares and said, "Come at me."

Xiaoman froze for a moment. Elder Qu said, "Use your sword, any way you like. I'll use only this broom, and I won't leave the nine squares."

Xiaoman gripped Matchstick and thrust. The sword had barely moved when the broom handle touched his shoulder, lightly, like a knock on a door. He switched sides and thrust again; the broom handle touched the shoulder on that side. He thrust seven or eight times, each faster than the last, each trickier than the last, and that broom handle was always half a beat ahead of his sword, and always touched the same place: his shoulder. Elder Qu stood in the centre palace and never so much as shifted his feet.

"Your shoulder moves first," said Elder Qu. "The moment your shoulder moves, you've told me yourself where the sword is going."

Xiaoman wouldn't accept it. He brought out the hunter's tricks: he kicked up a spray of powdered snow into the old man's face and ducked low under it, the sword going for the legs. The broom handle came out of the snow and touched his shoulder. He tried a feint, the sword swinging left while he went right. The broom handle was already waiting on the right, and this time it didn't touch his shoulder. It touched his forehead.

Xiaoman stopped, panting hard. Elder Qu said, "Again."

This time Xiaoman didn't attack. He stood outside the squares and looked at his master, looked for a long time: at the master's shoulders, at the master's feet. The master moved nothing. So he didn't move either.

Elder Qu moved. He took only one step, from the centre palace into the Xun square. The step was extremely slow, so slow that Xiaoman saw it perfectly clearly, and he couldn't get out of its way. The broom handle slid along his blade to the hilt and gave a push inward, and Xiaoman went over backwards and sat down hard in the snow. Matchstick was still in his hand, but half his wrist had gone numb.

Elder Qu stood in the Xun square and said, "This step is called entering."

Xiaoman sat in the snow and looked up at his master. Elder Qu held out the broom handle and pulled him up.

"You can lay out the nine squares, but walking them is something else," said Elder Qu. "Follow my feet and walk them once."

Xiaoman had never seen anyone walk the way Elder Qu walked that step. The left foot went first, the right foot came round behind the left, and the left caught up with the right and closed beside it: three movements, and that made one step. Step after step, winding and crooked, like a lame man picking out the dry spots in the mud. Elder Qu walked very slowly, went through all nine squares, stood in the centre palace and said, "This is called the Step of Yu. When Great Yu tamed the floods he spent thirteen years walking the Nine Provinces, till his legs rotted from the water and he went lame, and he limped out this step: three steps, two zhang one chi, nine footprints on the ground. People after him walked on his lame leg, and walked the road he had walked." He paused. "He got the Luo Writing out of the Luo River, he cast the Nine Cauldrons, and these nine squares are his too."

Xiaoman walked it after him. The first time, when his right foot came round the left, he tripped himself. The second time, he didn't. The third time, he went through all nine squares and stood in the centre palace without a sound: a hunter walks without a sound anyway, the foot coming down toe first, on a spot already looked at.

Elder Qu watched him finish and said nothing. He turned, took the broom from the corner by the wall, and began sweeping the powdered snow off the bluestone. After two strokes, with his back to Xiaoman, he said, "Hunter's feet."

Xiaoman didn't know whether that was praise or not, so he stood there. When Elder Qu had finished sweeping he said, "I'll leave you some homework. Every day at first light, go and stand in the Xun square, and stand there until the leaves on the clifftop stop spinning."

Xiaoman said, "How long do I stand before the leaves stop spinning?"

Elder Qu said, "When the wind stops, they'll stop. You get still first. The wind will come by itself."

As Xiaoman went down the mountain he looked back once. Elder Qu stood by the bluestone, looking down at the nine chestnuts, looking at them longer than at anything else. The two for "two and four the shoulders" were set a little crooked compared with the rest, tilted inwards, both of them, like a man hunching his shoulders. Elder Qu said nothing, and put the nine chestnuts one by one into his sleeve.

---

On New Year's Eve the abbey was half empty: those who lived near had all taken leave to go home for the New Year. That night he turned fourteen.

Zhou Heng broke his rule that night. In the stewards' office he lit a whole candle, not half, a whole one, called in the few disciples who'd stayed on the mountain, and gave each of them a bowl of dumplings. Cabbage filling, twelve dumplings to a bowl, no more, no fewer. Zhou Heng sat behind the table and watched them eat, and didn't touch one himself.

When everyone had finished they thanked him one by one and left. But Zhou Heng stood up, tucked the ledger under his arm and said, "Shen Xiaoman, wash the bowls." With that he went out towards the main hall.

Xiaoman washed the seven bowls, and only then noticed a ledger left on the corner of the table. Very old, a size smaller than the one Zhou Heng usually carried, its cover rubbed shiny. Zhou Heng recorded even how many pieces a candle was broken into, and had never left anything behind; and tonight of all nights he'd left this, lying open.

The page it was open at had gone completely yellow, and it was all small odds and ends: "beans, two sheng, fourteen coppers", "salt, half a jin", "Sister's hair string, three coppers". The writing was crooked and wobbly; one look told you it was a child's hand. At the end of the page there was only one line, but it was written so hard the strokes had gone through the paper:

**Eighteenth of the tenth month. The Zhang River. Sister.**

Xiaoman looked at it a good while before he worked out what was wrong. Every line in Zhou Heng's accounts was followed by a number: one herb, three spirit stones, half a candle. Only this line had nothing after it.

He shut the ledger, tucked it inside his robe and went to the main hall to give it back. The door of the hall stood ajar, and a lamp was lit inside. He had just reached the gallery when he heard Zhou Heng's voice. He had never heard that voice before: it didn't sound like keeping accounts, it sounded like begging. "...Abbot, this year, let me go."

The Abbot's voice was gentle. "It's ten days' road. What's in that river isn't something a Qi Condensation cultivator can deal with. If you go, the Zhou family will be one fewer again." After a while he went on: "That business in Ye: they call it finding a bride for the River Lord, but really someone is using the River Lord's name to squeeze money out of the common people every year, and behind the people squeezing stands a great sect. If one of the abbey's elders goes, that's the Sunken Cauldron Abbey falling out with them. If a disciple who came up from the outer sect takes a tablet and goes, that's only an errand. But whoever goes has to have Foundation Establishment, or he won't come back. In the outer sect, no one reached Foundation Establishment this year."

The hall was silent a long time. Zhou Heng said, "...Yes. Next year, I'll come again."

Xiaoman shrank back behind a pillar of the gallery. Zhou Heng came out of the hall, walking very slowly. Xiaoman held out the old ledger: "You left this." Zhou Heng took it, looked at him and said, "Did you read it?" Xiaoman said, "...Senior brother Zhou has good handwriting." Zhou Heng put the ledger inside his robe and walked away.

Xiaoman stood in the gallery a while. The eighteenth of the tenth month, the Zhang River, someone with Foundation Establishment: those three things he kept.

Later he heard about Zhou Heng from Steward Deng. Zhou Heng was from Ye. His father died early, and at home there was only him and an older sister. The year he was eight and his sister fifteen, the shamans of the River Lord's temple picked her, saying she was the bride the River Lord fancied. On the eighteenth of the tenth month, he chased that boat along the bank of the Zhang River for two li; the boat reached the middle of the river, tipped, and was gone. The next year he climbed Mount Meng with a bundle of firewood on his back, came into the Sunken Cauldron Abbey, and had stayed nearly twenty years. "It's not revenge he's after," said Steward Deng. "Every year that river still takes one more. He's kept accounts all his life, and that's the one entry he can't balance."

---

On the first day of the first month, spirit stones were handed out, and Zhou Heng sat behind the table at the dining-hall door giving them out. Xiaoman had gone into the inner sect's register, and by rights his outer-sect duty should be handed back, so he untied the night-watch token from his waist and set it on the table. Zhou Heng didn't even look at it, and pushed it back with one finger: "It'll only sit empty." After a pause he added, "Keep wearing it. If the garden's one plant short, the Discipline Hall asks you first."

Zhou Heng took another tablet out of the drawer and slapped it on the table. This one wasn't ebony but pine, a hand long, carved with three characters: Stewards' Hall. "Inner-sect people have one more thing on their account: errands. The errand tablets hang in the Stewards' Hall. Take one, do one job; when it's done, the tablet is turned over and you get paid the figure on it. Chores, the outer sect can take too; buying and escort, the inner sect takes." He paused. "The red tablets, for fighting demons: below the fifth layer, you may not take them."

The Stewards' Hall was west of the main hall, an empty room with no statues of the gods, only two walls.

The tablets on the east wall were ebony, all black, a hand long, the writing filled in with white chalk: "Kitchen · Helping", "Mountain Path · Carrying Water", "Scripture Library · Copying Sutras", "Pill House · Tending the Furnace", "Beast Pens · Feeding Spirit Beasts", "Talisman Room · Mending Talisman Paper". These tablets didn't involve fighting; they were outer-sect chores, anyone could take them. You took one and went to Zhou Heng in the stewards' office for the job, and the pay wasn't spirit stones but merit, a line in Zhou Heng's ledger; some were paid in goods, and kitchen help got a crock of pickles a month. On the east wall one nail was empty, and the white chalk under it was still there: "Herb Garden · Night Watch". Xiaoman stopped in front of that empty nail for a moment. Last autumn Zhou Heng had said in the stewards' office, "Herb garden night watch, Shen Xiaoman. Pay: spoiled plants, a basket a month," and he had taken it for a hard job Zhou Heng had handed out on a whim. So that tablet had come off this wall, and had been hanging at his waist all winter.

The west wall belonged to the inner sect, and the tablets came in colours. The white wood ones were buying: "Red lingzhi, three. Shopkeeper Qian at the market takes them. One spirit stone." The yellow wood ones were escort: "A cart of Xiapi herb merchants, by the north road. Three spirit stones." The red wood ones were demon-killing: "Spirit Herb Valley, north of Mount Meng: a demon at night, sixth layer, no spirit wisdom yet, eats spirit herbs, hurts people. Twenty spirit stones each." "North road out of Xiapi: the mountain road eats people, three lives. Three spirit stones." The blue wood ones were finding people: "West village, Pei County: boy missing, seven years old, a mole on the left ear. Five qian of silver." There were a few ebony ones too, about abbey business: "Scripture Library, airing the books, three days. Merit recorded." One against the next, like a wall of teeth. Some were turned over, with a vermilion "Done" on the back and a small board nailed below with a few names. Under some of the names a black line was drawn.

On the topmost row of the wall hung a single black tablet, with no writing, only a place name carved on it: "The Si River · The Ferry". Xiaoman looked at it a long time and couldn't make sense of it.

The steward who kept the Stewards' Hall had one arm, was named Deng, and sat on the threshold weaving straw sandals without looking up: "Understand it?" Xiaoman said, "The black lines are..." Steward Deng said, "The tablet was taken and the man didn't come back." He turned the sandal over. "Thirty years of the inner sect: eleven lines." Xiaoman asked again, "The one at the very top—" Steward Deng said, "That's a black tablet. One a year goes up, two at most. It doesn't say what the job is. The job, I tell you face to face." He looked up and gave Xiaoman a look. "Come and look again after the ninth layer."

Xiaoman was at the fourth layer and couldn't take a red tablet. The white and yellow ones he could, but they never came to him. He stood under the two walls half a day, just watching people come and go. At first light a few old senior brothers came in, their eyes sweeping only over the white tablets, their hands faster than their eyes. A good white one, "Red lingzhi, three," came off and away, and what was left for those who came later was "Skullcap root, one basket", the kind where you ran your legs off for one spirit stone. Steward Deng called this "eating the tablets", and the ones who ate them he called old hands. Some came in and didn't glance at the white ones, only at the red, every one of them with a face more lined than Elder Qu's. Ask, and they were all stuck at the ninth layer for ten years or eight: they took red tablets, traded their lives for spirit stones, and traded the stones for pills. The black lines were mostly drawn under red tablets. Nobody took the blue ones; a "Finding a person" tablet hung there till it grew mould, and nobody took it down.

In the first month, the answer from home arrived. The market held its fair on days ending in nine, and the letter carrier from Mount Meng set up a little table outside Shopkeeper Qian's shop, taking letters and handing them out; his letters from home all came by that little table. Mother's writing was small and close: the roof was mended; the five taels of silver had arrived in the twelfth month, and your Pa hasn't gone up towards Black Pine Ridge since. The last line said: Granny Wu asked me to pass something on to you. She said, "Tell the boy the tooth's still there." I asked her what tooth, and she wouldn't say, only laughed.

---

At long last spring came and the herb garden opened. At the end of the month he took the night-watch log to the Stewards' Hall to report the job done (the east wall's errands were all collected at month's end by Zhou Heng, sitting under the east wall taking in the logs and paying out wages). Zhou Heng leafed through the log, wrote a line in his ledger, and pushed a basket of spoiled plants across: the wage written on the night-watch tablet. Rotten roots of solomon's seal, insect-eaten milkvetch leaves, crooked angelica seedlings, and at the bottom of the basket two broken whiskers of purple jade ginseng. He fed nearly all of it to the Rice Bucket, and most of what came out was ash and smoke, no use at all. He didn't mind; he took it as getting to know its temper. One plant fed, one plant noted, and as the notes piled up he felt he was slowly getting the hang of the Rice Bucket. In half a month he fed it half a basket and got three [Qi-Gathering Pills]{注=qi-pill}, blue-white and clear. The three looked alike, but their lines differed: one had one line, like the one a month in the outer-sect allowance; one had two lines; and the last one, which he counted three times against the light through the crack of the woodshed door, had a full nine.

Father said hunting wasn't luck, it was footprints. He sorted the half basket that was left into four heaps by kind, and squatting there he traced back half a month of footprints one by one. It took him all night, and he worked out three recipes: solomon's seal with one spirit stone gave a Qi-Gathering Pill; milkvetch with a breath of his own spiritual power gave a Spring-Return Pill; a broken whisker of purple jade ginseng with a breath of spiritual power gave a Qi-Return Pill, which, swallowed when your dantian was empty, poured most of a vat of spiritual power back in. Mixed any other way, it was still ash. A recipe decided which pill came out, not how many lines it had: the same root of solomon's seal, the same spirit stone, would give one line this time and two the next, and only every few months would a three or four jump out. A nine, after that one in spring, he never saw again. He didn't write a single word of the recipes on paper. He kept them in his belly.

From then on, every Qi-Gathering Pill the Rice Bucket spat out, he ate. A one-line pill raised the vat in his dantian a finger; a two-line one, a finger and a half. But the vat rose and the layer didn't move. He did his master's homework: every day before dawn he went up to the clifftop and stood in the Xun square, watching the fallen leaves spin. The first month, the leaves spun and his eyes spun with them. The second month, his eyes stopped spinning and the leaves still spun. Then one morning in the third month a leaf rose from the cliff edge and drifted at his face, and he didn't dodge and didn't cut. He just moved half a step to the side, and the leaf floated past his ear. He had seen the moment it rose. That morning the vat in his dantian sank with a *thump*, and he was through to the fifth layer. Next time he went up the clifftop he told his master about that moment, and Elder Qu said, "Settling the wind." The Rice Bucket had filled the vat for him; the layer he had passed himself. On the Rice Bucket's pills, Shen Xiaoman came on by leaps and bounds. There was only one trouble: solomon's seal came every month, and every month the spirit stones ran out. The allowance was ten, and the Rice Bucket didn't produce a pill every time; ten went in and only a few came back. He stood under the west wall of the Stewards' Hall, looking at the tablets with "spirit stones" written on them, and for the first time they didn't look like tablets. They looked like pills.

Two days later, under the desks of Ahe and Sun Ergou in the lecture hall, there was an oilpaper packet each, with a Qi-Gathering Pill inside. Xiaoman said only that it was interest. Sun Ergou's was for the half flatbread in the cowshed, two lines; Ahe's, he didn't say what for. It was the nine-line one.

Ahe turned the pill once against the light from the window, her lips moving: she was counting lines. At the ninth line her lips stopped. She asked nothing, wrapped it up as it was, tucked it inside her robe, and didn't eat it.

Sun Ergou ate his that same night. By daybreak Sun Ergou had broken from layer zero of Qi Condensation to the first layer. He stood in the doorway of the common bunk with one hand spread in the sunlight, looking at the thin thread of yellow qi in his palm, looking till tears and snot ran down together. Master Chu had said that drawing in qi would take him five years at the least, and he had been through the gate only half a year. The storyteller will do the sum for him: for half a year he had squatted on the woodpile every night counting breaths, and at the bottom of that dry well a little damp had already been worn into being. The two-line pill was a ladle of water poured on that damp. The ladle was Xiaoman's; the well he had dug himself, night after night.

That crying did the damage. A layer-zero mixed spirit root drawing in qi in one night: by noon it had gone round the whole outer sect, and before dark it had reached the inner sect.

---

By the time it reached the inner-sect dining hall, the story had changed. A boy who had been at the fourth layer at the contest was at the fifth by spring, and giving away pills by the packet. And he was the one who watched the herb garden. Stealing spirit herbs to make pills made more sense than a dead man's pouch.

Zhou Heng was handing out spirit stones at the dining-hall door and heard it. He didn't look up. He opened the night-watch log and turned the pages one by one to the end, and said, "The herb garden: not one plant short. The spoiled plants are the night watch's wage. It's written on the Stewards' Hall tablet: one basket a month, and it's in the accounts." He closed the log and spoke to that table: "If you say he stole, bring me the stolen goods." Nobody in the dining hall had stolen goods. That table bent their heads and ate.

When people had gone, Zhou Heng called Xiaoman back, tucked the ledger under his arm and said in a low voice, "That Qi-Gathering Pill of Sun Ergou's: I've seen it. Two-turn." He paused. "The outer-sect allowance is one line." He said nothing more and left. After that, Xiaoman went on handing in his job at the Stewards' Hall at the end of each month and bringing back a basket of spoiled plants, and Zhou Heng never asked a single word; but every time he pushed the basket across, he gave Xiaoman one more look. One spring, there were a few more roots of solomon's seal in the basket.

Zhou Heng could block the word "steal", but he couldn't block a roomful of mouths. The next day the story had changed again: whether he stole or not, who knew, but Shen Xiaoman had pills, good pills. That was deadlier than "steal". On this mountain, the people stuck at the ninth layer for ten or eight years were more than the pills; the people above him were more than the pills too. That story reached the pill house.

---

Every pill at the Sunken Cauldron Abbey came out of the pill house. The pill house stood at the north-west corner of the main hall, a low building with its doors and windows shut all year and its walls smoked black at the foot. Inside there was one furnace, and the one who kept it was the inner sect's Pill-master Min: Foundation Establishment, perfected, thirty years tending this furnace, and stuck outside the threshold of Core Formation for a dozen of them. The abbey's monthly Qi-Gathering Pills and the one Foundation Pill at each year's Grand Contest were all his work. From the stewards down to the inner sect, everyone's pills came out of his furnace, and nobody dared offend him. At festivals the gift boxes at the pill-house door piled up past the knee, and he didn't look at any of them: anyone who liked could take them back, and anyone who didn't, he returned as they were, shutting giver and box outside together. He gave nobody face, and in thirty years nobody had quarrelled with him. The furnace never went out all year. A firing lasted ten days or half a month; he stood in front of the furnace pouring his own spiritual power in, breath after breath, day and night without rest, and couldn't leave its side for a step. But the medicine in the furnace changed every quarter hour, what to add and when to change the fire, and his spirit sense was all in the fire, with nothing left over. That "Tending the Furnace" tablet on the east wall of the Stewards' Hall had hung there three years for exactly this: someone to watch the medicine for him. It wasn't that nobody wanted it. Inner sect and outer, no fewer than ten had come to ask. He called them into the room one by one, had each smell a furnace of medicine, and when they'd smelled it, sent them away; not one stayed. Those the stewards recommended, the same. For three years when he fired the furnace he could give the medicine only half his attention, and the quality of a furnace of pills had fallen by half a tenth. The holes in the hem of his robe came from standing by the furnace where the sparks spat.

At noon on the third day a Daoist boy came to the herb garden looking for Xiaoman: "Pill-master Min wants you at the pill house."

Xiaoman went. When he came in, Pill-master Min was squatting in front of the furnace, a thread of fire-type spiritual power swimming out of his fingertip, pushing the ashes aside an inch at a time. His back was to the door, thin and stooped, and his grey robe had a dozen burn holes in it, most of them in the hem. He didn't turn round. He said, "Shut the door." Xiaoman shut the door. The only light in the room was a little dark red in the firebox; the smell of medicine was bitter and heavy, and it made his eyes sting.

When Pill-master Min had finished with the ashes he stood up, sat down behind a low table and said, "I hear you've got pills. Let me see one."

Xiaoman stood without moving. He ran through this man in his head: every one-line pill in the abbey came out of this furnace. Fobbing him off with an allowance pill would be like holding up somebody's own child and asking him to name it. He took an oilpaper packet from inside his robe, opened it, and set a clear blue-white pill on the low table. A two-line one, spat out by the Rice Bucket that month. The nine-line one had long been inside Ahe's robe, and he didn't have a second.

Pill-master Min didn't reach out. He looked first. After a while he picked it up between two fingers, held it close to his eyes against that bit of red in the firebox, and turned it slowly. His lips moved, just as Ahe's had that day: he was counting lines. At the second line he stopped. "Two-turn," he said. "This one is better than what comes out of my furnace." He held the pill under his nose and smelled it, smelled it again, closed his eyes, and smelled it a third time.

"This pill," he opened his eyes, "didn't come out of a furnace."

Xiaoman said, "How can the pill-master tell?"

Pill-master Min said, "No fire in it." He set the pill back on the oilpaper. "A pill out of a furnace keeps the fire on it for three years. However many times it's been turned in the fire, that's how many fire-breaths it carries, and the nose knows at one sniff. This one hasn't a trace of fire." He looked at the pill, and after a while said, "It's as if it grew out of the ground."

Xiaoman said nothing.

Pill-master Min said, "Where's it from?"

Xiaoman said, "The secret realm. A dead man by the pool, a bottle in his pouch." He said it neither fast nor slow, in the same voice as Father saying "No rabbits on the front slope."

Pill-master Min looked at him, looked a long time. In the firebox something went *crack*. He didn't say he believed it, nor that he didn't. He only wrapped the pill back in the oilpaper as it had been, pushed it back and said, "Thirty years. This furnace of mine, ten pills a firing, two of them come out one-line." He didn't ask a second time.

Xiaoman put the packet away inside his robe, but he didn't leave. His eyes fell on the furnace and stayed there a while, and he said, "Pill-master, if only two out of ten come out, what about the other eight?"

Pill-master Min raised his eyelids. Nobody had asked him that in a long time. "The fire's wrong," he said. "Too much martial fire and the medicine scorches; too little civil fire and it stays raw."

"How do you know when it's too much?"

"By smell."

"By smell?" said Xiaoman. "My Pa, when he hunts, can smell whether a rabbit's just gone by or gone by half a day ago. Is medicine like that?"

Pill-master Min didn't answer. He raised one finger, gathered a wisp of fire-type spiritual power at its tip and sent it into the mouth of the furnace, and the fire in the firebox jumped at once in answer. "Once the medicine goes in, it smells different every quarter hour," he said. "First bitter, then bitter going sweet, then out of the sweet a wisp of scorching. That wisp lasts half a breath. A breath early, and the medicine isn't done; a breath late, and the whole furnace is wasted." As he talked his voice dropped, as if he were talking to the furnace. "A dozen or more have come, and every one of their noses fell short on that half a breath."

Xiaoman asked again, "Then this pill has no fire in it because it's never been in a furnace? If it's never been in a furnace, where do the lines come from?"

The wisp of fire-type spiritual power at Pill-master Min's fingertip stopped in mid-air. He chewed on that question a long while, and said, "I don't know." After a while he added, "Thirty years, and this is the first time anyone's asked me something I can't answer."

Just while Pill-master Min was lost in thought, the smell of the medicine in the furnace changed: a wisp of scorching had come into it. His spirit sense was all on the fire, and he didn't notice. Xiaoman twitched that dog's nose of his and said quietly, "Pill-master, this... is this the smell of a pill scorching?"

Pill-master Min whipped round, drew his fingertip back, and the fire dropped by half at once. The furnace of medicine turned over inside and settled. He didn't speak straight away. He looked Xiaoman up and down twice and said, "What's your name? Shen Xiaogou? Little Dog?"

A trickle of sweat ran down Xiaoman's temple. "Begging the pill-master's pardon," he said, "my name's Shen Xiaoman."

"Xiaoman." Pill-master Min said the two characters over once. "A little attainment, and knowing it's full. Not greedy. A good name." He paused. "That tablet has hung there three years, and not one of the people I called in could smell it. You came into this room for the first time and smelled it; and what you asked were all the right questions. You're made for pills. The fire I pour myself; no one can do it for me. The medicine, from now on, is yours to watch. Tomorrow go to the Stewards' Hall, take the tablet down, and come and report."

Xiaoman said, "...I don't know how." Pill-master Min said, "If you don't know how, learn. A nose can't be learned. Everything else can."

From under the low table he pulled out a thin handwritten volume, blue cloth cover, the corners worn fuzzy, and tossed it into Xiaoman's arms. On the cover were two characters, *The Gold Elixir*, and below them a line of small writing: *Baopuzi*, Inner Chapters, scroll four. "Ge Hong wrote it. Everyone under heaven who makes pills starts with this one," said Pill-master Min. "How pills are graded by turns, how to seal the clay, how to raise the fire, the fasting before you open the furnace: it's all in there. Copy it three times, and not one character wrong. The book gives you the principle; the fire you have to smell yourself. Circle what you don't understand, and ask tomorrow."

Xiaoman said, "Yes. I'll copy it tonight."

He left the pill house and had gone a dozen steps before he felt the sweat across his back. The man hadn't kept his pill, hadn't asked twice, and hadn't gone to the stewards' office; he himself had asked a pile of questions, and by the end had asked a tablet into his own hands. But he knew that on this mountain there was now one more pair of eyes watching him, eyes better at watching than Zhou Heng's, and they would watch him every day from now on. Only this time the one watching wanted him to stay.

Once the pill-house door had shut, the talk in the inner-sect dining hall slowly went quiet. When anyone asked, someone would answer: "Pill-master Min's looked at it." What came of his looking, nobody knew; after half a month, nobody brought it up again.

The next day he went to the Stewards' Hall and took the "Pill House · Tending the Furnace" ebony tablet down from the east wall. Steward Deng looked up and said, "Nobody's taken that one in three years." Xiaoman went to the stewards' office for the job, and Zhou Heng wrote a line in the ledger: Tending the furnace. Merit recorded.

Tending the furnace meant watching the medicine, not the fire. The fire was Pill-master Min's own: in the days of a firing he stood over the mouth of the furnace with both hands held above it, pouring in spiritual power breath after breath, without blinking. Xiaoman's work was beside the furnace: watching the medicine, smelling it, grinding it, handing things over. The first night Pill-master Min taught him nothing, only pointed by the furnace: "Sit there." So Xiaoman sat there all night, watching that bit of dark red. As he watched, he sensed something was off: under the furnace there was no firewood, no charcoal, not even bellows. The red in the firebox came up through a crack in the stone below. He put his hand out to it, and the crack was hot.

The second night, Pill-master Min at last spoke. "There are four kinds of fire." He held up four fingers and bent them down one at a time. "Common fire: firewood, charcoal, husks, the fire you cook on and warm yourself by. It can't make pills, only stew medicine. Earth fire: fire from under the ground, one vent in an old pit that hasn't gone out in a thousand years, steady, not hot-tempered. This furnace of the abbey's sits right on top of one, and has for three hundred years. But earth fire is dead; it can't make a pill by itself. The pill-master has to pour his own spiritual power in to lead it, and then the fire lives. How much he pours, that's civil and martial. Pill fire," he stopped a moment, "is the fire in a person's own body. Only those who have formed a core have it. One breath of it is purer than earth fire." Xiaoman asked, "And the fourth?" Pill-master Min looked at him, and it was a good while before he said, "True fire. Like the Supreme Old Lord's Samadhi True Fire."

The third night there was a furnace of medicine in, and in the small hours Pill-master Min suddenly said, "Smell it." Xiaoman smelled it: a wisp of scorching had come into the smell of the medicine, like the moment the crust at the bottom of a pot is about to burn. Pill-master Min half closed the furnace door and said, "Remember that smell. A breath late, and the whole furnace is fit only for mixing in." After a pause he went on: "The *Cantong Qi* has a line: martial at head and tail, civil in the middle. At the start it must be fierce, to wake the medicine; in the middle gentle, to cook it; at the end fierce again, to lock it in. When to change, your nose tells you. The book won't." He watched the medicine; Pill-master Min watched the fire.

On the seventh day Pill-master Min fired a furnace of Qi-Gathering Pills. The drawers of herbs were laid out in a row, and he named them one by one to Xiaoman like reading a register: "Solomon's seal is the sovereign; milkvetch and angelica are the ministers; ginseng whisker is the envoy; spirit-stone powder is the guide. One sovereign, two ministers, one envoy: lack one ingredient and the medicine won't come together." Xiaoman squatted by the herb drawers and said nothing aloud, but checked them off one by one in his head: solomon's seal, he'd fed it; milkvetch and angelica, he'd fed those too, only separately; spirit stones, fed; only ginseng, never. He thought of the Rice Bucket's mouth that sometimes spat and sometimes didn't, and understood at once: it wasn't bad temper. The recipe was missing an ingredient.

That night he tipped two broken whiskers of purple jade ginseng out of the herb-garden basket and fed them in all together with solomon's seal, milkvetch and angelica, and one spirit stone. *Crunch*, *crunch*, *click*. The Qi-Gathering Pill it spat out was blue-white and clear, with two lines. He looked at it a long time in the moonlight leaking through the crack of the window, then put it in the oilpaper packet he kept next to his skin.

---

On the day of Qingming he found a rock on the back mountain that faced Mount Meng and burned a stack of yellow paper for Grandfather. Grandfather's grave was in town, and he couldn't go. He recited the rhyme Mother had taught him, and as he reached "five in the centre" the paper burned out and the ash rose and spun away down the mountain.

That evening he went to the east corner of the herb garden to find Ahe. She looked after the herb garden by day and he watched it by night, so they ran into each other twice a day. At the foot of the wall in the east corner she had pulled up a strip of weeds and cleared a strip of earth a hand wide, and told no one. In spring she had broken the wax on the bamboo tube and poured out the three black grains (old grain dug out of the black earth in the secret realm, grain someone had once farmed, shining black and hard as pebbles) and buried them. Three shoots came up, and one by one they yellowed. So every night she made the orchid seal, palm down, an inch above them, and fed them her own spiritual power. She was used to feeding a whole garden of herbs, and after one bed her forehead would show a film of sweat; these three seedlings she had to feed into the small hours, until her face was as white as window paper. After twenty-odd nights of feeding, the tips of the three seedlings' leaves finally stopped yellowing, and stood up, tender and fresh.

Xiaoman squatted beside her and watched a while, and said, "They drink your spiritual power."

Ahe said, "They drink more than a whole garden of herbs." She drew her hand back and wiped it on her front. "Old grain. Who knows how many years it's been hungry."

The nine-line Qi-Gathering Pill, she had carried all this time. That night she squatted at the end of the strip and swallowed it, one hand pressed to the soil, and sat till dawn. At dawn she was at the seventh layer of Qi Condensation. How far a pill like that ought to push you, she had looked up in the Scripture Library, and she knew: it had fallen short by a stretch. At the foot of the wall the three seedlings had turned a green so deep it was nearly black, overnight.

On the hottest nights of the dog days, when the herb-garden rounds were done, the three of them often sat on the low wall of the herb garden to cool off: Xiaoman, Ahe, Sun Ergou. Sun Ergou told them his mother said Ergou, Second Dog, was a lowly name, and lowly names are easy to raise; when he made something of himself she'd give him a good one. Xiaoman asked, "What do you want to be called?" Sun Ergou thought a long while, and said, "...Ergou, still. Ma's used to it." Xiaoman didn't laugh. Ahe didn't laugh either. Under the wall Xiaozheng rolled over and snorted.

---

The abbey's lecture hall wasn't split into inner sect and outer, but by layer, and Master Chu taught all of it. The newly entered learned how to pinch out a fireball; those past the fourth layer, on the days written on the board at the door of the lecture hall, learned the advanced things. The first year's lectures were on two things you didn't have to make yourself: talismans, and magic tools.

"What is a talisman?" Master Chu drew a sheet of yellow paper from his sleeve, drawn on in cinnabar, and held it between two fingers. "It's a seal someone else has made for you, incantation and all, sealed onto paper. When you pinch out a fireball it takes a dozen breaths; this one, you put a thread of spiritual power in to light it, flick it out, and there's your fireball." He flicked the talisman out of the window, and with a *pff* there was a black hole in the old snow in the courtyard. "Three advantages. It's fast. It doesn't cost your own spiritual power, only the thread that lights it. And whatever layer the person who drew it is at, that's the layer of what comes out: you at the fifth layer can use a talisman drawn by a Foundation Establishment martial uncle." A whole row below went "Oh." Xiaoman thought of the three in the secret realm: invisibility, ice spike, mountain-pressing. A girl at the fourth layer had frozen a ninth-layer man with them.

"There are three disadvantages too." Master Chu held up his fingers. "Expensive. Use one and you have one fewer. Third—" He took out another, which looked exactly like the first. "Can any of you see where this one differs from that one?" Nobody could. He flicked it out too. The talisman flew halfway, turned a corner, and with a *pff* burst on the tip of his own goatee, singeing off half an inch. Not one person in the hall dared laugh. Master Chu twisted the tip of his beard, his face unchanged. "This one has one stroke fewer than that one. The *Baopuzi*{典=baopu-fu} says talismans were handed down by Lord Lao, 'all heavenly writing', and the characters on them no man can read. Characters you *can* read, copied three times, still turn 'fish' into 'Lu' and 'empty' into 'tiger'; ones you can't, copy them wrong and you won't even know. Ge Hong put it plainly: 'A wrong talisman is not merely useless; it will do harm.' So with talismans, only buy what the drawer drew with his own hand, never copies; and when you've bought one, try one first somewhere no one is."

The back of Xiaoman's neck went cold for a moment. That damned horse-stepping talisman the Rice Bucket had spat out: he'd never tested it once before slapping it on the soles of his own feet.

"Magic tools are another matter." Master Chu laid his ruler across the table. "A talisman, once used, is gone. A magic tool has a ready-made road carved inside it; you pour your spiritual power in and it walks the road for you. When you've used it, the road's still there. Sword qi on a sword, fire from a gourd: that's how they come about." He had someone carry in a blunt sword, a low-grade magic tool the abbey used for practice, and made the whole hall line up and take hold of it one by one and pour in. "What goes in is your own spiritual power. It doesn't save you any; it only gathers it. If you have a bowl of water and fling it out, you wet a patch of ground. Pour it into a bamboo pipe and it'll squirt three zhang."

When Xiaoman's turn came he gripped the hilt, and the vat in his dantian tipped outwards. The blade lit up for a moment, five colours stirred together like a bowl of mixed-grain porridge nobody had stirred properly, and then with a *hum* it went out. Someone behind laughed. Master Chu came over and tapped the spine of the blade with his ruler: "A magic tool knows its road. This one has metal's road carved in it. You pour all five in at once, four of them have nowhere to go, and they block the fifth too. Only metal." Xiaoman closed his eyes and shut four of the five doors, leaving only the narrowest. An inch of white qi came out of the sword tip, thin as a needle. Master Chu glanced at it: "Only an inch. Enough to peel a pear."

"One more thing, and you'll all run into it sooner or later," said Master Chu. "That is mirrors." When he said the word, every eye in the hall slid towards the window. Ma Xiaobao sat there, his face blotched white here, bruised blue there, his eyes on the view outside. "The *Baopuzi*{典=baopu-jing} says that things in the mountains which have lived long can take human shape to fool you, 'only in a mirror they cannot change their true form.' In a mirror, they can't change. So the Daoists of old who went into the mountains all carried a bright mirror nine inches across on their backs. Mirror-type magic tools don't strike anyone; they only shine. And what they shine on is the thing you're hiding." He paused. "So whoever has something hidden on him, keep away from mirrors."

He said it to the whole hall, and his eyes didn't go towards the back row. Xiaoman kept his head down and picked at a crack in the desk with his fingernail.

---

In a flash, five years went by.

These five years, Shen Xiaoman went up roughly a layer a year. The storyteller will tell the first three first: fifth layer, sixth, seventh. The thick earth that had grown from that handful of soil in the secret realm took in a breath and kept a breath. The Rice Bucket spat out one or two Qi-Gathering Pills a month; now and then a three or four, which he put away and didn't eat. Once a layer was eaten half full, he stopped, and the other half of the vat he filled himself, breath by breath at night. Mother had explained his name to him: full means it will spill over. A little short of full, you still know hunger.

The breath in *The Breathing Classic* took a different shape at each layer. At the fifth layer, when he breathed in you could hear it in the vat, like water poured into an empty jar. At the sixth, the water was higher and the sound was gone, and a breath out lasted half again as long as a breath in. At the seventh, the breath sank towards the soles of his feet. The thing Father had said that year in the snow, he only now knew where it sank to: to the heels.

As for spirit stones, at first he earned them off the west wall of the Stewards' Hall.

In the autumn of the third year he took his first tablet: Spirit Herb Valley, north of Mount Meng, a demon at night, sixth layer, no spirit wisdom yet, eats spirit herbs, hurts people, twenty spirit stones each. Steward Deng looked at the tablet, then at him: "Fifth layer, are you?" Xiaoman said, "Fifth layer." Steward Deng said, "The demon's sixth, one above you. No going alone. Errand tablets, three people at least." Xiaoman turned round, and two people stood behind him: one was Sun Ergou, who'd tagged along to watch the fun, first layer; the other was Ahe, still holding the little herb-garden hoe. Ahe said, "I'll go. I'd like to see what kind of demon is fond of eating spirit herbs." Steward Deng looked at her, said nothing, and wrote three names in the book.

Spirit Herb Valley lay north of Mount Meng, most of a day's walk. All the fields in the valley grew spirit herbs the abbey wanted, a few herb-farming families looked after them, and they handed over the crop once a year. The demon was real: when night fell, something squatted on the field ridges, the size of a rabbit, with a bird's beak, a pair of round owl's eyes, and a snake's tail trailing behind. When anyone came near, it fell over and played dead; at dawn, a patch of the field's spirit herbs was gone. The valley people had beaten it with hoes, and it rolled over and ran off, and the herbs still went missing. Two of the men keeping watch at night had already been hurt: one had his calf pecked through, the other his shoulder broken by the snake tail. The old herb farmer in charge of the valley showed them a hoe handle: there was a gash in it, clean through from top to bottom, the broken edges as neat as if cut with shears. Xiaoman measured the width of the gash against his finger and said nothing. A thing the size of a rabbit couldn't open its mouth that wide; and a thing that could open its mouth that wide wouldn't be chased off by a hoe.

The three of them squatted on the field ridge all night. After the third watch the thing did come, hopping out of the rows of spirit herbs, and the moment it saw people it lay down, belly up, not even its tail moving. Sun Ergou held up the lantern, bent over it a long while, and whispered, "It, it's asleep." Xiaoman nocked an arrow to shoot, and Ahe pressed his bow down: "Wait."

She squatted down, and didn't look at the demon's belly; she looked at the spiritual power on it. That ball of spiritual power was muddy yellow shot with blue, drawn in tight, not like a sleeping thing, more like a man with his fist clenched behind his back. "Sixth layer, wood at the bottom of it," she said quietly. "It's in the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*{典=shanhai-qiuyu}. The [qiuyu]{注=qiuyu}: when it sees people, it sleeps." Xiaoman's fingers tightened on the string. He was at the fifth layer himself.

The qiuyu's eyelids moved.

It didn't get up. It rolled over, four feet to the ground, and in one bound it was in front of Sun Ergou. Sun Ergou was still bent over, holding up the lantern, mouth open: "It, it—" The beak shut on the lantern with a *crack*, the lantern broke into three pieces, the oil splashed across the ground, and the flame ran along the oil up his sleeve. He screamed and sat down hard on the field ridge, right where the snake tail could reach.

Ahe's right hand made a seal, fast, and pointed at the ridge. Her spiritual power sank into the ground from a distance, and *whoosh*, three green vines shot up out of the earth: two wound round its hind legs and one cinched its waist, pinning its back half where it stood, so it could only twist its body and bite as far as its beak and tail would reach. It strained, and with a *snap* one vine broke. "This demon's all brute strength," Ahe frowned, not letting go of the seal in her right hand. "I can't hold it long!" Her left hand had already made another seal, and from three zhang away she pressed it lightly towards Sun Ergou's sleeve: a thread of blue spiritual power flowed across, the flame lay down and went out, and the scalded red skin under the sleeve faded, little by little. "Don't move!" she shouted. "If you move, I can't look after both ends!"

Xiaoman didn't look at her; his feet were already moving. He had the nine squares in his head (Elder Qu's first lesson of the He-Luo Sword Formula: first the feet, then the sword), and now he stepped into the Xun position, left foot in the south-east square. Before the sword went out, his body slanted half a square, Matchstick's tip hanging down, brushing the mud: under the Xun trigram the bottom line is broken; the soft is underneath. Matchstick at the fifth layer, filled full of spiritual power, had a sheen of oil floating on its edge. He waited, waited for the breath when the wind drops.

Every square he stepped in was on the edge of the ring it could reach; every time a vine broke, the ring grew a foot. The beak tore into the square where he'd just been standing, *crack*, upper and lower bill shutting on nothing. He stepped to the next square. It bit at his ribs, and he stepped another square; bite after bite. It was faster than him, much faster, but before every bite its eyeballs had to move first, just so; each bite was fiercer than the last, and shorter. The snake tail he couldn't dodge, though: it lashed across his calf like someone cracking a whip on him, and his trouser leg went wet. Two more lashes, and three welts swelled up one after another.

Just then Sun Ergou moved. He saw the fire was out, scrambled up to run, and in his panic ran headlong into Xiaoman's square. Xiaoman turned the sword tip aside and shouldered him out of the way with his left shoulder, and that shove cost him half a step. The snake tail swept across with a *crack* and lashed Sun Ergou clean off the ground; he flew three zhang, crashed into the spirit-herb rows, and didn't make a sound. On Ahe's side, another vine went *snap*. "Xiaoman," her voice was stretched tight, "hurry, I can't hold out long."

She let half a finger of her right hand go and turned her left towards Sun Ergou: blue spiritual power flowed along the furrow, and the big black-and-blue bruise on Sun Ergou's ankle faded down little by little. He opened his eyes and groaned. A film of fine sweat showed on Ahe's forehead, her breathing quickened, and her eyes never left the vines.

At that moment the last vine broke with a *snap*.

Free of its bonds, it came at him with its whole body. No more bite by bite; it rammed: beak, snake tail, four claws all at once. Xiaoman's nine squares fell apart in an instant. Back to the right was the patch where Sun Ergou lay, still not up; back to the left was the deep ditch between the herb rows, and if he went down there Sun Ergou would be left entirely under its claws. Forward was its mouth; back was Sun Ergou's life. Left and right were both dead ends. His sword tip drew one half-circle after another in front of him, only defending, not daring to attack. The beak passed against his neck and sliced off a lock of hair; the snake tail cracked across his waist and drove him back three squares, the sword nearly out of his hand.

"Xiaoman, hold on!" Ahe shouted. She dropped the vine seal, made another with her right hand and pointed at the ground under the qiuyu's feet. Three feet of earth there heaved up, *puff puff*, and a great patch of thorns shot out, every branch covered in barbs, every leaf edged with a ring of fine teeth; clump crowded against clump, with not a gap to set a foot in. It put one foot down and the barbs went into its flesh; it moved, and the teeth drew a bloody line along its leg. Not a single spirit herb was touched. The qiuyu suddenly slowed. Claw up, claw down, every step it had to pull its leg out of the thorns first. Usually it lay down whenever it saw people; this time it couldn't even lie down. Lie down, and it would be covered in spikes.

That half beat was what Xiaoman had been waiting for. With his left hand he made a sword seal, ran two joined fingers from the root of the blade to the tip, and drew a fine thread of metal-type spiritual power out of his dantian, down his arm and into Matchstick. A coat of bleak white light plated the dark red blade, like frost on a copper kettle on a winter night, and the edge became at once thinner, and sharper. The weakest of his five doors was good for just this once.

He touched his toes to the field ridge beside Sun Ergou and his whole body shot up off the ground, over the qiuyu's head. In the thorns it couldn't shift; it could only crane its neck and watch, eyes wide, as a shape came down at it from under the moon. Matchstick, plated in metal, point down: a streak of white light, into the back of its neck, all the way to the hilt. It didn't even finish one cry. It jerked twice on the sword and was still. He was still in mid-air, and his mouth wasn't idle: "Lucky my metal beats it."

Xiaoman came down, and came down in Ahe's great patch of thorns. Ahe's hand dropping the spell and his feet hitting the ground happened in the same blink; the thorns shrank back with a *whoosh*, and still they were that one instant late.

He landed flat on his back with his limbs in the air. Barbs caught his sleeves, the teeth drew a few lines across the backs of his hands and his face, and he got a handful of thorn tips. The thorns drew back clean away. He scrambled up, his face covered in bloody scratches, looked at Ahe, raised his hands and grinned: "What a spell!" He pulled a face. "I'd better never make you angry."

Ahe was panting. She looked up at him: "Good, as long as you remember." She came over, grabbed his wrist, and made a seal with her other hand. A layer of blue spiritual power came through her palm, cool, like grass leaves after rain lying against the skin. The bloody lines on his hands and face closed over one by one, the beads of blood set, and the pain went off little by little; the lash marks on his calf and the bruise on his waist faded a shade. "Don't move," she said, stony-faced. "There are still some thorns in the flesh. I'll pull them out in a minute." Xiaoman sat as good as gold and didn't dare pull another face.

When the valley people came round with torches, Xiaoman was sitting on the field ridge. The thorn scratches on his hands and face had just closed; his trouser leg was cut open, the three lash marks on his calf were still swollen, and beneath them was a deep beak mark that Ahe had only stopped bleeding. He didn't know when it had pecked him; he hadn't felt it. There was a big bruise on his waist too. He took out a Spring-Return Pill, swallowed it, and said, "It doesn't sleep." Ahe squatted and looked at his leg, and said, "The book says it sleeps when it sees people. The book doesn't say the sleep is a sham." She wrote that line in her little notebook.

At dawn, the valley people gave them two strings of cash and a basket of eggs. The old herb farmer also skinned the qiuyu with a knife and got three things out of it: a pair of owl's round eyeballs, perfectly round, golden yellow, still bright against the torches at night; a bird's beak, its edges sharp as shears; and a length of spotted snake-tail skin, soft and tough. The old herb farmer said, "The eyeballs, the herb shops will take, to make the guide for a night-sight medicine. The beak and the skin, you divide among yourselves." The three of them divided them: the eyeballs went to Xiaoman, since he'd killed it; the beak to Ahe, who wanted to take it back and add a note against the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*; the snake-tail skin to Sun Ergou, who had taken a tail lash for everyone and ought to get something. Sun Ergou held that length of snakeskin and couldn't say a word for a long time. Back at the abbey, Steward Deng turned the tablet over, wrote "Done" on the back, and counted out sixty spirit stones, twenty each. Ahe took her share, didn't count it, and pulled out her little notebook to write a line. Xiaoman said, "What are you writing?" Ahe said, "Eggs. Twelve." After a pause: "Interest separately."

The storyteller will put in a word here: on this trip, Ahe saved three people's lives, and Xiaoman won a duel against a thing pretending to sleep, and didn't win it prettily. The next five years were mostly like that: she watched the ground, he watched people; she thought of the way, he struck; and the one who got beaten was Sun Ergou.

In the twelfth month of that year Xiaoman went down the mountain to the market to find Shopkeeper Qian. At the fair Shopkeeper Qian set out only one board, but at the end of the market street he had a shop too. The front was so narrow one man had to turn sideways to get in, but inside it went deep, rows of drawers stacked from floor to roof beam, each with a slip of red paper pasted on it, the writing his own, crooked and wobbly. Xiaoman waited till the shutters went up and came in by the back door, and set a small clay jar on the counter: a year's saved Spring-Return Pills, one-line and two-line, twenty or so. Shopkeeper Qian rolled them one by one between his fingers, looked at them against the lamp, clicked his abacus a long time, counted out a hundred and forty spirit stones and pushed them across. A year's inner-sect allowance was a hundred and twenty; this jar was worth more than a year. Shopkeeper Qian added, "If you've got good ones, three lines and up, my shop can't afford them. Good things don't stay with me. They go to the auction at Pengcheng, once a year." Xiaoman made a note of it.

The spirit stones were pushed across, and he didn't hurry to pocket them. What Yinyue had said on the night of the Grand Contest stayed in his ears: whoever this queen is on, that's who he looks for. He went once round the shop, and everything he picked was something to keep him alive.

First, herbs. Solomon's seal, milkvetch, purple jade ginseng, a bundle of each; and a few the new recipes needed: poria, fleeceflower root, sweet flag, half a jin of each weighed out. To take back for the Rice Bucket. Pills were his life, and the ones he could make himself were more reliable than any he could buy.

Next, talismans. Shopkeeper Qian fetched the ladder and took a flat wooden box down from the topmost drawer. Inside lay three talismans drawn in gold dust: "Escape talismans. Press it in your palm, make a seal, and you're three zhang away." Xiaoman asked how much, and Shopkeeper Qian said, "Forty a sheet." Xiaoman gritted his teeth and bought one, wrapped it in three layers of oilpaper, and kept it next to his skin.

Then weapons. Matchstick was a good sword, but where a sword couldn't reach, you needed something else. He chose a short flying sword, a span long, willow-leaf shaped, with a red silk cord tied to the hilt. Held between the fingers, a seal sent it out two or three zhang, and then called it back. Low-grade, Shopkeeper Qian said; a fifth-layer Qi Condensation cultivator could drive it.

Last, a soft mail vest. Dusty grey, so thin it could be folded into a sleeve; Shopkeeper Qian said it was woven from demon-beast sinew. He hacked at it twice with a knife and left two white marks: "A Qi Condensation blade won't get through." Xiaoman asked, "And a Foundation Establishment one?" Shopkeeper Qian put the knife down and gave him a look: "Against Foundation Establishment, nothing you buy will help." Xiaoman thought about it, and bought it anyway.

Of a hundred and forty spirit stones, fewer than twenty came back up the mountain with him. He sent ten taels of silver home, and wrote in the letter: Pa's knees, see a doctor. From then on, every year in the twelfth month he went down the mountain once, sold a jar of pills and bought a round of things; every year Shopkeeper Qian took the pills, and every year said the same thing: "I don't ask where things come from, and I cheat neither old nor young." At the market he sold these pills as "good goods", and whoever asked who made them got the same answer. After that, whenever Xiaoman went out on a job, he first did a couple of deals at Shopkeeper Qian's as a matter of course, selling a couple of pills and buying a few things; when he came back, he sold off what he hadn't needed.

Twice a month Elder Qu taught him the Xun-four sword on the clifftop with the broom handle, and he followed along with Matchstick. Elder Qu didn't say much when he taught the sword: one form a layer, one sentence a form. In five years the cloth on the hilt was rewound three times.

Qin Yan didn't accept that bout at the contest. In the spring of the third year she came up the cliff for the first time. She didn't draw her sword; she stood at the edge of the cliff watching him a good while first, then said, "That bout, you won by running." Xiaoman said, "Off the stage is a loss. That's the contest rule." Qin Yan said, "That's why I've come up. One more bout. This time the stage is the whole cliff, and it has no edge."

She used the Flowing Cloud Sword, handed down properly in the abbey, every move with its name, opening, change and close, not a hair out of place. Xiaoman used Elder Qu's Xun four, a foot here and a foot there, with no method to it. The first bout, inside twenty moves, her sword point stopped over his heart, and stopped steady. Xiaoman looked down at that point and understood for the first time: Elder Qu had taught him how to watch and how to wait, but not how to go on, one cut after another. That was something Qin Yan had and he didn't yet.

Qin Yan sheathed her sword, and with the tip of the scabbard cut a notch in the old pine at the edge of the cliff: "Next month, I'll come again."

Once, after a bout, the two of them sat under the pine catching their breath. Qin Yan wiped her sword with her sleeve and didn't look at him: "Your left hand always goes to guard your chest." Xiaoman's hand was pressed there right then, and it stiffened. Qin Yan said, "You did it at the contest, and you do it up here. I thrust at your shoulder, you take it on the shoulder; I thrust at your chest, you'd rather trade an arm." She pushed the sword back into its sheath. "If a swordsman has one place on him he won't let anyone touch, that place is his opening. I can see it. Others will see it sooner or later." It was a while before Xiaoman said, "Aren't you going to ask what it is?" Qin Yan stood up and brushed the pine needles off her clothes: "My Pa says, if you come on a snare someone's set in the hills, go round it. Don't pull it open to look." She walked to the edge of the cliff and turned back. "Next month, I'll go only for your chest. Practise how not to trade an arm." After that, for three months running, her sword point went for his heart every time. In the fourth month, he stopped trading an arm.

In the spring of the fourth year, the lesson was following the wind. That day a wind rose on the clifftop. Elder Qu stood in the Xun square with a leaf between his fingers and let it go; the leaf flew up, and his broom handle followed it. The leaf went east, the broom handle went east; the leaf turned a circle, the broom handle turned a circle, not fast, not slow, like a man walking a dog on a lead. "Once you're still, you can see the wind rise," said Elder Qu. "Once you've seen it, don't push against it. Pushing is Gen, and you're not a mountain. Go with it half the way and half its strength is spent; the other half is yours." He recited a line: "The *Zhuangzi*{典=zhuangzi-liezi} says, 'Liezi rode the wind and went, light and lovely.' Liezi walked on the wind, and walked so easily. And there's a line after it: 'Yet there was still something he depended on.' He still had to wait for wind." He leaned the broom back in the corner. "This sword formula borrows the wind. A man who borrows wind, however lightly he goes, still needs wind. The founder left only half a scroll, up to Core Formation; above Core Formation, what's wanted is the skill of not borrowing anything, and that isn't in this half. You borrow for now." As he left he added, as if in passing: "What you can't break through, don't hit. Wait for it to leak."

That summer the Rice Bucket spat out a three-line Spring-Return Pill, the only one he'd seen since he came through the gate. Xiaoman wrapped it in a clean cloth, and after the next bout set it under the pine beside her scabbard: "This is for your Pa's leg." Qin Yan looked at the cloth bundle and didn't reach for it: "One like this goes for two hundred spirit stones at the market. Your allowance is ten a month." Xiaoman said, "You said it yourself: if you come on a snare someone's set in the hills, go round it, don't pull it open to look. You didn't ask what I guard on my chest; don't ask where this pill came from either." Qin Yan looked at him a while and said, "All right. Then I won't ask." She picked up the bundle and put it inside her robe, put it away deep, and after a long time said, "That bout at the contest: I wanted to win, I wanted Foundation Establishment, and I went the long way round, all for one of these. Losing to you, I accept. But that night, I hated you." Xiaoman said, "I know you hated me." Qin Yan said, "You knew, and you still traded an arm?" Xiaoman said, "I had my reasons why I had to win too." She looked at him a while, nodded, and didn't ask what they were.

Two months later she came up the cliff and didn't draw. She sat under the pine, took a letter from inside her robe, read it and folded it, folded it and opened it again. "My Pa's walking," she said. "On a crutch. He got as far as the yard gate." Xiaoman said, "My Pa's is his knees. Whenever it rains, he can't squat." Qin Yan said, "If he can't squat, how does he read the hoofprints?" Xiaoman said, "He lies down on the ground to read them." Qin Yan burst out laughing, *pff*, and showed that little pointed tooth.

That day the two of them didn't fight; they talked mountains all afternoon. Her Pa hunted bears by crouching all night up a tree on the downwind side: the bear couldn't smell him, and he loosed only when it was under the tree. His Pa hunted deer on the same principle: wind first, then deer. Later on Qin Yan said, "It was the wind that ruined my Pa's leg. That night he was crouched on the downwind side, crouched just right, and when the sun went down the wind turned round and blew every bit of his man-smell over to the bear. The bear didn't come from in front. It came up the tree from behind him." She looked down the cliff and said nothing for a long time.

Xiaoman said, "The first time my Pa took me into the mountains, that's exactly what he taught me: the moment the sun goes down, the mountain wind turns. By day it blows uphill, by night downhill. Before that moment, you have to move." Qin Yan turned her face and looked at him a while: "When your Pa taught you, how old were you?" Xiaoman said, "I was seven that year." She nodded, and said slowly, "My Pa hunted twenty years, and nobody taught him that. He paid a leg for it; and when he'd paid, the first thing he did was teach it to me." She paused. "Your skills and mine, both from our fathers. Yours came cheaper."

When she left, she cut a notch in the old pine with the tip of her scabbard as usual, and cut it in his row. Xiaoman said, "We didn't fight today." Qin Yan said, "We didn't fight swords. We fought over whose Pa taught earlier. That bout, you win."

That autumn, Ahe came up the cliff for the first time. She carried a little bamboo basket, and in it a jar of freshly boiled wound salve: Xiaoman came down the cliff every month covered in cuts, and she found treating them one at a time too much bother, so she'd brought it straight up. She reached the clifftop just as a bout had ended; Xiaoman sat under the pine getting his breath, and Qin Yan was wiping her sword.

Ahe set down the basket and didn't look at Xiaoman first. She looked at the hand Qin Yan held her sword in: the web of the thumb had a split in it, scabbed and split again. She said, "Senior sister Qin, give me your hand." Qin Yan looked at her and held her hand out. Ahe made a seal with her right hand, and a point of blue light at her fingertip went once along the cut; the split closed, leaving only a white mark. Qin Yan turned her hand over and looked at it, and said, "What layer are you at?" Ahe said, "One below you." Qin Yan's eyebrows moved: "You've only been through the gate four years." Ahe said, "Your layers you climbed step by step yourself. They're sounder than mine." Qin Yan said, "Junior sister Ahe, you've not only got a good spirit root, you've got sense and a kind heart. The abbey's lucky to have a disciple like you." When she'd said it, she smiled and showed that little pointed tooth.

So the two of them sat under the pine and talked, and talked half the afternoon. Qin Yan asked her how a vine could grow so fast; Ahe asked her how a sword could put out three feet of qi, and how, once it was out, she got it back. One talked about the fields and one about the mountains, and in the end they met: farmers watch the sky, hunters watch the wind, both families eat at Heaven's pleasure, and any year Heaven didn't please, the whole family went hungry. Qin Yan said, "What does your family grow?" Ahe said, "Millet. Half of it comes up empty every year." Qin Yan said, "My Pa hunts bears. Every year he comes home empty-handed half the time." They both laughed.

Xiaoman put in a word from the side: "My Pa—" Ahe didn't turn her head: "I know everything about your Pa." Qin Yan said, "I've heard about his Pa from him too." Xiaoman had to shut his mouth, take the jar of salve himself and rub it on his arm.

Ahe took the little notebook from inside her robe, turned to a page and read it to Qin Yan: "Qin Yan. Sword qi, three feet. At the stage edge, she pushes one inch further. Holds back thirty per cent. Doesn't trample people." Qin Yan said, "What did you write that for?" Ahe said, "I wrote it before the contest. I was afraid he couldn't beat you." Qin Yan said, "He can't beat me." Ahe said, "That's why I had to write it." With a stick of charcoal she added another line underneath, and when she'd added it she turned the notebook round to show Qin Yan. The line said: One little pointed tooth. Prettier smiling than not.

Going down the mountain, Qin Yan walked in front, Ahe behind, the two of them still talking. Xiaoman carried the empty basket at the back, and slowly did a sum in his head: from now on, whenever he got beaten on the cliff, once he was off the cliff he'd have to hear about it all over again.

Following the wind he learned on the east bank of the Yi River.

That autumn a red tablet went up on the west wall: "East bank of the Yi River: at night a bull-headed monster comes ashore and has dragged off three plough oxen. Four spirit stones." It was put up by the headman of a poor village on the Yi, the writing done for him by a county clerk. The old hands gave it one look and walked off: four spirit stones, three oxen, no idea what the thing was, not worth going. It hung seven days. On the morning of the eighth, Xiaoman took it down. Steward Deng said, "Fifth layer, taking a red tablet?" Xiaoman said, "Fifth layer can take them." Steward Deng looked at him a while and wrote three names in the book. At the door of the Stewards' Hall, the steward who had called the names at the contest, Ma Xiaobao's uncle, passed along the gallery with a bowl of tea, glanced at the tablet, glanced at Xiaoman's face, and went off sipping his tea.

When he looked at that tablet he wasn't reading words, he was reading footprints. Three oxen, and it didn't say there was blood. A thing as big as an ox, bitten to death by something, there'd always be blood on the field ridge; no blood meant it hadn't been bitten to death, it had been dragged off alive; and dragged off not into the hills but into the water. What could drag an ox? He thought about it all night and couldn't work it out. When he couldn't work it out, he did what Father did: wind first, then deer; if you can't see the deer, first put your people in place.

By the time the three of them reached the east bank of the Yi it was dark. The headman led them down to the river flat. The flat was a mess of ox hoofprints, and among the hoofprints was a drag mark two feet wide, running from the ox pen all the way into the water. On either side of the drag mark ran a line of claw prints, not deep, not shallow, and down the middle of the claw prints a winding groove, as if a thick rope had been dragged through the mud. Ahe squatted to look at the groove and said, "That's a snake's tail dragging." Then she looked at the claw prints and said, "Four legs. And what it has are hooves." She opened her little notebook, turned to the back and read: "The *Classic of Mountains and Seas*{典=shanhai-lu}, Southern Mountains, Mount Di: 'There is a fish there, shaped like an ox, that lives on the hills, with a snake's tail and wings; its wings are under its ribs; its voice is like a lowing ox; it is called the lu. It dies in winter and lives in summer.'" Xiaoman said, "It's a fish?" Ahe said, "The [lu]{注=lu} is a fish that lives on land, looks like an ox, has a snake's tail, and wings growing under its ribs. Dies in winter, lives in summer: when winter comes it sleeps like the dead, and before it sleeps it has to eat its fill." She shut the notebook. "Right now it's at its hungriest."

Xiaoman asked, "How big is it?" Ahe said, "The book doesn't say how big. But it can drag an ox. Work it out yourself." The headman wanted to take men to guard the ox pen. Xiaoman waved him off and had the whole village go indoors, bar the doors and put out the fires. Sun Ergou unslung the pine board from his back (he had planed it himself behind the woodshed, taller than a man, with three iron bands nailed round it) and planted it at the mouth of the drag mark. He stood behind the board with a torch stuck in beside it, which lit his face white and bright. Ahe stood two steps behind him, her right hand lightly holding a seal, fingertips pointing at the mud of the flat. Xiaoman didn't stand in the light. He backed twenty paces downwind and squatted in a clump of reeds, Matchstick across his knees.

This was the seventh tablet they'd taken that year, and nobody needed to tell anybody anything.

When the moon came up, the water stirred.

The thing came up out of the water. First to show was an ox's head, two horns, a pair of ox eyes glowing green in the torchlight; the body that followed was an ox's too, with a blue-black hide, oily-sleek, like a fish; behind it trailed a snake's tail longer than its body, sweeping back and forth across the flat. It was a size smaller than an ox, and once ashore it didn't stop. Head down, it charged straight at the torch. All it had eyes for was that ball of light, and the board beneath it.

Sun Ergou drove both feet into the mud, bent his knees and sank his backside down. This was an earth-type stance Master Chu had taught in the lecture hall, which everyone in the outer sect had learned and hardly anyone took seriously, called the Mountain-Root Stance. Sun Ergou had practised two years, and of everything he'd practised this was the one he'd mastered: once he stood down into it, his feet seemed to grow roots. *Dong*: the ox head hit the board, and the board slid back three feet, ploughing two furrows in the mud, and stopped. Sun Ergou was still standing, his face squashed sideways by the board, shouting: "I'm a mountain... I'm a mountain..."

It backed off a step and rammed again, and the board didn't give. But the snake tail came round the edge of the board and swept, *whoosh*, at Sun Ergou's ankles.

Ahe made a seal with her right hand and pointed at the sweeping tail. A clump of thorns heaved up out of the flat, *puff*, barbs outward, and the tail swept into it and came away hung with spikes. The thorns climbed up along the tail and up its hind legs, and cinched tight, coil by coil. It strained in pain and couldn't break free: its ox head pressed against the board and its hind legs pinned to the flat by the thorns, it could go neither forward nor back.

The skin under its ribs opened: two wings, thin as a bat's, as big as a sleeping mat when spread. It meant to fly, to pull itself out thorns and all. Before the wings opened it had to take a breath, and as it took the breath the flap of skin at the root of the wings turned back for an instant and showed the pink flesh underneath, like a fish's gills.

That instant was what Xiaoman had been waiting for in the reeds. The short flying sword held between his fingers, he made the seal and let it go. A streak of white light flew low across the mud of the flat and bored into that pink flesh.

It gave a cry like a lowing ox, with a tail on the end of it like a man shouting under water, and its half-spread wings fell in. It struggled a few times, and stopped struggling, and lay on its side in the thorns, one ox eye turned up at the sky.

Only then did Sun Ergou come out from behind the board. His legs were so stiff they wouldn't bend; he shuffled over step by step, reached Xiaoman, and sat down with a thump, looking at his two hands: the iron bands on the board's edge had rubbed two patches of skin off his palms, all bloody. Ahe squatted down and took his wrist, sent a thread of spiritual power down along it, and said, "Bones are fine." With her left hand she made a seal, a thread of blue light welled from her fingertip onto the wounds, and at once they were mostly healed. Sun Ergou looked at his palms and grinned: "Senior brother Shen, I, I held." Xiaoman said, "You held. It hit you twice." Sun Ergou said, "Three times." He held up three fingers. "You didn't see the first one. I was counting."

When it was light, the headman brought the whole village down to the river flat and dragged it out of the thorns: a size smaller than an ox, the blue-black hide turning blue in the sun. The whole village ate meat for three days, and the meat tasted of fish, but wasn't fishy. The headman gave them a basket of eggs and a crock of wine. Xiaoman asked for two things: the membranes of the two wings under its ribs, thin and tough, clear against the sun, and able to hold the wind; and the hide off its back, which was astonishingly thick: the headman worked at skinning it with a knife a long time, and the edge turned over three times. He rubbed the hide and the membranes with salt, rolled them up, and carried them back up the mountain.

By the time they got back to the Si River it was already dusk. Sun Ergou had sat the stance half the night and his legs were still stiff; he couldn't manage the steps, so Ahe propped him up and took him up first. Xiaoman squatted in the shallows below the ferry, rinsing the hide once more: the salt hadn't gone all the way in, it stank of fish, and if he carried it into the West Gallery, four to a room, he'd be cursed for it.

Suddenly the river went quiet.

It wasn't the wind dropping; it was the water stopping. The few waterweeds in the shallows, which had swayed with the current, now stood straight up, as if stuck in ice. The autumn insects on the bank, singing all together a moment ago, all went dumb at once. The moon shone on the water, and the water was flat as a mirror, without a ripple. The wooden tablet on Xiaoman's chest went icy cold for an instant, so cold his hand flinched, and the hide fell into the water.

"Fish skin," someone said behind him. "Good stuff."

The voice was dry and deep, like someone leaning over the mouth of an old well and calling down. Xiaoman didn't turn round. The hairs on the back of his neck stood up one by one.

He snatched up Matchstick from the stone where it lay and, without turning round, sent the sword backwards with the wind off the river. A man in white stood on the stone steps of the ferry, the moonlight falling on all that white, white enough to look blue; one hand behind his back, not moving. The sword point reached half a foot from his cuff and stopped, as if it had struck the surface of the water in a well, and couldn't go half a hair further. Xiaoman's eyes went round, his mouth open, a breath stuck in his throat. This cut had all the spiritual power he could pour into it, and in front of this man it couldn't even touch his sleeve.

The man laughed. The laugh wasn't loud, it was dry, one note after another, like something rising slowly up from the bottom of a well, reaching the mouth of the well, not spreading, and sinking back. The sword sprang back of itself, and the jolt numbed Xiaoman's hand and split the web of his thumb.

Xiaoman didn't make a second cut. He stepped back, the Step of Yu, left foot first, right foot round— and the man was already standing behind him. He hadn't seen the man take a step. A chill crawled from the base of his spine all the way up to the back of his head.

He pressed the escape talisman from inside his robe into his palm and made the seal. A flash of gold, and he had landed in the shallows three zhang away. He turned his head, and the blood drained from his face completely: all that white was right in front of him, just as far, just as near as before, and not a drop of water on the hem of the robe.

"Three zhang," the man said, and laughed again. "Good talisman."

One finger touched his chest, very lightly.

Xiaoman's whole body folded backwards and he lay down in the water. The soft mail next to his skin split with a *hiss*, like paper gone soft in water. A thread of dark cold spiritual power went in through that finger into his heart, like an icy wire poured in, freezing from his chest out to his fingertips. Half his body was suddenly not his own, and he couldn't draw a breath. The water closed over his ears, and the sounds from the bank were muffled. The man in white squatted down. That face, so white you couldn't tell its age, was less than a foot from his; there was no anger in it and no pleasure, only that little smile hanging at the corner of the mouth, like a skin of frost on the rim of a well. From his sleeve he took a bronze mirror, the same size as Ma Xiaobao's, and slowly, face down, pressed it against the wooden tablet on Xiaoman's chest.

"In here," the man said. Then he laughed low, very patiently, like someone who has looked for something a long time and finally got a hand on it, and is in no hurry to take it.

The wooden tablet was cold as a lump of ice. Xiaoman tried to move and couldn't; he tried to shout, and when he opened his mouth, what poured in was the Si River. He stared, his pupils shrunk to two pinpoints, and watched that mirror press down on his chest. For the first time in his life he knew a person could die like this: no knife, no fist, one finger, one mirror, someone squatting in front of you, like squatting to see whether a well has water in it.

A burst of silver light exploded from the wooden tablet.

If anyone had been on the bank, all they'd have seen was a flash below the ferry, like someone throwing a handful of broken silver into the water. What Xiaoman saw was her. Yinyue sprang out of the tablet, not as a fox, not as a woman, only as a ball of light, and threw herself at the mirror. The man in white flew backwards, flew three zhang, came down in the middle of the river and threw up a sheet of water. The ball of light wavered in mid-air, thinned, thinned, thin as a skin of frost on winter window paper. She looked back at him once, and said only one word:

"Run."

The light went out, and the wooden tablet went cold.

He crawled up the stone steps. The frozen half of his body pressed against the stone, one step, one step, three hundred steps. He didn't remember which step he'd crawled to, only that every step was his own blood. Halfway up, someone stood up in the middle of the river. The man didn't follow. He stood waist-deep in the water, wiped the mirror with his sleeve, and when he'd wiped it looked up the steps. His voice was still level, coming flat across the river, not one word blurred:

"Why run? You look for your cauldrons. When you've found them, I'll come and take them."

Xiaoman crawled up the last step, over the threshold of the mountain gate, and lay with his face against the stone terrace. The terrace had been swept, clean, without a single leaf. He lay against it, listening to himself breathe in, breath after breath, failing, and breathing again.

He thought of Zou Qingsong in the secret realm. That time, his blade had struck, and cut after cut had slid off, but at least he could see where the man stood. This time, he hadn't even seen how the man got behind him. That time, she'd pulled out two hairs; this time, she'd set herself alight. Next time—

There wouldn't be a next time. Before the next time, he had to grow to where she wouldn't have to set herself alight again.

Back at the abbey, Steward Deng took the tablet, turned it over, wrote "Done" on the back, and nailed a little board below with three names on it, the first being Shen Xiaoman. Four spirit stones lay on the counter: one for Ahe, one for Sun Ergou, two for him. Steward Deng looked at the blue-white on his face and the half of his body still stiff, and said, "That was a sixth-layer thing." Xiaoman said, "We didn't know when we fought it." Steward Deng said, "If you'd known, you wouldn't have taken it."

That night he sat cross-legged on his bed in the West Gallery, breathing out and in. The wooden tablet lay against his chest, cold; he held it half the night and couldn't warm it. The thread of cold in his chest hadn't all gone, and he couldn't breathe deep, only in short breaths, one after another. In the small hours, at the bottom of the vat in his dantian, something suddenly sank with a *thump*. Not a pill; he hadn't eaten a pill that month. What sank was that wait in the reeds: waiting for the instant it drew breath. He was at the sixth layer.

---

The lu's hide and wing membranes he kept in the woodshed all winter. The fishy smell grew stronger the longer they kept, and the three senior brothers in the West Gallery took turns cursing him all winter. When the year turned and spring came, he set the roll in front of the Rice Bucket. The Rice Bucket had no nose, but its lid lifted and lifted, plainly sniffing; on the third lift the lid went *clang* wide open, and it swallowed the lot, hide, membranes and the straw rope round them, in one gulp, and burped, and the burp smelled of grilled fish.

It had a bellyache all night. Gurgle, gurgle, like a pot of porridge that wouldn't come to the boil, and in the small hours it let off a *pff* of wind that filled the woodshed with the stink of fish. Just before dawn the lid opened and it spat out a pair of shoes.

They were ugly shoes. Grey-brown ankle boots, the fish-scale pattern still on the leather, scale by scale, and at each heel a slit, like a fish's gill. Burned into the soles were the words **Tailwind**, and under them a line of writing the size of rice grains: "Wind at your back, road at your feet."

That night the wind was from the north. Xiaoman put them on, stood still in the clearing behind the woodshed and sent in a thread of spiritual power. The "gills" at his two heels opened with a *pff*, like two small animals snorting. He thought a moment about that line of small writing, faced into the wind, and took a step back.

With that step back, he rose. Not flying: his feet weren't a foot off the ground. He slid backwards close to the ground, like a leaf on the surface of water pushed along by the wind, the two slits at his heels opening and closing; one slide was ten zhang, and he stopped only at the foot of the wall. He turned round and took a step back against the wind, and the two "gills" opened and opened, *pff*, *pff* again, and he didn't move an inch.

Inside his robe, Yinyue went "Hmph." It was her first sound all winter, and the voice was still thin, as if through a layer of window paper: "Fish-skin shoes. That make noises, too." Xiaoman stood at the foot of the wall and didn't dare move for a long time, afraid that if he moved, the sound would be gone.

He tried it back and forth three times, and his dantian was half empty: sixth-layer spiritual power couldn't hold up a fourth. He sat at the foot of the wall getting his breath, and understood one thing: these shoes weren't for fighting, they were for running. Mother had said, if you can't win, run; with these shoes on, before you ran, you'd have to look at the wind first.

Matchstick grew along with him. In the year of the fifth layer, a thread of spiritual power sent in would hold that sheen of oil on the blade for a stick of incense. At the sixth, the sheen thickened a shade; other people's blades struck it and slid off with a *hiss*, without leaving even a white mark on it.

---

Qi Changsong came out of seclusion in the spring of the third year. The whole abbey waited on the training ground to watch, and what came out was still a ninth layer: his back covered in black sweat, stinking, but in one piece. Someone went up to ask, and he stood at the edge of the training ground with his hands behind his back and said, "That was the first time." And: "My body's remembered it. Next time I'll come again." That year on the eighth of the twelfth month he went up on the stage again and took first place again; when the year turned and spring came, he went into seclusion a second time, and this time he reached Foundation Establishment. The abbey changed what it called him: Martial Uncle Qi.

The first time Martial Uncle Qi stood on his sword was early one morning, while the outer sect was doing its morning practice on the training ground. First a streak of blue light rose from the back mountain, lurching and slanting like a drunk swallow. As it came closer they could see a man standing on the sword, square-faced, both feet clamped on the spine of the blade, body pressing forward, exactly the way he'd pressed people on the Five Elements stage that year. But a sword isn't the ground: the harder he pressed forward, the harder the sword dived. The sword looped once round the sky over the training ground and, with a great *crash*, plunged headfirst into the old cypress in the east corner.

A hundred-odd people on the training ground went red in the face and thick in the neck holding it in, cheeks puffed out like toads, and not one dared laugh aloud. It was that very tree. Two years ago on the eighth of the twelfth month, a sheet of yellow paper with a pig drawn on it had dragged him into it, and it was this one.

The kitchen auntie came running out with a clothes-prop, ran to the foot of the tree, looked up, saw it was Martial Uncle Qi, and her eyes went round as two coins, and she couldn't lift the pole: "...Martial Uncle, shall I poke?" The tree was quiet a while, then from among the cypress branches came Qi Changsong's voice, as level as ever: "Poke."

It took three pokes to get him down. Qi Changsong landed, his hair stuck full of cypress needles, for all the world like a hedgehog just out of its nest. He brushed off the cypress, put his sword back in its sheath, looked up, and saw Xiaoman in the crowd. He looked at him a while and said, "That pig of yours, any left?" Xiaoman said, "...None." Qi Changsong nodded: "Pity. It flew steadier than me." With that he walked off with his hands behind his back, step by step, very slowly, every footfall planted very firm.

That day the outer sect laughed behind people's backs all day: Sun Ergou under his quilt going "ha ha ha ha", laughing a while, then pinching his voice to do a "Poke," then laughing again. Xiaoman didn't laugh. A new piece had been knocked off the old cypress's bark, raw and white, next to the piece from two years before. A man had waited twenty years for one pill, broken through on his second try, flown for the first time, and come down in a tree. And himself? Still halfway up the mountainside of Qi Condensation.

---

In the fifth year he took fewer tablets from the Stewards' Hall, and spent most of his days on the clifftop. Elder Qu came twice a month to teach the sword; on the days without lessons he stood in the Xun square himself, watching the wind rise and the leaves fall, walking the following-the-wind form over and over, till dark before he went down. Qin Yan still came up once a month. On the old pine, her row of notches grew longer year by year, and his was sparse. Every bout ended much the same: just as he was about to lose, his feet slid, and he was out of the ring. Qin Yan sheathed her sword: "Running again." Xiaoman said, "My Ma says, if you can't win, run." Qin Yan grumbled at him out loud, but every time she watched that slide of his, where the feet landed, which way the body leaned, and when she'd watched it she said no more. Others took him for a coward; she knew it was the skill that kept him alive. He joked and laughed with everyone; only with her could he say "I'm scared," and when she heard it, she didn't laugh at him. In Ahe's little notebook there was one page that was all dates, and after every date the same line: Senior sister Qin, here again.

That autumn he reached the seventh layer. Elder Qu taught him entering the gap. "Xun is entering": he had heard those words in his first lesson, and only at the seventh layer was it taught. Elder Qu set a bluestone on the ground and said, "Cut it." Xiaoman brought the sword down; sparks sprayed across the ground, the sword bounced back, and there was a white mark on the stone. Elder Qu ran the broom handle slowly over the stone, stopped at one place, and touched it lightly with the tip of the handle, and the stone split in two with a *crack* along a line no one could see. "Stone has gaps too," said Elder Qu. "You didn't see it; that doesn't mean it isn't there." He recited a line: "In the *Zhuangzi*{典=zhuangzi-wuhou} there's Cook Ding, who butchered oxen for nineteen years, and his knife was still as if fresh from the whetstone. He said: 'The joints have spaces, and the blade has no thickness. Put what has no thickness into what has space.' The ox has gaps, the knife has no thickness; take the thing with no thickness into the thing with gaps." He drew the broom handle back. "With the lu, the gap was in the flesh, and the eye could see it. Higher up, the things you meet, the gaps are in the spiritual power. Your sword has thickness. You can't see the gap."

Xiaoman spent a winter on the clifftop watching for that form. The gaps in stone he slowly came to see; the gaps in trees too. The gaps in living things, Elder Qu said, wait till you meet them and look then. He thought of that instant in the underground river with Zou Qingsong, all the man's spiritual power pressed into the gourd's mouth, the light on his left arm going dim. That was a gap. But that instant Zou Qingsong had given him himself; what he wanted was to see it on his own. Elder Qu didn't hurry him. Twice a month, as before, he crossed broom handle and sword with him, and as before touched his shoulder.

The seventh layer had one more benefit, which Master Chu spoke of in the lecture hall. "You're at the seventh layer. The five you learned in your first year, you can put your arms down for those now," he said. "It isn't that seventh-layer people don't have to swing their arms. It's that those five are a long way below you. Spells come in heights too. If you're much higher than the spell, you pinch and it comes out, the incantation just a movement of the lips. If you're not much higher, the hands still have to go half the way. If you're as high as the spell, you swing both arms round full as ever, and not one word of the incantation may be dropped. A fireball, I use one hand; ask me for a Foundation Establishment spell and I swing my arms the same, shout the same, and look no prettier than you did five years ago." He paused. "So if one day you see a Core Formation elder swinging his arms and chanting a long incantation, don't stand and watch. Run."

"Being a step below isn't enough. It has to be familiar too," he said. "When you used to swing the whole set, it was your arms showing your meridians the road. Walk a road a few thousand times, and the body knows it by itself; then the arms can come down." Someone asked if there was a knack to it. Master Chu said, "In *Returning to the Fields*{典=guitian-maiyou} there's an old oil-seller who put a copper coin over the mouth of a gourd and poured the oil in through the hole in the coin, without a drop on the coin. When people asked him, he said, 'There's nothing to it. Only my hand knows it well.'" His goatee twitched up. "Five years ago I told you four words. These are the four words."

That night Xiaoman tried it in the woodshed. Right hand hanging, only the thumb pinched to the tip of the middle finger, a low line under his breath: "Bing and ding well up; gather the qi into a ball." A fireball the size of a fist hung steady at his fingertip, and Xiaozheng's eyes lit up in its glow. Five years ago he'd swung his arms for the best part of an hour and shouted himself hoarse, and got one the size of a chestnut that dropped on Sun Ergou's toe. He switched to the left hand, made the zi seal: an ice spike. Two hands, one thing each, and he didn't need to hold the seals ready first. The seventh-layer spell Master Chu had newly taught, he still had to swing his arms round full for, and not dare drop one word of its eight lines. Only now did he understand what had been going on in Spirit Herb Valley, when Ahe held the vines with one hand and healed from three zhang away with the other: she was two years ahead of him.

The furnace-tending tablet he never handed back. In the pill house, that year Pill-master Min taught him grinding. You ground sitting on a low stool with a copper grinding boat on your knees, one foot working the grinding wheel back and forth. "Solomon's seal, grind it till you can't see a fibre," said Pill-master Min. "Angelica, grind it till you can't smell the sweetness. Grind too much and the medicine's breath escapes; not enough and the fire can't get in." He ground all winter, till with his eyes closed he could tell with a pinch of his fingers how far each herb had been ground. Pill-master Min never praised anyone. Once he picked up some solomon's seal Xiaoman had ground, smelled it, said nothing, and tipped it into the furnace. That furnace gave three one-line pills. When Pill-master Min had counted the lines, he gave Xiaoman a look.

In the third autumn of the five years, Ahe was fifteen, and she read to the end of "Birth", the first chapter of *The Hidden Talisman Classic*. The Abbot called her into the quiet room behind the main hall, took a little porcelain bottle from his sleeve, tipped out a pill and set it in her palm, and said, "Take it tonight." No contest, no list, and no line for it in Zhou Heng's ledger. That night there was no moon; a pot-lid cloud sat over the roof of the main hall. She swallowed the pill and sat cross-legged, and first her dantian warmed, then it was like the ground in spring, a winter's frozen soil loosening layer by layer, with something underneath pushing up, push after push. She was afraid for a while: afraid she couldn't hold, afraid of failing the pill in her master's sleeve. But what she feared most was Pa. Old Zhou had farmed those few mu of slope fields all his life, and every thin year his back bent a little more. The day she came through the mountain gate he stood behind the fence and didn't see her off. She thought of Pa's bent back, and the thing pushing up in her dantian suddenly stopped pushing, went soft, went slow, like rain falling into ground dried right through.

That night, under the pot-lid cloud, a warm wind rose out of nowhere and circled the main hall three times, waking the autumn insects, who sang half the night. A thread of blue light leaked through a gap in the cloud, thin as a silk thread, and fell straight on the roof of the quiet room, and stayed till almost dawn. Under the pot-lid cloud, in the gaps between the tiles along the ridge of the main hall, a row of thin green shoots came up overnight, shining green in the autumn; and in the east corner of the herb garden, the three grain stalks that had been empty for half a year, by dawn, their ears had grown heavier. The Abbot sat outside the quiet room all night, and saw all of it. At dawn he went in to see her. She sat on the cushion with red eyes, but her face was smiling. The Abbot said only one thing: "Of the five chapters of the *Hidden Talisman*, you've finished 'Birth'. From today, you read the second chapter: 'Growth'."

Ahe nodded. But what she was thinking about was something else: who to tell first.

The next evening, Xiaoman's round brought him to the east corner, and as usual he sat on the low wall. Ahe was squatting by the grain stalks, her palm held over the soil, and without turning her head she said, "I've laid it."

Xiaoman said, "Laid what?" The moment it was out, he understood. She was the same back, two plaits, a jacket washed pale; but when he looked at her, the bit of spiritual power he used to be able to see on her was gone, like a lamp someone had put a shade over.

What came out of him first was a laugh, a real laugh, and he jumped down off the low wall laughing: "Really?" Ahe said, "Last night." Xiaoman said, "Did it hurt?" Ahe thought about it and said, "It hurt. A bit more than you on contest day." She added in a low voice, as if afraid the soil would hear: "There's one more thing. Last night, at that moment, an ear of grain appeared in my dantian." Xiaoman looked down at her hand: nothing there, only the little pinch of soil under her palm, which, as she finished speaking, gently heaved. She drew her hand into her sleeve and said no more.

He was still laughing.

Ahe turned her head and looked at him. It was getting dark; one side of her oval face caught the last light from the west, her eyebrows thin and curved, her eyes clear and bright, and when she looked at someone it was as if she were counting something in her head. Xiaoman suddenly felt she'd grown into this without his noticing when. He moved his eyes away to the grain stalks, and couldn't have said what that moment just now had been. She drew back the hand held over the soil, patted her front, and said, very serious: "I'll go up first and look at what it's like up there for you. Which step is slippery, where the holes are, I'll write it all down, one by one, and when you come up, I'll hand it over." A pause. "With interest."

Xiaoman said, "How much?" Ahe said, "Not worked out yet. The later you come up, the more interest."

Xiaoman laughed again. This time it was half a real laugh and half giving in. That night he sat in the woodshed till the candle burned out, and said quietly in the dark: "...Go easy on the interest."

That New Year's Eve Zhou Heng went to the main hall to plead with the Abbot as usual, and pleaded for the same thing: to let him go to Ye and put an end to the "River Lord takes a bride" business on the Zhang. Every year on the eighteenth of the tenth month, Ye threw a girl into the Zhang River, saying she was married to the River Lord; his sister had been one of the ones thrown in. He had pleaded for it year after year. The Abbot said the same thing as always: "Whoever goes has to have Foundation Establishment." Only this time, when he'd said it, he glanced towards the quiet room in the back hall, and said no more.

Zhou Heng came out of the main hall and stood in the gallery a good while. That glance of the Abbot's, he worked over and over; a man who'd kept accounts half his life, and he couldn't make it come out. He walked back, and passing the woodshed saw a little light through the crack in the door. Someone inside was breathing out and in, breath after breath, drawing in very long. Zhou Heng stood outside the door a moment, and didn't knock. He knew who was in there.
