# Now · 2: I Missed Both Free Throws, and Brought Home a Dog

On the afternoon of the fifth day of the National Day holiday, Shen Mang was lying alone on a bench-press bench in the student gym, holding his breath, when everything went black and forty kilos of barbell came down.

In the black was a little stool, and his great-grandmother sat on it. She pulled her reading glasses down her nose. This time she didn't beckon, and she didn't say anything about dumplings. "Mang, love, catch your breath first." She patted her knee. "Your great-grandad pushed a millstone all his life, a breath for every step, and I never saw him hold his breath to push it."

The bar came down on his chest. A little further up, and it would have been his neck.

For this, we have to go back five days.

---

Half past seven on the morning of the first of October, and there was no one in the gym. The whole sports hall was empty. The glass door had a notice on it, "Holiday opening: 7:00 to 21:00"; there was nobody at the front desk, only a water cooler going glug, glug. He lay down on the bench and took hold of the bar, and the bar was warm. The room faced north, so it wasn't the sun. Someone had warmed it with their hands, from one end to the other. First thing in the morning, nobody in the whole building: who had warmed this bar?

"I did." At the far end of the mirror, Ma Xiaobao turned round, towelling his neck. "Just finished. Hundred kilos, five reps." He came over and looked down at Shen Mang on the bench. "You too? Sixteen days to the seventeenth."

Shen Mang recited Ahe's notice to him: trials on the seventeenth of October, three parts, fitness, skills, strength. Pass all three and you were in the rotation; pass two and you were a reserve; pass fewer than two and your name was struck out of the notebook. The strength part: bench press, forty kilos, five reps in a row. How much he could press today, he knew: the empty bar was twenty kilos, five on each end made thirty, and he could do three.

"Ten kilos and two reps short. Child's play." Ma Xiaobao lifted his chin and aimed his nostrils at him. "That's how I trained in high school: train every day, train till you can't push any more. Hold your breath when you push, push it up on one breath. Let the air out and the power goes." He patted the bar. "Go on, you push, I'll watch."

Shen Mang took a deep breath, held it, and pushed. He did one, two, three. As the third went up his face went bright red and his ears were ringing.

"Good pushing." Ma Xiaobao picked up his shaker and headed out. At the door he turned round. "On the seventeenth, for the strength part, I'll be your spotter. I'll count for you myself."

The glass door swung behind him. Shen Mang lay on the bench panting and thought: he taught me that so readily; does he want me to pass, or not to pass? He thought about it a long time and couldn't work it out. He decided to train twice a day.

He'd got that gym card on the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival, three hundred a semester: two hundred of it was his train fare home, and a hundred was his mum's holiday money for him. He'd told his family the school had a training camp over National Day. It was the first lie that big he had ever told them in his life.

---

The next morning he went to the Rare Books Department. The library stayed open over National Day, but the Rare Books Department only opened in the mornings. The room was as cold as ever, and as ever there was only Granny Ge.

"Granny, is there anything about building strength? Something like the *Sinew-Changing Classic*, the kind that someone born without strength can train with?"

Granny Ge looked at him over the top of her reading glasses. "No *Sinew-Changing Classic*. But there's one tough line. Ge Hong copied it into his book from something older: '[My life is in my hands, not heaven's.]{典=baopu-huangbai}'"

Shen Mang ran the seven words over in his mouth. It was like swallowing a hot coal.

But then Granny Ge got going: when she was young and sent down to the countryside, there was a big fellow in the production team who could carry two sacks at once, and later he went to the county haulage station and could eat six steamed buns at a sitting... Shen Mang listened, and all that was left in his head was those seven words. When he went to get his bag on his way out, she seemed to be still going on in there about the haulage station, and threw in one line in passing: "...the ones who hauled sacks, the ones who pounded earth, everybody doing heavy work chanted. The chant isn't sung for anyone to hear. It's so that when you heave, you breathe out."

He said "Oh." The bag lockers stood beside the low bookcase by the door, and he crouched down and once again pulled the first volume of *The Notebooks of the Nine Cauldron Adept* out of the thick case on the bottom shelf, and opened it at random near the front: "Thirteen years old, ninth month, entered the abbey. Tested for spirit on the stone; all five colours lit; the whole hall laughed." Below, on a new line: "An old man who swept the floor looked at me once: steady legs, careful heart."

What abbey, what stone, he didn't know. A whole hall laughing at one child, that he understood. He pushed the volume back, shouldered his bag and left. He took those seven words away with him; the chant and the notebooks, he didn't give a second thought.

---

For the seven days of National Day, there was only him left in 617.

Old Cai had gone home, Specs had gone home. The fourth, empty bed had been piled with Old Cai's boxes of instant noodles to begin with; from the third day on, two more boxes were stacked on top. They were Shen Mang's. He had done the sums: October's living allowance had come on the first, with the two hundred train fare he'd already spent taken out of it; then the card, then the holiday money spent too. What was left had to last till the first of November, and it was only enough for instant noodles. He put a ham sausage in every bowl and told himself that counted as adding meat.

His phone was keeping the accounts for him too. Over the summer his mum's cousin had installed that Linggen app for him, and its accounts page was a circle cut into slices: the gym card, three hundred, one big slice; a tub of protein powder ordered on the first of October, another slice; the "Food" slice thin as a single noodle. He looked at that noodle a while, then pressed the screen dark. Linggen knew about these accounts. His mum couldn't know, and Ahe even less.

Once in the morning, once in the afternoon, every day he pushed until he couldn't push. At night he lay alone on the lower bunk scrolling bench-press videos until two.

On the fourth night his mum video-called. "Is the training camp tiring?" "Tiring, yeah, pretty tiring." "Just you? What about the two in your room?" "...They're not on the university team." His mum squinted into the screen. "What's that you're holding?" He moved the bowl out of shot. "Late-night snack. I finished the canteen food." "Have you got enough money?" "Yeah, I've got enough."

When the call ended he ate the half sausage. One lie, how many lies does it take to cover it? He counted. Today's was the third.

On the fifth day the north wind had blown all night, and in the morning the sky was a shining blue. He carried his quilt up to the roof to air it, and looking northwest he saw a blue-grey ridge of hills along the edge of the sky, layer over layer, faint as if traced in pencil. The dorm warden, beating a quilt, said, "Mount Meng. Seventy-odd kilometres. You don't see it more than a few times a year." He had grown up on the Dezhou plain and in eighteen years had never seen a real mountain. He couldn't have said what was good about that ridge. He just looked at it, and as he looked, some place inside him went quiet for a moment.

That afternoon he put himself up to forty. Five days of pushing, and thirty was still three reps, not one more. He was panicking: five days of training and nothing gained; what about the seventeenth? My life is in my hands: then he wasn't training hard enough. Forty, even one rep would do; one rep, and he'd know he could reach it.

There was no one else in the gym. He took a deep breath, held it, lifted the bar off the rack, brought it down to his chest, and pushed. Halfway up, the bar stopped. Holding that breath, he gritted his teeth and pushed up against it. His face went red, then purple, the veins on his neck stood out one by one, the ringing in his ears turned to a roar, everything in front of him went white in rings, and the white closed in from the edges, closed into a slit...

Everything went black.

---

It was the bar that woke him. Forty kilos lying across his chest, crushing the breath out of him, both hands still gripping it, gripping till his fingers were white. He didn't know how many seconds he'd been out. He wanted to shout and couldn't; he wanted to push and couldn't. Pushing against the floor with his feet, he rolled the bar down bit by bit onto his stomach, rolled it to the tops of his thighs, and only then sat up. His chest was burning, a red mark lying right across it.

The glass door banged, and Ma Xiaobao came in with his shaker, saw him, and stopped. "...What are you doing?"

"I was just... resting."

Ma Xiaobao looked at the bar that had rolled onto his legs, then at the red mark on his chest, and the smile left his face. He lifted the bar back onto the rack with one hand, like putting away a clothes pole. "Pushing on your own? No spotter? I taught you to hold your breath, I didn't teach you to hold it on your own!" He took out his phone. "Manager, gym, the new guy's trying to kill himself!"

Shen Mang tried to grab the phone and missed.

Ahe was there in ten minutes, her white coat still on, her ponytail come loose from running. Shen Mang had known her nearly a month, and it was the first time he'd seen her run. She crouched down and looked at his eyes first, had him follow her finger left and right, asked him the date and where he was, and pressed the red mark on his chest, and he flinched.

"How long did everything go black for?"

"...I don't know."

"When you pushed, were you holding your breath?"

Shen Mang said nothing. Beside them, Ma Xiaobao coughed. Ahe stood up and gave Ma Xiaobao a look and didn't say anything. Just that look, and Ma Xiaobao headed for the door of his own accord. "I'll go down and get a bottle of water."

Ahe dragged a mat over from the corner, had him lie flat with his legs up on the bench, pulled up a stool and sat by his head, took the cap off her pen and clicked it back on. "Hold your breath and force it, and the pressure in your chest shoots up all at once. The blood coming back to your heart can't get in, the heart can't pump it out, and your brain's supply gets cut first. Then when you let go after pushing, your blood pressure drops through the floor. Today you blacked out on a bench, and the bar came down on your chest. If it had come down on your neck, you wouldn't be sitting here listening to me."

Shen Mang kept his head down.

"Take a deep breath and brace your stomach: that part's right." As she said it she put out one finger and jabbed him in the stomach without warning. Shen Mang went "Hey!" and his stomach tightened by instinct. "That's the effort. Like when someone's about to punch you in the gut. All weightlifters do it: the pressure in your belly holds your back up, so the power can get through. But it's a one-off: brace, push, breathe out. Not holding a breath and fighting it out. When the bar's coming down, breathe in, brace. On the way up, through the hardest part, keep bracing and let it leak out as you go, make a sound, 'sss' is fine, 'hhh' is fine, just don't hold it till you drop."

Ma Xiaobao came back with a bottle of water and put it by the mat, not daring to come close. Ahe unscrewed the cap and handed it over. "Drink all of it, and lie there a full half hour before you get up."

Shen Mang lay on the mat drinking the water mouthful by mouthful. Ahe's phone rang twice; she said, "I've got someone here, I'll call you back later," hung up, and bent over her notebook writing up her own things. For the half hour she didn't look at the time once, and when it was up, only then did she raise her head. "Get up. Still dizzy?"

"Not any more."

She took the plates down to thirty kilos and stood over his head, her two hands held ready under the bar.

He brought the bar down, breathed in, his belly swelled, he braced, and pushed. "Hhhh..." The breath went out through his teeth bit by bit, the bar went up bit by bit, and nothing went white in front of his eyes and nothing roared in his ears. That was the first. He brought it down again, breathed in; pushed again, hhhh: the second. Then the third, the fourth. The fifth stopped for a moment halfway; the "hhh" in his mouth went a little longer, the bar wobbled, and in the end it went up, and clanked back onto the rack.

Thirty kilos, and he had pushed five in a row. At this weight, for the last five days, he'd only ever managed three.

He lay on the bench looking at the ceiling, still letting that breath out, his chest warm inside. Ahe didn't smile. She made a note in her notebook and said, "It's not that you've got stronger. It's that for the last five days you've been holding your breath till you blacked yourself out by the third rep."

Shen Mang suddenly remembered Granny Ge's line by the door: the ones who hauled sacks, when they heaved, they breathed out. So it hadn't been rambling after all. She had rambled on a long time, and the one line she'd slipped in on the way was the useful one, and at the time he hadn't thought anything of it and had shouldered his bag and left.

She asked him three more things. "What time do you go to bed?" "...Midnight." He gave her a number off her own chart. "How often do you train?" "Every day. Twice a day."

Ahe's pen stopped. "The same muscle, pushed every day: when's it supposed to grow?" She tapped the notebook with her pen. "Training is putting in an order. Muscle grows while it rests. Enough sleep, enough food, and then it grows. Once you've worked a muscle, leave it a day or two before you work it again."

"Then what should I eat?"

"Eggs, milk, meat, tofu. One of them in every meal." She looked up. "What do you usually eat?"

The red mark on his chest still burned. He thought of the three boxes of instant noodles stacked on the fourth bed, of his mum's "Have you got enough money?", of what it would be like if under his line in her notebook she added two words, "Can't afford"...

"I eat in the canteen."

She looked at him a moment, said "Mm," and wrote it down. He watched the pen go down and felt that what she'd written wasn't the word "canteen". It was him.

That night he boiled a kettle, opened a packet of instant noodles and added a ham sausage. He watched the sausage float up in the soup and sink. He fished it out and ate it and didn't taste a thing.

---

On the seventh of October, Old Cai and Specs came back. The moment Old Cai came in and saw the stack of boxes on the fourth bed, he stopped dead. "What's this... stocking up for winter?" Specs didn't laugh. He picked up a packet, turned it over to look at the table on the back, took out his phone and tapped at it a long while. "To build muscle you need about one and a half grams of protein per kilo of bodyweight a day. You're fifty-nine kilos, so ninety grams a day. One of these packets is about ten." He put it down. "You'd have to eat nine packets a day."

Old Cai said, "Nine packets, that's not building muscle, that's building noodles."

Shen Mang didn't answer. He did as Ahe said: bed before midnight, push one day, rest one day, breathe out as he pushed.

The day Ma Xiaobao ran into him resting at the gym, he was on a treadmill shaking his shaker. "Resting? I taught you to train every day, she's teaching you every other day. Who are you listening to?"

"I'm listening to the one who doesn't make people hold their breath."

Ma Xiaobao was taken aback for a second, the shaker stopped in mid-air, and only after a moment did he say, "Fine. See you on the seventeenth, then."

From then till the trials, every other day Ahe came to the gym in her lunch break to spot him for half an hour. She stood over his head, her two hands held ready under the bar, counting the reps one by one, and when she'd counted she wrote it down. Lying on the bench he saw her upside down: chin, the tip of her ponytail, and a pen that bobbed up and down. His strength really was going up: thirty kilos for five; thirty-five for five; forty, first three, later four. But every morning when he squeezed his arm, it was still the same skinny arm.

Once, when he'd finished pushing and was lying there panting, she suddenly asked, "Why did you come to play basketball?"

His heart leapt into his throat. He stared at the strip light on the ceiling. "...Wanted to finish the thousand metres."

"You've already finished the thousand metres."

"Then... wanted to run it a bit faster."

Ahe said "Mm," didn't ask again, and bent her head to write something down. Shen Mang lay there feeling that the breath he'd just held had been held longer than when he'd pushed forty.

---

On the sixteenth of October, the day before the trials, Ahe weighed him in the equipment room: fifty-eight point nine kilos. Compared with the line for the fourteenth of September, his weight was down point seven, and his body fat up point four.

"Your weight's dropped," Ahe said.

"But I've got stronger."

"You've got stronger because your brain's learned to make everything heave at once. The first month or two, that's mostly what grows," Ahe said. "As for muscle, not an ounce."

Shen Mang squeezed his skinny arm. "I was born with this build. Other people are born one ninety-three."

She added a line under that line and looked up. "What you're born with sets the ceiling. How far you are from it, that's training. You're a long way from your ceiling. Provided it's got something to grow with." She looked at him. "What do you eat? Tell me again."

The door of the equipment room was open, and out in the hall someone was shooting baskets, bang, bang.

"...I eat in the canteen."

Ahe didn't ask again. She closed her notebook. "Trials at eight tomorrow morning."

That night he couldn't sleep. Twelve, one. On the top bunk Old Cai rolled over and said into the dark, "Busy Mang, can't sleep? I'll teach you a trick." He cleared his throat. "Hhhhh..." He let out a long breath. It was what Shen Mang had taught him three weeks before. Shen Mang smiled in the dark and breathed out after him: "Hhhh..." One breath from the top bunk, one from the bottom; across the room, Specs swore from under his quilt, "Balloons again," rolled over, and after a while breathed one out too. He didn't know which breath he was on when he fell asleep.

---

The seventeenth of October, Saturday, eight in the morning, the ground floor of the sports hall. The twenty-three who had come for the trials stood in three rows. The old team members sat at the side to watch, Ma Xiaobao in front, the big shaker at his feet.

First, fitness: the twenty-metre shuttle run, back and forth to the beeps on a recording, the beeps getting faster each time, and if you couldn't keep up, you were out. Shen Mang had been panicking all morning, but once he started running, his legs knew this. For a month he hadn't missed his twenty minutes a single day. The beeps got faster and faster, people dropped out one by one, and he was still running: out, in, out. Near the end his legs stopped obeying him again and his throat started burning again, just like the fitness test in September. At the side Ma Xiaobao yelled, "If you can't hack it, come off, don't throw up on the floor!" and that yell woke him up. He didn't snatch for air at his throat; he breathed out, breathed out clean, and held on for three more lengths, held on until a beep went and his foot hadn't reached the line, and only then stopped. Out of twenty-three, he came fourteenth. Someone at the side said "Huh."

Second, skills. Dribbling round cones to a layup, he knocked over a cone and missed the layup, put back the rebound, and it went in; free throws, three out of ten, and the pass mark was exactly three, right on the line. Coach Lei put a tick on his sheet.

Third, strength. The bench was set in the very middle of the court: forty kilos. Ma Xiaobao got up, sauntered round behind the bench, held his two hands ready under the bar and winked at him. "Go on, I'll count for you." Then he leaned down and lowered his voice. "You've blacked out once. If you black out again today, I'll be catching you right in front of her."

Shen Mang lay down. Twenty-odd people round him, a row of old team members at the side, Ahe behind the scorer's table with her notebook open. He felt that every eye in the hall had landed on the red mark on his chest that still hadn't quite faded.

He brought the bar down, breathed in, and pushed. "Hhhh..."

"One!" Ma Xiaobao shouted it for the whole hall to hear. "Two!" "Three!"

The fourth slowed halfway, slow as an old ox that can't pull the cart. The "hhh" in his mouth went long and loud. He didn't hold the breath; it went out thread by thread, and the bar edged up inch by inch, and went up. "...Four."

The fifth came down to his chest, went up a fist's height, and stopped. He pushed against it, breathing out, and couldn't move it; he didn't hold his breath. He remembered his great-grandmother on her little stool saying "Catch your breath first." The bar didn't move at all. Ma Xiaobao's hands came up under it and took the bar away and put it back on the rack.

"Only four. One short." Ma Xiaobao announced it to the whole hall, then patted the bar. "Our team mascot. Gave it his all."

The old team members at the side burst out laughing, and the laughter spread through half the hall. Shen Mang sat up on the bench and sat right in the middle of that laughter, his face burning from his ears down to his neck. He thought of that line in the notebooks: the whole hall laughed. One child, a whole hall.

Behind the scorer's table, Ahe didn't laugh. She bent her head and wrote something, then looked up. "Next." The laughter paused, and the next person stood up.

At noon the list came out, and Ahe stood in front of the scorer's table reading it out name by name. Four had passed all three parts and were in the rotation; three had passed two and were reserves. When she reached the reserves she read them just as she had the others, not a word stressed: "...Third reserve, Shen Mang." Third reserve was the last of the seven. After that she didn't read out any more names.

Coach Lei closed his sheet and said to the old team members, "Fourteenth out of twenty-three. Last month, dead last in the thousand metres. Passed two parts out of three; by the rules, reserve. There's nothing funny about it."

Ma Xiaobao came over and patted him on the shoulder. "Congratulations. You're the last one in." Then he lowered his voice. "Passed two, failed one. That thing I was going to tell her for you, I'll hang on to it for you for now."

In the afternoon the team had its photo taken. All twenty of them stood under the basket, a row crouching in front, a row standing behind. A few of the old team members started fooling about and, with a shout of "Up!", hoisted Ma Xiaobao onto their shoulders, and he sat up there on those shoulders, one hand raising his shaker, grinning at the camera. Shen Mang stood at the very end. The student taking the photo called out, "The one on the far right, move in a bit!" From up on the shoulders Ma Xiaobao added, "The extra! Move in!" Everyone below laughed. He squeezed in a little and couldn't squeeze in.

The photo went into the team group chat. He zoomed in: Ma Xiaobao sitting high at the very top; him at the far right, half of him out of the frame, only an arm, half a face, half a smile left. He looked at that half a face for a long time and in the end saved it. It was the first team photo since he'd made the team, and he was in it.

---

Monday the twenty-sixth of October, two in the afternoon, the anatomy lab. When the white sheet came back it was the chest again; this session was the heart.

The heart of their Silent Teacher, Liu Guizhi, was smaller than he'd imagined, the size of a fist, and dark in colour. The lecturer pointed with her tweezers to the top of the heart, to two white threads running down from the neck, finer than shoelaces. "The vagus nerve. One on each side, down from the base of the skull, along the great vessels all the way, to the heart, to the lungs, to the stomach and gut. The nerves that run the organs come in two sets: one hurries things up, coming out either side of the spine; when a person's anxious or afraid, it speeds the heart up. One calms things down, and that's these two. When it speaks, the heart slows."

They took turns to look. When it was Shen Mang's turn he leaned in close. The white thread lay quietly beside the vessel, doing nothing. He put up his hand. "Then how do you get it to speak?"

The lecturer gave him a look. This boy always asked one thing that wasn't in the book. "It doesn't take orders from you directly," she said. "But it moves with your breathing: when you breathe out, it speaks a bit more. Why, you'll get in physiology next semester."

Shen Mang said "Oh." Breathing out, he could do; what breathing had to do with heartbeat, he couldn't think, so he didn't give it a second thought.

---

On the night of the thirtieth of October the list for the inter-department cup went into the group chat: the Acupuncture and Tuina school team had entered twelve, and he was the twelfth. At centre was Big Liu, the one-eighty-eight who'd run off to the street-dance club in September and been dragged back by Ahe with one entry form. Ma Xiaobao was back with the School of Chinese Pharmacy, on the other side. Below that someone had forwarded a notice from the school, and Old Cai read it out to him: "Each school's team funding for the next year will be set by its inter-department cup placing; teams not in the top four will no longer be fielded separately." Acupuncture and Tuina had come sixth last year. In the "Team manager" box on the form, the signature was Ahe's. This team she had pulled together class by class, entry form by entry form, on her own two feet.

The next morning he took a ball, meaning to go and shoot a few at the hall first. The hall door was locked, with a sheet of paper on it: "Scrimmage this afternoon, cleaning this morning." So he made do with the concrete in front of the dorm block, rehearsing his shooting action at a plane tree. By the twentieth-something go, a window opened on the sixth floor and Old Cai leaned half out with his phone pointed at him. "Go on, Busy, one more, I'm streaming." "Streaming what?" "To the dorm group. Specs says he's going to do a scientific analysis of your release angle." Specs's voice came out of the window: "I didn't say that. What I said was, when he lifts his elbow, his wrist is crooked." Shen Mang hugged the ball to his chest and stopped rehearsing. Whether his wrist was crooked or not, he didn't dare look down to see.

The afternoon of the thirty-first of October: the team scrimmage. A new notice had been put up at the hall door: "1st to 15th December: hall floor being replaced." Nobody gave it a second look.

The stands held nearly a hundred people. Nobody ever watched team scrimmages, but this time somehow word had got round that the inter-department cup lists had just gone in, and people from every school had come to see who was who on the university team. The School of Chinese Pharmacy had brought a whole row, every one in a new jersey, with "Ma's Pharmacy" across the chest dazzling under the lights. Old Cai and Specs sat in the third row, Old Cai with a bag of sunflower seeds, Specs with a book.

Shen Mang sat at the very end of the White team's bench, and sat there for fifty-seven minutes. In those fifty-seven minutes he fetched nineteen balls, handed out thirty-one bottles of water, and clapped who knew how many times, till his palms were red. He also watched Ma Xiaobao take six free throws.

Ma Xiaobao took free throws like drinking water. He walked up to the line, waved to the stands first, and someone in the stands screamed; bounced the ball once, turned his head to tell the referee a joke, and even the referee couldn't help laughing; then tossed it up without a care, swish, not even touching the rim. Five out of six. The one he missed, he laughed first himself and spread his hands at the stands, and the stands laughed louder than when he scored.

Shen Mang sat on the bench watching, and went cold inside. He had understood one thing: that ring of eyes, on Ma Xiaobao, was central heating; on him, it was an icehouse.

Then things happened one after another, as if someone had lined them up: the captain, Xing Bin, fouled out with his fifth; the White team's power forward stopped short and his calf cramped, and he rolled on the floor clutching his leg; one had jammed a finger in the first half and it was taped up like a carrot; one had just gone to the toilet and not come back; the other two reserves had lab classes and hadn't come. Red fifty-eight, White fifty-seven, and only a fraction of a second left on the clock. Coach Lei looked at the clock, then at the bench. There was only one person left on the bench.

He waved at that person. "Zero! You're on!"

Shen Mang stood up, his legs like jelly. Coach Lei grabbed him by the jersey and bellowed in a low voice, "When you get the ball, pass! Pass to anyone! Don't dribble, don't shoot, don't think!" He nodded like a pestle pounding garlic. As he walked onto the court the stands went "Ohhh...", long and drawn out; some laughed, some held up their phones.

White inbounded from the backcourt, and the ball came to him. He caught it. That was already his biggest achievement of the day. Before he'd worked out where to run, Ma Xiaobao came down on him with his whole body, reached into his arms as if going for the ball, and then, with the same movement, closed both arms round him and gave him a good hard hug, as fond as meeting a distant relative at the railway station. Whistle. Red had used up its fouls for the quarter long ago; one more, and it was two shots. Ma Xiaobao raised his hand to the referee. "My foul, put it on me." Then he looked down at Shen Mang and smiled. "Sending you to the line."

He had two free throws. Make one, and it was a tie; make both, and White won.

That white line: he'd watched it from the side for a month and never thought it far away. Standing on it now, the basket seemed a hundred miles off, small and high, like a bowl hung in the sky. Nearly a hundred people in the stands, and not a sound from any of them. No sound was more frightening than sound. He could hear his own heart, thump, thump, fast as someone stamping on the floor upstairs. His fitness band said a heart rate of a hundred and forty-one.

He bounced the ball twice. The ball was wet. It was his palms that were wet.

The first one dropped straight down in front of the rim without touching anything. The stands erupted. Some laughed till they cried, someone shouted "Airball!", and in the third row Old Cai spilled his sunflower seeds all over the floor.

From the bench, two voices reached him at once. Coach Lei's was loud: "Deep breath! Hold it!" Ahe's was neither loud nor hurried: "Breathe out first!"

Coach Lei's was louder, so he listened to Coach Lei, took a deep breath, held it till his neck swelled, and threw the second one up. Clang, off the front of the rim. The final whistle went.

Ma Xiaobao came over and slapped him twice on the back, hard enough to send him lurching forward, and said, not loudly, but loud enough for the first three rows of the stands to hear, "At the inter-department cup I'll foul you again. Foul you once, give you two." The stands laughed again. Shen Mang couldn't hear what was funny in that. What he heard was a game plan: any match from now on, as long as he was on the court, the other side wouldn't guard him, wouldn't go for the ball, would just hug him; one hug, and he'd be standing on that line, alone, with eyes on every side.

---

The losing side swept the hall on Sunday morning; as the White players left, they shoved a broom into his arms. "You missed the free throws, you sweep." At seven on Sunday morning the hall entrance would be full of people doing their morning exercise, and he didn't want to sweep in front of them. So that evening, in the empty hall, there was only one person, one broom, and, behind a folding table, one team manager.

Ahe was doing the accounts. The kit, the courts and the referees for all eight schools in the inter-department cup were in that notebook of hers, and her calculator went tap tap tap. At one page she paused and added a line; Shen Mang, sweeping near the table, craned his neck and caught a glimpse: "Shen Mang, free throws 0/2, holds breath before release."

"He hugged me on purpose," Shen Mang said.

"That's called Hack-a-Shaq." Ahe didn't look up. "Back in the day, the biggest centre in America, O'Neal, was strong enough to pull the basket down, he just couldn't do free throws. So the other side sent someone out specially to foul him: hug him, pull him, put him on the line. Every one he missed was a free possession."

"So I get the same treatment as O'Neal."

"He was two sixteen." She put her pen down. "Come here. Hold out your hand."

Shen Mang leaned the broom against the table. She turned his right hand palm up, then drew two fingers of his left hand over and pressed them on his right wrist, on the thumb side. "Don't look at the band. Feel it with your fingers."

He found it: the pulse in his wrist, one beat, one beat.

"Breathe in slowly. Count four."

He breathed in as she said, and by the third count the beat under his fingers had tightened, quickened a little.

"Now breathe out slowly. Count six."

Halfway through the breath out, the beat loosened, the gaps between one and the next grew longer, like someone walking slowing down their pace. His heartbeat really had calmed down. He breathed in again: faster again. Out again: slower again. He stared at her, wide-eyed, like a small child finding out for the first time that he has a shadow. "It... it goes with my breathing?"

"It always has. Breathing in, your heart beats a bit faster; breathing out, a bit slower. Everyone's the same," Ahe said. "Breathing in is the accelerator, breathing out is the brake. Breathe out longer than you breathe in, and the heart slows a notch."

Those two white threads he'd seen in anatomy (the lecturer had called them the vagus nerve) suddenly seemed to stir under his fingers. The lecturer had said, when you breathe out, it speaks a bit more. So this was what she meant.

"The moment you stand on the free-throw line your heart rate's a hundred and forty-one, and then you take a huge breath in and hold it." Ahe tapped the notebook with her pen. "Holding it, you can't put the brake on. It's the breathing out that's the brake."

"So one breath out and I stop shaking?"

"Not like that." She answered fast. "When you're scared, the stuff your body lets out takes minutes to go down. One breath out won't clear it. But it can push it down a notch. And a notch is enough for you to get the ball onto the rim. Before you shoot, first breathe out long, longer than you breathe in. When you've breathed out, then shoot."

"I know how to breathe out. The day of the resit, I ran the whole thing breathing out."

"I know. That day you knew how, and today, the moment you stood on the line, you forgot." She paused. "Besides, that day you breathed out to empty the old air from your lungs so the new could come in. That's running breathing-out. Today's breathing-out is the brake. One breath, two uses."

Shen Mang thought of the page he'd copied out in the Rare Books Department, pressed in his drawer. "In that old book I copied from, there's one way of breathing in and six of breathing out."

"Six," Ahe said, "and all of them are out."

She looked at him. The look was a little different from the way she usually looked at a number. She bent her head and wrote down one more thing.

When he'd finished sweeping, he took a ball from the ball cart and walked to the white line. Half the lights in the hall were off, and in the half-light the basket seemed closer, if anything. He remembered what Specs had said that morning about his crooked wrist, and as he lifted his elbow he set his wrist straight. In for four, out for six; in again, out again. At the end of the second breath out, the drumming in his chest slowed and his hands were steady. He shot.

The first went in, the second went in too, the third hit the rim, the fourth went in again.

A rush of warmth went through him, and he turned round to shout, and saw someone leaning in the doorway, one ninety-three, holding the big shaker he'd left behind, watching with a grin.

He turned back. In for four, out for six. The beating in his chest wouldn't slow down at all. The ball hit the backboard and bounced far away.

Ma Xiaobao unscrewed the shaker and took a swig. "See? Soon as anyone's watching, you can't do it." He wandered off, shaking his shaker.

The ball rolled under the folding table. Ahe bent down and picked it up. "He's right. Nobody watching, three out of four. Somebody watching, not one. Just practising shooting is no use. From next week, after every practice, the whole team stands on the baseline and watches you shoot ten. Every one you miss, the whole team runs a shuttle with you."

The colour drained from Shen Mang's face. "The whole team? All watching me shoot?"

"The whole team, all watching you shoot." She pushed the ball back into his arms. "The first round of the inter-department cup is the seventh of November. On that day there'll be ten times as many people in the stands as today."

He held it in a long while and finally came out with: "How come he's not scared?"

"What people are scared of isn't the same for everyone. He's got something he's scared of too."

"What's he scared of?"

"I don't know. I've never written it down."

It was the first time he'd heard her say there was something she'd never written down.

She picked up the folding table and walked to the door, then turned round. "In the game today, when I shouted at you to breathe out, you breathed in instead."

Shen Mang stood in the middle of the empty hall with the ball in his arms, faced the white line, and let out another long breath.

---

He didn't go to the canteen for supper that night. The year group chat already had the video, captioned "On how far a free throw can be from the basket"; on the campus confessions wall someone had posted, "Who's number Zero? Can we send him to the inter-department cup? Our school wants to draw Acupuncture and Tuina." At just after six his mum sent a sixty-second voice message. First line: "Mang, love, that one shooting baskets in the parents' group, that's you again, isn't it?" Second line was his dad, far from the phone, loud: "Free throws, I know those, you just stand and shoot, nobody stops you. If you can't make it standing up, next time shoot sitting down." The remaining fifty seconds were his mum telling his dad off.

He put the phone face down on the desk. Across the room, Specs looked up from behind a library copy of the *Zhuangzi*, his finger on a line. "'[Follow the Du as your constant line.]{典=zhuangzi-yangshengzhu}' The Du is the line right down the middle of your spine. When you took the second free throw, your back was hunched."

"What about the brake line, then?" said Shen Mang. "Ahe says breathing out is putting on the brake. Those two white threads in anatomy..."

Specs thought about it. "The vagus nerve comes down both sides of the neck and runs the heart, the lungs, the belly; it goes down the front of the body. If you really have to find it a seat, put it on the Ren line, the one right down the middle of the front." He pushed his glasses up. "That's an analogy. The medical books don't say that, so don't go putting it in an exam."

On the top bunk Old Cai slapped the bed board. "Got it! Back straight, that's the Du; one long breath out, that's the Ren. First step in Qi Condensation: open up the Ren and Du channels!" Specs choked on his own breath and went red in the face. He opened his mouth to swear at him, thought about it, and didn't.

---

After nine he was too hungry to hold out, but he didn't want to walk past a thousand-odd pairs of eyes carrying a tray, so he went round to the back door of Canteen One. Out the back was the rubbish point, a row of big green bins, a dim yellow light beside them, and at the door an old man with a baked-sweet-potato cart halfway through packing up. He paid three yuan for the last one. The old man, poking his fire hook into the stove, jerked his chin towards the bins. "Watch out for the little thing. Somebody dumped it here over the summer. Got kicked about. Won't let anyone near. A couple of days back two students were teasing it with a ham sausage, holding their hands out to it, and it nearly had them." With that he wheeled his cart away.

From behind the bins came a low "Rrr."

In the shadow of the light was a mongrel, not big, its reddish-yellow coat matted into clumps with dirt, a tuft of hair standing straight up in the middle of its forehead like a horn. It crouched low, ears flat back, growling in its throat, its eyes fixed hard on the sweet potato in his hand, and then on his face.

When Shen Mang was small, out in the Dezhou countryside, the neighbours' big yellow dog had chased him down three streets and torn a hole in the seat of his trousers; his mum had mended that hole, and he still remembered it. His legs went first, and his heart leapt up, faster than on the free-throw line that afternoon. The dog was afraid too: shrinking back, the hair on its back rising strand by strand, tail clamped between its hind legs, the sound in its throat getting fiercer and fiercer.

First he tried the two students' way: stood up straight, held the sweet potato out, and coaxed, "Come on, come on, come on." The dog shrank back, then lunged forward, snap, its teeth closing half a foot in front of his fingers. He jumped back, higher than he'd ever jumped for anything in his life, and nearly dropped the sweet potato. His mum had drummed it into him since he was small: if a dog so much as scratches you, you go and get the rabies jabs, every single one. Half a foot closer and he'd have spent tonight queuing at the campus clinic. The dog backed off behind the bin, the hair on its back bristling higher, and the sound in its throat twice as fierce.

One afraid, one more afraid; one fiercer the more it was afraid, one more afraid the fiercer it got. Suddenly he felt he'd seen this before: that afternoon, on the free-throw line, the more afraid he was the more he held his breath, and the more he held it the more he shook. Shen Mang, oh Shen Mang, on the free-throw line you're scared of a ring of people, and here you're scared of a dog. Today you've done the whole set of being scared.

He told himself: breathe out.

He slowly turned side-on, crouched down, and looked at the ground. He breathed in, counting four; breathed out, counting six, out long and slow, as if letting a whole day's breath out of his body. One, two, three. The jumping in his chest slowed down, and the sound in the dog's throat stopped too.

He broke the sweet potato in two and put the bigger half on his open palm, the back of his hand to the ground, not pushing it forward, and just crouched there, waiting patiently. The dog's nose moved first, but its eyes stayed nailed to his face, nailed there for a good half minute, before it inched over step by step, stretched its neck out, took the half sweet potato from his palm in its teeth, and backed off under the light to eat it, two bites, a look up at him.

He crouched without moving. The hand holding his own half of the sweet potato shook on his knee, again and again; not shaking from fear any more, but from glee. Inside, in a very small voice, he shouted:

Done it!

When the dog had finished, it licked up even the crumbs on the ground, hesitated a while, slowly came two steps closer, sniffed his slipper, and lay down one step away from him, chin on its front paws, eyes half open, half shut, still keeping an eye on him. Round its neck hung a green plastic tag, and he leaned over to read it by the light: "Yizhou University of Chinese Medicine Animal Welfare Society · Neutered · Vaccinated". He breathed out in relief. A moment ago he'd been so busy being pleased that this hadn't even crossed his mind.

"What's your name?" The dog ignored him. "Then you're Sweet Potato. You ate half my sweet potato, so you're Sweet Potato." With one hand he took out his phone and snapped a photo: the dim yellow light, one slipper, and a step away, a fuzzy head. He sent it to Ahe.

Ahe replied: "Dogs can't join the team." A while later she asked, "How many counts in, how many out?"

"Four in, six out. Five breaths out in all."

"I've written it down."

Under the light at the rubbish point, one person crouching, one dog lying down, and nobody watching. When nobody was watching, he could do it. But on the seventh of November there'd be a thousand pairs of eyes in the stands. Sweet Potato rolled over, turned the other cheek, and at last shut its eyes. His legs had long since gone numb from crouching, so numb they felt like someone else's, and he still didn't dare move. The light was still on in the canteen's back kitchen, where someone was scrubbing pots, water splashing.

---

At six in the morning on the second of November he was alone on the free-throw line, in for four, out for six. Coach Lei came in. "Before a free throw, take a deep breath and hold it, that's what steadies your hands. I've coached teams twenty years."

He pressed two of Coach Lei's fingers onto Coach Lei's own wrist. "Breathe in for four... out for six."

Halfway through the breath out, Coach Lei's eyebrows twitched, and he put his hands behind his back. "...Keep shooting."

At the other end of the hall: creak, creak, creak. Ma Xiaobao was shaking his big shaker, a one-litre cup with a stainless-steel whisk ball inside banging against the sides, creak, creak, like a hungry rat grinding its teeth. He shook it thirty times, unscrewed the lid, tipped back his head and downed half, tipped in two more scoops of powder and went on shaking. "Four a day. One in the morning, one after training, one in the afternoon, one before bed." He smacked his lips. "Drink it all, and still hungry."

At twenty past six Coach Lei came in carrying a big cardboard box and dumped it in the centre circle. On its side were four red characters: Ma's Pharmacy. "This month's sponsorship. Share it out among yourselves." A crowd gathered round and opened the box like New Year presents. First, whey protein powder, a tub big enough to sit on; second, a tin of white powder, "creatine monohydrate"; third, pressed in at the bottom, a stack of little golden boxes, a red horse carrying a great antler on the front, and six big gold-stamped characters: **Dragon-Horse Power Capsules**, and under them a line in small print: "Thousand-Year-Old Recipe · Ginseng & Deer Antler Essence · Triple Muscle Gain". The protein powder and creatine were shared out among a few of the old team members; the stack of Dragon-Horses the old members didn't even look at, and a few of the new ones grabbed every last one. Shen Mang was too slow and didn't get any.

Ma Xiaobao picked up a box and tossed it into his arms. "Here. Teammate price, zero yuan. This is the triple-strength one. Next time I foul you, at least you'll have the strength to get the ball to the rim."

Shen Mang held the golden box as if holding a gold bar fresh from the furnace. Triple muscle gain. On the day of the trials he couldn't get the fifth rep up, and Ma Xiaobao had taken the bar off him; his strength line in Ahe's notebook was still red. He turned the box over and over, and his heart was beating faster.

"How many do you take at a time?"

"I don't take any." Ma Xiaobao shook his shaker. "That's for selling to outsiders. My dad said."

"Then what do you take?"

Ma Xiaobao weighed the shaker in his palm and didn't turn round. "Imported. The NBA all use it." Creak, creak, and off he went.

The tip of Ahe's pen stopped on her notebook for a moment. She took the box from his hands, turned it to the back and pointed at a line of print smaller than ants: "Ginseng extract. Deer antler powder. Caffeine, a hundred milligrams per capsule: one capsule's the same as a cup of Americano. Caffeine is real; it can make you feel strong and wide awake for a while. But it's borrowed, and what you borrow you pay back. A borrowed accelerator doesn't build muscle." She pointed her pen at the box. "One box, three things. Protein powder is food, not medicine, it's taken out of milk, same idea as an egg. Creatine is some use. This," the tip of her pen tapped the golden box, "saying it builds muscle is a lie. Three things in that box: one real, one half useful, one fake."

At the side of the court Specs held up his phone. "I looked it up. In the *Materia Medica*, the entry on ginseng: '[Taken long, it lightens the body and lengthens the years.]{典=shennong-renshen}' Not one word about building muscle."

Ahe reached into Shen Mang's schoolbag and pulled out a folded sheet of A4: the "Shen Mang · Menu (Two Weeks)" she'd given him at the canteen door yesterday. On the paper was a ring of grease, exactly the size of a bowl's rim, soaked into the word "Rice".

"That's the mark of an instant-noodle bowl," she said. "In October I asked you what you ate, and you said the canteen."

Shen Mang's face burned. This was the moment he'd been dreading most: in her notebook, under his line, the two words "Can't afford".

Ahe's pen didn't come down. She folded the paper and put it back. "Tell the truth next time. No money, the menu can change."

That night, Shen Mang pushed the golden box to the very bottom of his schoolbag and zipped it shut. The grease-stained menu he smoothed out flat. First line: "Breakfast: two eggs."

At ten, no video call came; a phone call came instead. His mum's voice was very low, and in the background someone was calling out queue numbers. "Your dad got out at a service station, his leg gave way and he went down on his knees. He couldn't get up by himself." His mum couldn't read the hospital scan report all the way through; when she reached the word "meniscus" she got stuck. "The doctor says three months' rest at least. The lorry, the lorry, the fleet's taken it back for now." She paused, then said, "That training camp of yours, do you still have to pay for it?"

"...No need to pay. The team feeds us."

The phone changed hands at the other end. It was his dad, sucking in air with the pain, and still getting a dig in: "It's nothing. Kneeling's just standing, a bit shorter." Before he'd finished there was another "Sss".

Shen Mang gripped the phone, and his mouth was faster than his head. "Dad, don't breathe in for now. Breathe out, breathe out long, longer than you breathe in." The other end went quiet, except for one very long, very shaky breath leaking bit by bit out of the phone. After a good while his dad said, "...It does ease up a bit."

When he'd hung up he opened *Acupuncture and Moxibustion* to the page on the knee and copied out "Dubi" and "Zusanli", and when he'd copied them he read on to a line of small print below: meniscus tears, acupuncture can only relieve the pain; where it is torn, it mostly won't grow back. He stared at the words "won't grow back" for a long time and shut the book. His family's monthly income was now only his mum's pay from the supermarket; the two hundred train fare he'd spent on the gym card had suddenly stopped being train fare.

By now it was only five days to the first round of the inter-department cup.
