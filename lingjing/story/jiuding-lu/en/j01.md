# Now · 1: I Threw Up on the Thousand Metres, Then Learned to Breathe Out First

On his twelfth day at university, in front of half a grandstand of girls, [Shen Mang]{注=jin-shenmang} died at the four-hundred-metre mark of the thousand metres.

"Died" is a bit much; but as far as he was concerned, it wasn't too much by a single word. His throat felt stuffed with a handful of red-hot iron filings, and his two legs belonged to somebody else who had lent them to him and was now in a hurry to have them back. He was gasping with his mouth wide open, fast and shallow; one breath would only just reach his throat before the next was already rushing out, and the two of them collided head-on in his windpipe, neither willing to give way.

A minute earlier, he'd been running second. When the whistle went he'd shot off — and it wasn't the thousand metres that scared him, it was the grandstand, where the girls' group sat waiting to run their eight hundred. For the first hundred metres the wind rushed past his ears, someone in the stands pointed his way and said something, and his heart burned: Shen Mang, oh Shen Mang, so you're a running genius after all — eighteen years and nobody noticed, not even you.

He crossed two hundred, someone at the line called "thirty-nine", and he was delighted. Then his legs went heavy first, as if someone had poured a bag of sand down each trouser leg; then came a sourness, climbing up from his calves; then came the gasping. At three hundred, Specs went past on his left, and even spared a breath on the way: "Busy Mang, take care." At four hundred, Old Cai went past on his right and patted his shoulder: "I'll go on ahead." He wanted to say back, "Safe journey," but when he opened his mouth all that came out was a puff of hot air.

This person was surnamed Shen, given name Mang, eighteen years old, a first-year in Acupuncture and Tuina at Yizhou University of Chinese Medicine; the university was in the north of Linyi, Shandong, on the west bank of the Yi River. Ask what he was good at, and it was talking. From morning till night his mouth was never idle. Right now, he couldn't get a word out.

By six hundred, everyone had gone past him. The last one to overtake him was running one step and stopping one step and still had time to wave at people in the stands. Shen Mang watched that back draw away, his face burning in waves, burning worse than his legs: he had plenty of experience of coming last, but coming last in front of every girl in the school, that was a first. For the last hundred metres, from who knows where he found a breath, set his teeth, and actually ran a little faster.

At the finish line [Coach Lei]{注=jin-lei} from the PE department stopped the watch and read it out like a parking ticket: "Shen Mang, four fifty-eight. The pass mark is four thirty-two. You haven't passed. Come back for the retest on the twenty-fourth of September."

Shen Mang's knees went and he was about to squat down, but Coach Lei grabbed him by the back of the collar. "Don't squat! Stand up and keep walking!" He'd gone only a few steps when his stomach began to heave. A hand pushed a plastic bag into his hand: "Throw up in this. The track's needed tomorrow."

It was a girl's voice, not loud, not hurried. He bent over and, with a "Hwaaargh—", returned the bowl of beef noodles he'd had at lunch to the world exactly as he'd received it, tears and snot coming down together. Things went black in front of his eyes in waves, and in the black, hazily, sat his great-grandmother on the little stool in the yard back home, beckoning to him: "Mang, come here, the dumplings are just out of the pot." His great-grandmother had died six years ago. He thought: that's it, she's come to fetch me. I'm only eighteen. I don't want dumplings yet.

When the black passed, all he could see was a pair of white trainers, a stretch of blue volunteer's vest, and a hand holding a little notebook. The voice said, above his head, "You're the first one to throw up at the fitness test this year." A pen nib went scratching across the notebook, writing something down.

That something written down was exactly what he dreaded most. Carrying a bag of his own lunch, his legs so weak all he wanted was to sit, he didn't sit after all, and walked the rest of that lap step by step. As he walked, it wasn't the thousand metres he was thinking about. It was the notebook: what had she written down about me?

---

That night, Room 617 held a struggle session.

The person under criticism lay on the bottom bunk, staring at the slats of the bunk above. On registration day he'd climbed all six floors of three different buildings before he found this room, and the room had called him "Busy Mang" ever since; he himself called it getting to know the campus. In the top bunk, [Old Cai]{注=jin-laocai} held up his phone and played a video from the year group chat for the third time — someone in the stands had filmed it, with a caption: "On How Many Layers One Man Can Bring to the Thousand Metres". In the video he first charged along in second place, then got overtaken one by one, like a log slowly washed up on the beach by the tide. [Specs]{注=jin-yanjing} said, "Stop. Last hundred metres — did he speed up a bit?" Old Cai said, "He did speed up a bit." Specs said, "The last flicker before the candle goes out." The two of them laughed till they rolled off their beds. Shen Mang wanted to laugh too, but when he tried, his emptied-out stomach gave a cramp first.

When they'd finished laughing, Specs pushed up his glasses, his phone screen lit, and his face changed. "Busy Mang, it says in the student handbook: less than fifty points on the fitness test and you only get a certificate of completion, not a degree." Shen Mang dived face first into his pillow. After a long while muffled in the pillow he turned over and looked at the wall. Two sheets of paper were stuck on it. One was the timetable, where Specs had circled the first class on Thursday morning in red pen: Introduction to Acupuncture and Tuina. The other was a wall chart of the twenty-four solar terms from the welcome pack. Specs had put it up; he said anyone studying Chinese medicine without one on the wall was a disgrace.

Old Cai poked his head out. "Your essays are so good. How did you end up studying Chinese medicine?" Shen Mang said, "Reassigned. My sixth choice. My mum filled that one in." As he said it he looked at the wall chart, not at anyone.

Specs picked up an old library book from the desk, *Baopuzi: The Inner Chapters* — the Classical Chinese for Medicine teacher had posted a list of extra reading in the group chat, and he was the only one in the whole class who'd actually gone and borrowed anything on it. He read it out word by word: "[Man is within qi, and qi is within man.]{典=baopu-zhili}" When he'd finished he looked up at him. "What you threw up in the bag was noodles. You can't throw up qi — if you really got it all out, that's called breathing your last." Shen Mang said, "Then today I was a plastic bag away from breathing my last."

He asked again, "Hey, the girl who gave me the plastic bag — which school's she in?" Old Cai said, "I saw her. While you were throwing up she was standing by keeping count. Counting how many people threw up. Four hundred and something people in the year ran it, and they were all standing round gasping afterwards, but you were the only one who threw up." Shen Mang said, "…So I'm one of a kind."

At ten his mum video-called. Some keen parent had forwarded the clip from the stands into the freshmen's parents' group. "Your dad's watched it eight times." His dad's big face squeezed into the frame with a whoosh, two nostrils taking up half the screen, very solemn: "Mang, if you can't outrun them, walk. There's no shame in walking. When I did my fitness test, I walked the whole way." His mum said, "He came second to last that time." His dad said, "The one who came last was carried off." With that he leaned back, said "Ow", and carried on as if nothing had happened: "This car seat's too hard." His mum said, "It's his knee. He's just driven back from Rizhao. Eleven hours at the wheel." The camera swung and caught his dad's leg propped on a stool, the knee brace wrapped round it like a rice dumpling. His dad said, "This is called a knee brace. It's our medal, us drivers."

In the middle of the night hunger woke him. By the light of the streetlamp he found Grain in Ear on the chart with his finger — he'd been born on that day — and moved back one square: Xiaoman, Lesser Fullness. A line of small print underneath: "The grain is filling, not yet full."

"Xiaoman," he read under his breath. "Not full yet. Sounds like someone who never gets enough to eat."

His stomach rumbled, answering for Xiaoman.

---

The next day was the Hundred Clubs Fair on the central square. He was going to the chess club — he'd checked: chess is sitting down the whole time. More than a hundred stalls stretched from the library door all the way to the canteen. The street-dance club was playing music, the medicinal-cooking club was handing out sour plum juice, and a senior from the tuina club was shouting through a megaphone, "Free trial massage, doesn't hurt or your money back!" while behind him someone having a trial massage was screaming. He squeezed his way in, and halfway along was blocked by a hand-written banner: "University Basketball Team · Recruiting".

Beside the stall stood a portable hoop, and someone was dunking. Standing, he was like a wall; jumping, he was like a wall that had taken off — one ninety-something tall, and on his feet a pair of basketball shoes Shen Mang had once looked up online, seen the price, and quietly closed the page. Every dunk, the girls watching squealed. After a dozen-odd he landed, took a big shaker bottle and drank a mouthful of milky-white protein, hardly even out of breath. Shen Mang looked at him and felt it was very unfair: we're both human; I run a thousand metres and nearly throw up my lungs, he bounces a dozen times and it's like nothing happened. That's not fair.

Behind the folding table sat a girl with a ponytail, the little notebook open in front of her.

He knew that notebook: the one who'd given him the plastic bag at the finish line yesterday, and written him down, was this girl. From seeing her face to picking up a sign-up form took three seconds in all, and a second and a half of that was looking for a pen.

She turned to a page and read: "Shen Mang. First two hundred metres in thirty-nine seconds, second in the group; dropped to tenth by four hundred; last over the line, and threw up after it." She looked up. "You ran the first two hundred on borrowed oxygen. What you borrow you have to pay back, so after you stop you're gasping for ages."

So that was what she'd written down about him yesterday. His ears began to burn.

The big guy who'd been dunking wandered over, a watch on his wrist and a big shaker bottle in his hand, and tilted his head to read the form. "Shen Mang. That's the Mang in mangxiao, right? Glauber's salt. That's a laxative. In our pharmacy, Glauber's salt goes on the bottom shelf. It's cheap." He held out a hand the size of a pot lid. "I'm [Ma Xiaobao]{注=jin-maxiaobao}, third year, Chinese Pharmacy. My family runs pharmacies, thirty-odd of them. When you're writing prescriptions one day, come and fill them at ours, I'll give you a discount. No big deal." He said it very casually, as if he were saying it was nice weather today. Shen Mang shook the hand and felt that what he'd shaken wasn't a hand but a promise: you will never, in this life, beat me.

Ma Xiaobao turned to the girl. "Ahe, you're taking this kind too? We're recruiting players, not a mascot."

Her name was [Ahe]{注=jin-ahe}. Shen Mang's head went hot and the words just came out: "He dunks a dozen times and isn't even out of breath, and I run a thousand metres and nearly run myself to death. We've both got two legs — are his the originals and mine the free gift?" Ma Xiaobao laughed out loud and pointed his chin at Ahe. "Ask her. She studies sports rehab. Even the coach listens to her." Ahe didn't laugh. She answered like someone answering a question she'd answered many times: "Your legs are originals too. It's your heart and lungs that haven't been trained. In people who've trained, there's more of the stuff in the muscle that burns oxygen, and the heart sends out more blood each beat. When he wakes up in the morning, lying down, his heart rate is in the fifties. What's yours?" Shen Mang looked down at his fitness band, which his mum had bought online before term for ninety-nine yuan: eighty-eight. Ahe said, "Measured standing up and talking doesn't count. Tomorrow morning when you wake up, lie still and measure it again." He looked down again, and it had shot up further. He tucked the band up his sleeve. The thing was too honest.

She handed Shen Mang a ball. "Shoot one for me." The ball was heavier than he'd thought, and bigger. He copied what he'd seen on TV: bent his knees, raised his hands, ran through every game he'd ever watched in his head, and let fly. The ball went very high and very confidently, over the hoop, over the backboard, and, dead on the mark, knocked over Ma Xiaobao's shaker. Half a bottle of protein ran out over his basketball shoes. Shen Mang froze where he stood, sweat dropping off his forehead bead by bead. Ma Xiaobao looked down and smiled. "No problem. It's only a pair of shoes." He wiped them very slowly. "Got more at home."

Shen Mang felt he had never in his life heard a scarier "no problem".

Next to them, a tall first-year who'd just finished signing up took one look at this, put the pen down, and turned off towards the street-dance stall. Ahe wrote in her notebook: "Liu Yiming, 1.88 m. Signed, then ran." Ma Xiaobao was delighted. "Big Liu's gone, and in comes Little Shen."

"When the basketball team takes someone on, it looks at two things: can they be trained, and will they turn up." Ahe ignored Ma Xiaobao. "Whether you can be trained, I don't know. Whether you'll turn up — when you'd finished throwing up, you didn't sit down; you walked the rest of that lap yourself. I was counting the throwers-up at the time, and I happened to see." She pointed at the bottom box. "Sign your own name here."

Shen Mang signed it, pressing harder than on his university entrance exam answer sheet. In twelve years of school, the only certificate he'd ever won was a "Most Improved" in third year of primary school, which didn't even have a ranking on it; this form had only one line on it, and it was his. He looked at it once more before he handed it over. Ahe put it on top of the stack of blank forms — and not one form in that stack had been filled in.

Ma Xiaobao slapped him on the back so hard he lurched forward. "Congratulations. Number one recruit this year. Maybe the last one too. Monday, six a.m., the gym. I'll take your warm-up myself." With that he jumped and dunked another, and the hoop wobbled three times. Ahe didn't look up. "Ignore him. Normally the university team trains together; for the inter-department cup, everyone goes back to their own school — he goes back to Chinese Pharmacy, you go back to Acupuncture and Tuina. The two of you are teammates normally, and opponents in the inter-department cup."

That night she sent him a table: "Shen Mang · Fourteen Days to the Retest". Item by item: jog twenty minutes every morning, able to speak in full sentences while running; no running on an empty stomach, one egg for breakfast, provided by the team; asleep before midnight; Monday 6 a.m. at the gym as usual, no three-thousand-metre run yet. And the last item: "On retest day, the first two hundred metres must not be faster than forty-five seconds. The first two hundred is where you died." Underneath, on a new line: "Eggs at 1.20 each, fourteen days, 16.80, put down to your account."

Shen Mang replied: "If I pass the retest, is there a prize?" Ahe replied: "That depends on your result at the retest."

At midnight, a strange number: "This is Ma Xiaobao." Then a video — him dunking in the gym, someone sitting under the basket who'd been dunked on so hard they'd landed on their backside, circled in red, with a note next to it: The last new guy. A line of text under the video: "Monday morning, you stand in this spot."

---

Monday, the fourteenth of September, the first day of classes. Five-forty in the morning, and 617 was as dark as the inside of a wok. Shen Mang lay with his eyes open for ten minutes, then pressed two fingers to his wrist and counted for a minute against the stopwatch: seventy-six. He repeated the number to himself three times, afraid he'd forget it the moment he got up.

At the gym door, Ahe was crouched on the steps with an insulated bag at her feet. She handed him a boiled egg, still hot. "No running on an empty stomach." Shen Mang peeled off the shell. "Which number is this one?" "Number four. The table counts from the day of the fitness test, fourteen days altogether. The first two days you didn't get one, but they still go on the account." "Even the ones I didn't get?" "The table is fourteen days." He stuffed the whole egg in his mouth and choked on it, eyes bulging. He hadn't even swallowed this one and his account was already at four.

At first light, the whole team ran three thousand metres on the red track, and he ran his twenty minutes alone in the outside lane. Able to speak in full sentences while running — that was the rule. What should he say? On Thursday there was a test, writing out the twelve regular meridians from memory. He'd copied them onto a sheet of paper last night and had it in his pocket, so as he ran he recited: "Hand Taiyin Lung Meridian. Hand Yangming Large Intestine Meridian. Foot Yangming Stomach Meridian. Foot… foot…" Ma Xiaobao overtook him and looked back. "Who's he talking to?" The captain, Xing Bin, said, "He's talking to himself." Ma Xiaobao said, "Training his mouth, is he? If it was a mouth contest, he'd be top of the team."

Coach Lei stood on the bend holding the stopwatch, waiting for him to shuffle past. "What are you muttering?" "I'm learning the twelve regular meridians." Coach Lei was taken aback. In twenty years of coaching he'd seen people cry while running, throw up, sing; running while reciting scripture was a first. He thought about it, didn't shout, and pointed at his mouth: "If you can get whole sentences out, you're at the right speed. Keep reciting."

After the run, back in the gym, they split into groups for half-court. Ma Xiaobao weighed the ball twice in his palm and pushed it into Shen Mang's arms. "Here, you've got the ball, I'll guard you." Shen Mang dribbled twice; on the third bounce the ball went into Ma Xiaobao's hands. Ma Xiaobao spun, two steps, took off — and only one thought was left in Shen Mang's head: that red circle.

He didn't dodge. Not because he was brave. He just didn't have time to.

Ma Xiaobao came down on him from the sky, the ball slammed through the hoop, the hoop hummed and swayed, and Shen Mang landed on his backside on the floor, in exactly the spot in the video, not an inch out. The whole place burst out laughing. Ma Xiaobao landed and pulled him up with one big hand. "Nice positioning." Then, lowering his voice, with a glance at the sideline: "You came here for her, didn't you?"

Shen Mang said, "I was practising boxing out."

"Boxing out?" Ma Xiaobao looked at the sideline too, where Ahe was writing with her head down. "Boxing her out?" He patted his shoulder and walked off.

During the break, a new video appeared in the team chat — that dunk just now; someone had already posted it. The red circle was round a new person, and underneath Ma Xiaobao had replied: "This one's the new guy." Shen Mang turned his phone face down on his thigh and felt the whole gym was looking at him: looking at him sitting on the floor, and at that glance he'd made at the sideline as well. Just as he was looking for a crack in the floor to crawl into, the voice from the sideline called, not loud, not hurried: "Shen Mang, come over here and get weighed."

In the equipment room there was a set of scales and a thing that looked like pincers. The numbers on the scales had just settled when a big foot came over and pressed lightly on the edge of the scales with its toe, and the numbers shot up. Ma Xiaobao was leaning on the doorframe. "I'll give you an extra kilo, make you look respectable." Ahe didn't look up. "Take your foot off." The numbers dropped back: fifty-nine point six. Ahe wrote it down and glanced at Ma Xiaobao. "The scales are accurate. That kilo of yours goes on your account." Ma Xiaobao laughed and wandered off, swinging his shaker.

"These are skinfold calipers. You pinch to see how thick the layer under the skin is, and work out body fat." She pinched the back of his arm, below his shoulder blade, the side of his belly. Shen Mang was ticklish and flinched at every pinch. At the third flinch Ahe said, "Flinch again and I'll count the flinch." So he stopped flinching. She tapped at a calculator for a while. "Twenty-one point four per cent. For your weight, that's a fair bit. You're thin, but there's not much muscle on you, and there is a bit of fat."

Shen Mang looked down at himself. For eighteen years he'd thought he was thin; it turned out he was a fat person among thin people — like someone who'd always thought he was poor, checked, and found he was in debt as well.

Last came free throws. He shot ten and sank one. Ahe shut her notebook. "The test on Thursday, have you learned it? Last year at this test there were eleven blank papers." She turned up a notice. "October the seventeenth, team trials, three parts: fitness, strength, skills. Pass, and you're on the university team. Fail, and this line gets crossed out. With the numbers you've got today, you wouldn't pass any of the three."

"What's the point of weighing it?" he asked. "When you've weighed it, I'm still me."

"If you don't weigh it, you'll train for a month and not know if you've gone up or down," Ahe said. "If the number on the scales doesn't look good, that doesn't matter. What matters is that next time it's different from this time."

---

On the first day of classes, on the second floor of Canteen One, Ma Xiaobao's tray landed with a clang opposite him: two helpings of red-braised pork, a chicken leg, four eggs. He ate an egg in one mouthful. "You're eating shredded potato? Shredded potato doesn't build muscle." When he'd finished he pushed the tray away and rubbed his stomach. "Still hungry." As he got up, a little white bottle rolled out of his trouser pocket, covered in foreign writing. He bent to pick it up, unscrewed it, tipped two capsules into his palm and swallowed them dry. "Fish oil. Deep-sea fish. My mum insists." Then he tapped the rim of Shen Mang's tray with his chopsticks. "What you're on the team for — I can see it. She can't — everything she looks at is numbers. October the seventeenth, the trials. If you don't pass," he smiled very pleasantly, "I'll tell her for you. I'll put it nicely. I'll say there was this guy who ran the thousand metres till he threw up, for her. Don't worry. My lips are sealed."

Shen Mang sat in front of that plate of shredded potato a long time. There wasn't a single frightening word in any of it, and yet he'd broken into more of a sweat than after twenty minutes of running that morning.

That afternoon was the introductory anatomy lecture. The teacher drew a person on the blackboard and labelled it layer by layer from the outside in: skin, superficial fascia, deep fascia, muscle, blood vessels and nerves, bone. Shen Mang put up his hand. "Teacher, which layer are the meridians in?" Someone in the back row snorted with laughter. The soft-spoken teacher didn't laugh. She thought about it. "To this day, anatomy hasn't found any separate structure like the meridians." She put down the pelvis model in her hand and went on. "Two thousand-odd years ago there were no scalpels. All the ancients had were their hands. Press here, and an aching numbness travels over there; put a needle in here, and the pain over there eases; take the pulse, and it beats, beat after beat, joined up everywhere. They wrote it down one point at a time and joined the points into lines — on the silk manuscripts dug up at Mawangdui in Changsha, there are still only eleven; only later did they make it up to twelve, one for each month of the year." She paused. "Cut along the lines and look, and you find blood vessels, nerves and fascia, each going its own way." She drew the outline of a province on the blackboard in chalk. "The meridians are like this provincial border on a map. You can't dig this line out of the ground, but cross it, and the accent changes."

Shen Mang sat down, his head humming. The twelve things he had to write out on Thursday turned out to be twelve things nobody had ever seen.

For the next three nights, after lights out in 617, the bottom bunk recited scripture non-stop. By the fifth meridian, the first four started leaking out, like a sack with a hole in the bottom. Specs passed him a mnemonic handed down by the seniors: "Lung, Large, Stomach, Spleen, Heart, Small; Bladder, Kidney, Pericardium, Burner, Gall, Liver." Shen Mang recited it ten times. He remembered the mnemonic, but the moment he came to write the meridians out, all that was left in his head was two words: pie burner. Old Cai couldn't stand listening any more from the top bunk. "You can't learn it like that. The twelve regular meridians are twelve rivers, and the eight extraordinary channels are reservoirs. When the rivers are full they flow into the reservoirs, and when the reservoirs are full, you fly." "Why do you always end up at flying?" "If you don't fly, what are you cultivating it for?"

On Tuesday evening he went to the library looking for a book with pictures. In the open stacks there really was an *Atlas of Meridians and Acupoints*: a little bald man covered all over in red and green lines, like a bus route map. The routes were all drawn in, but which route left first and which left after, the book didn't say. On the way down he took the wrong stairs and went right down to the basement. At the end of the corridor was a grey fire door with a notice on it: "Rare Books Department (Special Collections Reading Room). Entry by pass only. Copying permitted, no loans." A cool draught seeped from under the door. In the cultivation novels he'd read since he was a kid there was always the same old saying: real techniques are hidden in old books nobody reads. He reached out and pushed. The door lock went *beep* and lit up red, and the door didn't open. That door, he remembered.

Couldn't find it; still had to write it out. Thursday morning, Introduction to Acupuncture and Tuina. [Teacher Yan]{注=jin-yan} wrote a line on the blackboard, the chalk going tock-tock:

"[The meridians are that by which one can decide life and death, treat the hundred illnesses, and regulate depletion and repletion; they must not fail to flow.]{典=lingshu-jingmai}"

"Books away. Write out the twelve regular meridians." He walked up and down the rows with his hands behind his back. "Last year somebody copied them on their arm."

On the inside of Shen Mang's left arm, twelve lines were copied in fine pen. He put his hand under the desk and rubbed hard with spit, and when he'd finished and looked, the twelve lines had smeared into one blur of blue. He picked up his pen, wrote "Hand Taiyin Lung Meridian", and then discovered it was the only one he remembered. The last words of the line on the blackboard were right in front of his seat: must not fail to flow.

Teacher Yan turned over one paper, stopped, and held it up. "Shen Mang. Handed in a blank paper." Someone in the back row laughed. "Anyone who can't learn all twelve might think about whether they've come in the wrong door, sooner rather than later."

When the papers came back, his had only two words, in the top right corner: Shen Mang. He folded it twice and pushed it deep into the bottom of his pocket, as if afraid someone would see it. A voice in his head said: Shen Mang, oh Shen Mang — a thing nobody can find, and you can't even remember it.

Five in the afternoon, the gym. Training had just finished. Ma Xiaobao wandered over shaking his big shaker and called out towards the sideline: "Manager, I hear our new guy handed in a blank paper today?" Ahe was writing with her head down and didn't look up. Ma Xiaobao patted Shen Mang on the shoulder. "Go on, recite one for the manager. Just the second one."

Half the gym stopped what they were doing. Shen Mang opened his mouth: "Hand Taiyin Lung Meridian… hand…" Nothing came after it. The line of blue writing was smeared up his sleeve, the mnemonic was in his head, but which came after "Lung, Large", he couldn't connect, however he tried. He stood there with his mouth open, like that moment on Monday when he'd sat down in the red circle, sitting in it all over again. His face burned from the roots of his ears down to his neck, and he could hear his heart thudding, beat by beat, against his throat. Ma Xiaobao didn't laugh either. He bent down to his ear and said, his voice very low: "Can't even learn twelve — you think you'll get through October the seventeenth? What I said still stands." Then he straightened up and wandered off, swinging his shaker.

Only then did Ahe look up. She glanced at him and wrote something in her notebook. What she wrote, he didn't dare look.

That night he didn't learn anything by heart. He laid the book open on the desk at the picture of the little man, stood in the middle of the room, and raised both arms over his head — that was how the little man in the picture was holding them.

He started from his chest: one inch under the collarbone, the Hand Taiyin Lung Meridian comes out here and goes up the inside of the arm, all the way to the tip of the thumb. The thumb is next to the index finger: the Hand Yangming Large Intestine Meridian takes over, comes down the outside of the arm, over the shoulder, and lands beside the nose. Beside the nose, the Foot Yangming Stomach Meridian takes over, comes down over the face, past the chest, past the belly, past the front of the knee, and lands at the second toe. The second toe is next to the big toe: the Foot Taiyin Spleen Meridian takes over and goes back up the inside of the leg and comes back to the chest.

His hand stopped on his chest, and he froze: it had gone all the way round and come back to where it started.

He went on and traced the second circuit: out from the chest, to the little finger; from the little finger over to the back of the hand, down to the front of the ear; starting inside the corner of the eye, over the top of the head, all the way down the back, to the little toe; up from the sole of the foot, back to the chest again — this whole circuit ran down the back. Third circuit: chest to middle finger; down from the ring finger, to the tip of the eyebrow; starting outside the corner of the eye, down the sides of the body, to the fourth toe; up again from the big toe, back to the chest, and joined on to the Lung Meridian again.

He stood there, both arms still raised, and suddenly understood it all. With your arms up, the yin meridians all run upward and the yang meridians all run downward. At the fingertips and toe tips, yin and yang hand over; on the face, yang hands over to yang; in the chest, yin hands over to yin. These weren't twelve lines each minding its own business — it was one line, running round a person three times: the first circuit down the front, the second down the back, the third down the sides. Like a relay team, running a lap and coming back to put the baton in the next runner's hand.

Specs, on the bed opposite, pushed his glasses up. "…Do that again. I'm recording it." Old Cai raised his arms too and traced along in the top bunk; at the fourth meridian his arm hit the ceiling. "Ow. On this top bunk, the yang meridians can't get down."

He shut his eyes and traced it all again, without one mistake. Next morning he went to resit the test, and passed. Teacher Yan wrote "Pass" on the paper, glanced at him, and said nothing else. He took the blank paper from the seventeenth of September out of his pocket, smoothed it flat, and stuck it up next to the solar-terms chart, blank side out. Specs said, "You've stuck it on backwards." Shen Mang said, "No I haven't. The blank side is for me to look at."

Monday, the twenty-first of September, two in the afternoon, fifth floor of the lab building. Shen Mang went into the dissection lab for the first time.

When the door was pushed open, the first thing to come out was the smell of formalin, so strong your eyes ran before you were even through the door. Forty first-years in white coats crowded at the doorway, and nobody wanted to go in first. In the room were six tables, each covered with a white sheet, and under each white sheet was the shape of a person. He stood in the back row, his hands clenched into fists in the pockets of his white coat. Three days to the retest; ten steps to the first dead person he'd ever seen.

By the leg of the table was a laminated card. The soft-spoken teacher bent down and read it: "Liu Guizhi, female, seventy-nine. In life she was a primary-school teacher. She left a message: tell the children not to be afraid, and to study well." Forty people bowed. Shen Mang bowed deepest, so deep he could see the tips of his own shoes. His mind was in a jumble, and in the end the only thing he thought was: Teacher Liu, I'm a bit of a coward. Please bear with me.

The teacher called her the "Silent Teacher": "This is your first teacher. She'll teach you for a whole term without saying a word." The rules, too, she laid down on the spot: no photos, no joking; lift the sheet only off the part you're looking at today, and keep the rest covered; anything you cut off, even a sliver the size of a fingernail, goes back where it came from — at the end of term the Silent Teacher would be sent whole for cremation, and every year at Qingming the university held a memorial service for them. Two layers of mask couldn't keep out the smell; after the first class, several people didn't eat meat for a week.

This class was the chest. A corner of the sheet was lifted. The chest had been opened beforehand, the two lungs drawn aside, collapsed, darker in colour than he'd expected. Shen Mang had been sure he'd throw up — he'd even brought a plastic bag, exactly like the one Ahe had pushed into his hand that time; he'd gone to the supermarket specially to buy it — but he didn't throw up. He just looked, and as he looked, he forgot to be afraid.

The teacher pointed with her tweezers at a big sheet of muscle stretched across beneath the lungs, thin, arching upward like a tent pitched upside down. "The diaphragm. This sheet between chest and belly — that's it. When you breathe in, it's mainly this doing the work: it contracts, the tent pulls down flat, space opens up at the bottom of the chest, and the lungs get pulled open and fill up. When it presses down, the contents of the belly have nowhere to go, so the belly pushes out. When it relaxes, it springs back, and the air comes out. Every breath you're taking right now, it's this moving. Twenty thousand-odd times a day."

She had them come up one at a time and, wearing gloves, press it gently with a finger. When Shen Mang's turn came his hand shook once, and he pressed — it didn't feel like flesh. It felt like a tight drumskin, thin and tough. He drew his hand back, looked down at his own stomach, and remembered the thousand metres: his shoulders heaving up and down, the cords standing out in his neck, his chest rising fast and shallow — and his stomach, from start to finish, never moving at all.

"Teacher," he put up his hand, "what if someone breathes with just their chest, and their stomach doesn't move?"

The teacher looked at him and recognised him: the one who'd asked last week which layer the meridians were in. "Then this big muscle is slacking off," she said, "and letting the little muscles in the neck and shoulders do its job for it. Little muscles can't do the heavy work. You can't get a deep breath that way."

The bell rang for the end of class. Shen Mang was the last out of the lab, and at the door he turned and bowed once more towards that table.

That evening he went to the sports ground on his own to breathe with his stomach. The first lap he thought hard about that tent: in, press down; out, spring back. Thinking about it, his steps went wrong, left foot and right foot unable to agree which went first; thinking some more, he found he didn't even know how to breathe any more — eighteen years of breathing without ever thinking about it, and the moment he thought about it, he forgot how. Ahe came by along the edge of the track and stopped. "What are you doing?"

"I'm training my diaphragm."

"You've got it backwards. When you breathe in, your stomach's going in." He looked down. So it was: his chest was puffed out, like a startled pigeon.

"When people panic, that's how they breathe, shallow and fast," Ahe said. "There's always a bit of every breath stuck in your throat and windpipe that never reaches your lungs, and it goes straight back out with the next breath — like squeezing onto a bus: the bunch of people by the door never get into the bus. The shallower and faster you breathe, the more of it is wasted. That day at four hundred metres, half of your gasping was wasted."

"So what should I do?"

"Let the people by the door get off first." She shut her notebook and walked off. A few steps on she looked back. "The retest is Thursday at four. Only three days left. If you can't fix your breathing, don't force it. Forcing it is worse than not changing it."

Shen Mang stood on the track, hands on his hips. If he didn't change, it would be just like the eleventh of September. If he did, three days wouldn't be enough to get it right. He knew now that it was the diaphragm slacking off, but how to make it stop slacking, nobody had taught him. How the people by the door got off first, she hadn't said either.

---

On the afternoon of the twenty-second of September he stood for twenty minutes in front of the grey fire door in the library basement. The door lock showed red. He was just about to leave when the door opened from inside and out came an [old lady]{注=jin-ge}, seventy or so, blue cloth oversleeves, reading glasses hanging on a chain, a pencil in her hand. "Hanging around the door for twenty minutes. I was watching you on the camera. I thought you were a book thief. Book thieves aren't as well-behaved as you."

"Granny, are there any books that… ordinary people can't understand, and if you do understand them you get really powerful?"

"Of course," said the old lady. "Dictionaries."

Shen Mang went red, and said honestly, "…I'm looking for a book about breathing."

She narrowed her eyes at him for a while, then turned and went back in. "Come in, then. Put your bag in the locker outside. Pencils only. Books are for copying, not borrowing. Keep your voice down; books don't like noise." The Rare Books Department was kept at a constant temperature and humidity, twenty degrees or so all year round, and stepping in out of the blazing September sun made the hairs on your arms stand up. Her surname was Ge; she said Ge Hong was one of her lot, the old Ge family — five hundred years ago they'd been one household. First she sent him to the sink to wash his hands twice — "There's oil on your hands, and paper bears grudges. Thirty years from now you'll still be able to see your fingerprints" — and as she looked for the book she talked: the air conditioning in this room was older than he was; her cat was called Rare Book, because it too was look-but-don't-touch; when she was young she'd mounted books in the conservation room and once spent three months patching a single sheet of Song-dynasty paper… Shen Mang listened, and felt that not one thing she'd said had got anywhere near breathing.

She brought out a case of thread-bound volumes. "Tao Hongjing, *On Nourishing Life*. Tang dynasty. The Prime Minister in the Mountains — whenever the emperor had a problem, he'd go into the mountains to ask him." Vertical lines, traditional characters, a whole page all about how to let breath out: chui, hu, xi, he, xu, si — six characters, every one of which he knew, and put together not a sentence he understood. He picked his way to one line and stopped:

"[When a person is spent to the limit, they breathe out much with xu and si.]{典=yangxing-liuzi}"

Tired to the very end, a person breathes out more *xu*, more *si*. Crouching by the track, breathing out one mouthful after another — that was exactly what he'd been doing. So, fifteen hundred years ago, somebody had seen him.

Tucked inside the case was a yellowed borrowing card. A few lines in fountain pen from the nineteen-seventies; in the nineteen-eighties, one name entered in a dozen lines one after another, the writing very small and very hurried, later blotted by water so it couldn't be read; below that, not a single line. "Nobody's opened it in thirty-odd years," Granny Ge said behind him. "You're the first. Put your name down." In the first empty line he wrote "Shen Mang, 2026.9.22", looked at that name above it, and something stirred in him.

Granny Ge shut the case and said, as if in passing, "What this set's about is breathing out first. Get it all out, and the new breath comes in by itself."

The people by the door get off first — what Ahe had said yesterday was just this: breathe out first. He stood there and didn't move for a long while.

When he went to fetch his bag, he crouched in front of a low bookcase by the door. On the bottom shelf lay a case of old books, the case rubbed pale, its label fallen off. He undid it: inside were eight volumes, each two fingers thick, with nothing on the spines but the numbers one to eight written in ink with a brush. He took out the first volume. On the flyleaf, in brush: *The Notebooks of the Nine Cauldron Adept*. He opened it. The first line:

"When I was young, people called me Leaky Ladle."

Shen Mang snorted with laughter. An Adept who started off by calling himself a leaky ladle — this old man was all right. He leafed on. It wasn't a technique. It was a diary, entry after entry, as short as lines in an account book. He came to one entry: "That winter, learned breathing out and in. The goose feather flew every day." His heart beat faster — a goose feather, he knew about that; there was one in Specs's *Baopuzi*. A few pages on, a single line on its own: "To cultivate immortality, first cultivate the heart."

He read that line three times. In the novels he'd read since he was a kid, the first page of the real technique always said exactly that. This old man was the real thing. He slid the first volume back into the case, exactly as it had been, but in his heart he made a note: he was coming back here.

---

On the way back to the dorm, clutching the paper he'd copied onto, he stopped at the foot of Building Three and looked up at the sixth floor. On registration day he'd climbed eighteen floors in three buildings, gasping at every one. He started climbing, and as he climbed, silently, he breathed out: two steps breathe out, two steps breathe in, and breathe out all the way. At the sixth floor he stood at the door of 617 with a hand on the frame — not because he was so out of breath he needed it, but because he couldn't believe it. He was breathing hard, yes, but the breath was deep, one after another, not squeezing up into his throat.

He pushed the door open. "I just climbed six floors and didn't get out of breath." Old Cai didn't look up. "When have you ever got out of breath climbing six floors?" "…Registration day." "That was eighteen."

That night after lights out he lay on his back with a thousand-page *Normal Human Anatomy* on his stomach. "Haaa—", a long breath all the way out, and the book sank; in through the nose, and the book slowly rose. On the seventh breath out, his stomach sank in, and from down below came a "pfff—" to go with it, a very small sound. Shen Mang thought: nobody will have noticed. Old Cai couldn't lie still in the top bunk; he shot half his body over the edge, holding his nose: "Busy Mang, you sprung a leak?" On the bed opposite, Specs laughed "hahahaha" till he was slapping the bed slats, and when he'd laughed enough, said: "I looked it up. Tao Hongjing was Liang dynasty, the Southern Dynasties, not Tang. And Ge Hong isn't five hundred years ago, it's about seventeen hundred." Shen Mang said, "She got the dynasties wrong. But that line about breathing out first, she didn't get that wrong."

Old Cai suddenly swung down from the top bunk and stood barefoot on the floor, his eyes shining. "You're doing breathing out and in! Teach me!" Shen Mang said, "This won't blow the top off a mountain. If it means I can run the thousand metres the day after tomorrow without throwing up, that's enough." Old Cai thought about it, decided that was a kind of magic power too, went back to his bunk, found the thickest book he had and put it on his stomach. So all that night, in the dark of 617, one after another the sounds rose and fell: "Haaa—" "Hsss—". Next door in 618 they knocked on the wall three times, and the third time someone shouted through: "Who in your room is blowing up balloons?"

---

The twenty-third of September, the autumn equinox. In the afternoon he went to the sports ground for a trial six hundred, at tomorrow's pace. The first two hundred metres went fine: his stomach swelled, sank back, and the pattern the anatomy book had pressed into him had actually made it down into his legs. He was so pleased that he forgot to count his steps, sucked in one breath too hard, all the way to the bottom — and at three hundred metres, under his right ribs, something went *ping*, as if someone had stuck a needle into him from the inside.

He had a stitch. Holding his side, bent double, he shuffled step by step to the edge of the grass and sat down, hurting too much to breathe, and the more he didn't dare breathe, the more it hurt.

Ma Xiaobao was on the track doing shuttle sprints, back and forth, back and forth, like a little lorry. He charged up to this end, saw Shen Mang curled up on the grass, slowed down, and crouched. "Stitch? Seven people at the retest tomorrow. I bet you come seventh — dead last. I'll bet a box of protein powder: if you're not seventh, the box is yours." He patted Shen Mang's face. "If you are seventh, don't bother coming on October the seventeenth either. Saves me saying that thing to her for you, in front of her." With that he turned and charged back onto the track.

Shen Mang sat on the grass, and his heart sank, cold. He couldn't even manage six hundred, and tomorrow was a thousand; there'd be people in the stands holding up phones, Ahe's notebook at the side of the track, Coach Lei's stopwatch at the finish. Shen Mang, oh Shen Mang: the ancients teach you how to breathe, and you breathe yourself a stitch.

He remembered what Granny Ge had said: breathe out first. He stopped breathing in, opened his mouth and breathed out — not hard, just slowly, long, "Hsss—", out until he couldn't breathe out any more, then a pause, and let the breath come in by itself; then out again. One breath, two, three. Under the hand pressed to his side, the needle withdrew, a little at a time.

The pain was gone. But he sat there until it was dark, and didn't dare stand up and run another step. That night, lying on the bottom bunk, his palms kept breaking out in sweat: if he threw up again tomorrow, the year group chat's video would get a sequel.

---

The twenty-fourth of September, four in the afternoon. Seven people at the retest. In the third row of the stands, at Ma Xiaobao's feet, there really was a box of protein powder, and on the box, in marker pen, one big word: "SEVENTH". He raised his shaker at Shen Mang.

At the foot of the two-hundred-metre marker was Ahe in her blue vest, the notebook open on her knee. Shen Mang stood on the start line, his palms full of sweat, his legs a bit weak, as if yesterday's needle under his ribs were still in there waiting for him. He took his eyes off the stands and looked only at the track.

The whistle went, and the other six charged off. That surge came up in him again: his heart quickened first, his legs went warm first, and he felt he could do anything. He set his teeth and forced his legs to slow down, and he dropped to the back on his own. Someone in the stands laughed out loud, and someone yelled, "Run, Forrest!" He felt that phone camera pointed right at his back, felt that box marked "SEVENTH" laughing behind him. He shoved both to the back of his mind and listened only to his own feet: two steps out, two steps in, with the stomach, not the shoulders.

When he reached the two-hundred-metre marker, Ahe called out: "Forty-six seconds!" One second slower than the table said. He relaxed inside: one second slow beat ten seconds fast.

By four hundred, two of the runners ahead had dropped back, with that fast, shallow gasping in their throats, the same as him last time. He went past them. By six hundred he was suffering too: the sourness in his legs climbing up from his calves, his chest going tight, and the less he could get in, the more he panicked, and the more he panicked, the more he wanted to breathe in fast, fast—

Breathe out first.

"Haaa—" Long, out till his stomach was hollow. Once it was all out, a new breath poured in by itself, poured right down to the bottom of his stomach. Out again, and in it came again. The stone on his chest loosened a little.

In the last three hundred metres he passed another runner, and another. In the last hundred he dug out every bit of strength he had left, legs flailing, still breathing out, breathing out like a tractor going uphill.

"Shen Mang, four twenty-nine." Coach Lei looked at the watch again. "…You've passed."

Shen Mang didn't squat. He walked on by himself, half a lap round the bend. He waited for the heaving in his stomach, and this time it didn't come. He opened his mouth, and all that came out was a long breath of hot air.

"I did it!"

He shouted so loud the person in the stands holding up a phone jumped and nearly dropped it.

Ahe turned her notebook round and showed him just one line: "9/24 Retest 4′29″ 3rd of 7 Prize: account cleared". Fourteen days of egg money, crossed out in one stroke. "First two hundred, one second slower than the table. Good slow." She paused. "In the second half you were breathing out the whole time. Who taught you that?"

He wanted to say someone from the Tang dynasty, then remembered it wasn't the Tang. "…An old lady who gets her dynasties wrong."

She glanced at him, didn't ask any more, and wrote something else in her notebook.

In the stands, Ma Xiaobao pushed the box of protein powder over to the railing with his toe and shouted towards the track: "Third out of seven. Lucky you." Ahe didn't look up. She added something to her notebook and said, "Over the last four hundred, he was first of the seven."

Shen Mang stood on the track, his chest rising and falling, burning hot. He didn't look at the box of protein powder, and he didn't look at the stands. He looked at Ahe, head down at the foot of the two-hundred-metre marker, looked for a full two seconds, and then bent down to tie his shoelace. In fact his shoelace wasn't undone at all.

At ten his mum video-called. "How did the retest go?" "Passed. Four twenty-nine." His mum didn't say anything. First she scrolled through the parents' group for a long while, then said, "No video in the group this time, so you really did pass." After a bit: "Coming home for National Day? Have you bought your train ticket?"

Shen Mang opened his mouth. "…Not yet."

---

In the night his legs rose up in revolt. Not pain — ache, from the soles of his feet right up to the tops of his thighs. Old Cai, in the top bunk, heard him tossing and turning, and dragged a foot bath out from under his bed — his grandma had sent it; it heated up, and had a little screen on the rim — and passed it down. "Borrow it. My grandma says when your legs ache, a soak in hot water beats anything." Specs took a packet of something out of his drawer and tipped it into the bath. "Sichuan pepper. My mum packed it. Don't know if it works, but it smells nice." Shen Mang put both feet in, went "Ssss—" and pulled them straight back out. Specs glanced at the screen on the rim. "Fifty-eight degrees. Old Cai, your grandma's scalding pig's trotters." He switched off the heating, picked up the bottle of cooled boiled water from the desk and poured half of it in. Shen Mang put his feet in again. This time it was hot, not scalding; wrapped in the hot water, the ache melted away bit by bit. He sat on the edge of the bed looking at the bath of water with a few peppercorns floating in it, and there was a place in his heart that was warm, and it wasn't the water that had made it warm.

That night he couldn't sleep. He reached over for the *Baopuzi* on Specs's desk. The bookmark was at the page about breathing: hold a wild goose's down feather over the nose and mouth, breathe out, and the feather must not move.

He pinched a fine wisp of down out of the zip of Old Cai's pillow, held it under his nose, held still, and slowly, slowly, breathed out—

The wisp flew off anyway.

Breathing out first, he'd learned; breathing out that softly and that long, he still had a long way to go — the old man in the notebooks had had his goose feather fly off all winter too. The wisp of down turned once in the light of the streetlamp, floated past the solar-terms chart on the wall, past the test paper stuck up blank side out, and out through the gap of the window that hadn't been shut properly — the window faced north-west — and into the September night, and was gone.
