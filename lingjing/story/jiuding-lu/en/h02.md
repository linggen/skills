# Chapter Two: One Deer Hide Settles an Old Debt / Half a Word of Thanks, Owed for a New Kindness

It was Shen Xiaoman who found the way out of the valley.

He looked for it in a great hurry. His father's left knee had to test the ground with its toes even on the flat; if his father climbed down this cliff on Black Pine Ridge, he'd never need to climb up anything again.

Yinyue wouldn't admit this. This queen had known where the way out was all along; her legs were just a little weak, so she was letting the mortal scout ahead. Xiaoman remembered what his father had said: first read the wind. He plucked a hair from his head — he'd learned his lesson this time, and kept well away from Yinyue — and held it up in the air. The hair floated west, and very decidedly. In a valley walled with cliffs on every side, if the wind blew one way only, then there was a gap on the east.

He ran east with the wind, and in the end the two cliff walls narrowed to a crack in the rock just wide enough for one person, with a thick curtain of vines hanging across its mouth, vines thicker than his arm. But the wind was squeezing in through the gaps in them, bringing a smell of pine needles with it. He turned round. "The way out's here." Yinyue was already standing behind him. She lifted her chin lazily, pointed both nostrils at him, and said, "This queen was just about to say so."

His woodcutter's knife had been lost yesterday when he rolled down the cliff. He was desperate enough to try his teeth on the vines when Yinyue stepped past him, faced the wall of vines, and said two words, quite calmly: "Move aside."

The wall of vines gave a rustling shiver. The raindrops hanging on the leaves all brightened at once, as if someone had blown a breath through the vines from the other side. Then, like a pack of dogs called off by their master, they drew back one by one, heads ducked, tails tucked. In the blink of an eye the vine wall had opened a path just wide enough for one person.

Xiaoman's mouth fell open. He turned to look at her. At that moment he saw her sway, put a hand over her mouth and cough. It was the faintest cough, but a few drops of something leaked between her fingers and fell on the grass, glittering. Silver blood.

Xiaoman sprang forward to hold her up, but before his hand reached her she was gone. His shoulder suddenly felt heavier: a little silver fox the size of his palm was crouched there, its tail curled round his neck, cool and soft. Its mouth didn't move, but its voice dropped straight into his ear: "What are you staring at? Never seen a demon queen cough blood?" A pause, and the voice sank lower, as if she were sulking at herself: "This queen used to stand above the ninth rank. This queen has now fallen to the Golden Core." Then: "When this queen fell, this queen swallowed a Nine-Turn Soul-Return Pill — that one pill is all that's holding this queen's breath in." Xiaoman said, "And now?" "Every time I take human form, I wear thinner. Every time I wake, I wear thinner too. Worn through, I can only sleep and sleep." "Sleep for how long?" "How should this queen know? …Walk."

Xiaoman held his neck stiff and didn't dare move it an inch. "Can other people see you?" "This queen lets only you see. To anyone else, you're a silly boy with a scruffy old grey dog pelt round his neck." Xiaoman thought about it. "…Then I'd better thank you first." "For what?" "This is the most expensive thing I've ever worn."

It didn't say anything, but the ball of fur lying on his shoulder kept trembling, and whether it was laughing or cold or hurting, there was no telling. Xiaoman didn't ask.

---

Out of the crack in the rock, Xiaoman worked his way back along the foot of the cliff and found a slanting fissure that split the cliff from the bottom to halfway up, with clumps of wild thorn growing in it. Above that halfway point was the slope where his father's knee had walked off the job yesterday.

He climbed. His left wrist was swollen and had no strength in it, so he could only grab a thorn stem with his right hand, push himself up half a foot, let go, and reach for the next one up. Every time he let go, for that one blink, the ball of fur on his shoulder tightened its claws a little.

Halfway up there was a rock jutting out of the fissure, just big enough for half a backside. Xiaoman sat on it to catch his breath and cleared his throat at the ball of fur on his shoulder. She'd opened the vine wall for him and coughed up blood — some things ought to be said at a moment like this. Very formally, he said, "Yinyue." "Mm. Go on." "I wanted to tell you—" "Your father is coming down." Xiaoman stopped short.

"One belt, two leg wrappings, tied together, not even twenty feet," its voice was very low. "He's tied it to a pine root on the slope, and he's already hanging halfway down. …Answer him first."

Xiaoman jerked his head up. Above him a sprinkle of dirt came pattering down. Twenty feet up, a man was hanging against the cliff, both hands clutching a cloth rope tied in several knots. One foot was kicking at the rock; the other — the left — hung straight down, with no strength in it at all. The man was still shouting, his voice so hoarse it hardly sounded human, over and over, and what he was shouting was Xiaoman's name.

He filled his lungs and yelled at the top of his voice: "Pa—!" The shouting above stopped dead.

Just then there was an uproar at the top of the slope. First a big rough voice shouted, "—Here! Someone on the rope! Someone below too!" It was Ahe's father, [Old Zhou]{注=laozhou}, a big-handed, big-footed farmer with a dark red face, who could be heard over half the hillside when he opened his mouth. The cloth rope snapped taut and was pulled up two feet — then at the top of the slope there was a *crack*: the pine root had half broken, and his father dropped, a foot, two feet. He didn't make a sound. Several hands lunged down at once and clamped hold of the end of the rope. His father pressed himself to the cliff face — but instead of going up, he reached one hand down.

Xiaoman climbed with hands and feet, all in one breath, his left wrist hurting so much that things went black in front of his eyes, again and again; he paid it no mind. That hand, its five fingers spread, mud packed under the nails, was reaching down, reaching so far it hardly looked like a human hand. Xiaoman knew that hand. Yesterday, on this slope, it had brushed his wrist and not caught it. He reached up his right hand and took hold of it. This time it held.

The men above gave a shout all together, and father and son were hauled up an inch at a time. Before Xiaoman had his feet under him he was pulled into someone's arms and held so tight he couldn't breathe. His father was shaking all over, shaking worse than the fox on his shoulder. Old Zhou lay flat at the edge of the slope, both hands still clamped on the waist of his father's trousers — the belt had long since been taken off to make the rope — at the very place Xiaoman had grabbed the day before.

His father just held his son like that, his mouth open, and couldn't get a single word out. Xiaoman said, "Pa, I'm all right. Your knee—" His father said only, "Shut up." It was the first time in his life Xiaoman had heard his father be fierce with him. Squeezed in that hug, the ball of fur on his shoulder didn't make a sound, but the tail wrapped round his neck loosened, little by little.

---

Old Zhou and the others had brought two hemp ropes and a door — the door was for carrying someone. They'd come in before dawn, following the forty-one marks Xiaoman had cut. Seeing Xiaoman standing there in one piece, Old Zhou looked at the door, then at him, and seemed a little sorry for it. "Well. Lugged that up here for nothing."

Xiaoman looked at the door and thought: Old Zhou, a whole night without sleep, two hemp ropes, one door. This debt wasn't an egg; you couldn't pay it back two for one. He didn't yet know what he'd pay it back with, so for now he wrote it down in his head.

The party was just about to head down the mountain when Yinyue suddenly spoke in Xiaoman's ear. "That deer." Her voice was smaller than before, as thin as a thread, as if one breath had to be split into three to get the words out: "Mouth of the valley… by the stream… drinking. Three hundred paces, north-east, downwind." Xiaoman stopped mid-step. "How do you know?" he whispered. "This queen is a demon queen." "You just said every time you wake you wear thinner—" She didn't answer that. She only said, "…This queen owes you one flatbread. Here's some of the interest first."

---

Xiaoman's heart leapt. The hoof print his father had touched by the mountain stream yesterday noon had been heading north-east. The first day of winter was tomorrow.

He tugged his father's sleeve and pointed north-east. "Pa, there's a deer."

Old Zhou, standing by, said, "What deer? How do you know it's over there?" Xiaoman remembered Yinyue's "This queen lets only you see", so he pulled a blade of grass and held it up. The tip leaned back past them; the wind was coming from the north-east. He said, "Wind's from over there, and there's a musky smell on it. It's a deer."

In fact he couldn't smell a thing. But his father had been smelling the wind all his life, and when he heard his son say this he lifted his face too, sniffed, sniffed for a long time, and then said quietly, "…There is." Whether at that moment his father had really smelled it, or just wouldn't let his son's words fall flat, even his father probably couldn't have said.

Old Zhou tried to stop him: "Your leg—" His father said, "Tomorrow's the first day of winter." Old Zhou stopped trying, and came along himself.

The three of them crept over. Old Zhou stepped on a dry twig and snapped it, got a look for it, and took off both his shoes, carrying them in his hand and going barefoot. After a full three hundred paces they heard the babble of water. Xiaoman parted the leaves and looked out: at the edge of the stream a stag stood with its head down, drinking.

Xiaoman had never in his life seen a deer like it. It was taller than his family's door, with a coat of reddish-brown that shone like satin in the sun, and on its head a pair of antlers like a clump of bare winter branches.

His father nocked an arrow and went to stand up — and couldn't. That left leg had spent a night lying on the slope and then a good while hanging on the cliff. He pushed against the ground twice, and the knee only gave a *click*. He tried to shoot kneeling, but the moment his left knee touched the ground his whole face went white.

Xiaoman said quietly, "Pa, let me." His father looked at him. Looked at him a long time, long enough for the stag to lower its head and drink twice more. Then he handed Grandpa's bow to his son.

The bow was much heavier than Xiaoman had thought. He'd counted the turns of ox sinew on its back — seventeen, as many as the basins at home — but he had never drawn it. The moment his left hand closed on the grip, his swollen wrist hurt so much everything went black. He swallowed the breath back down, nocked the arrow and drew.

Halfway, his arm began to shake, shaking so that the arrow jumped against the bow. He drew another inch, and something in his left wrist gave a small *crick*; an aching pain shot up the inside of his arm right into the hollow of his shoulder. Behind him his father said softly, "First read the wind."

Xiaoman read the wind. The reeds along the stream leaned this way for a moment, then stood up again. He waited. His arm was shaking, his wrist was hurting, and the stag had drunk its fill and might go at any moment. He kept waiting. The gust passed; the reeds wavered twice and slowly rose — and just then Yinyue said in his ear, "Half an inch to the left." Her voice was very soft and very tired, as if it had drifted from a long, long way off. Xiaoman moved half an inch left. The reeds were nearly upright but not quite. His fingers let go.

The arrow was off the string with a *whoosh*, and before he could see where it went, his shoulder suddenly felt light. The little silver fox slid off his neck and dropped, limp. He threw down the bow and caught it — its eyes were shut, its body cold as a lump of ice. Last night it had been burning hot, and hot at least meant alive. Xiaoman panicked, stuffed it inside his jacket and held it against his chest, and felt that it was still breathing, very, very slowly.

He opened his mouth. He wanted to say "thanks", or "thank you", or something — anyway, something with a thank in it. The words were already on the tip of his tongue, but its eyes were shut, and it couldn't hear any more.

The stag by the stream, though, didn't fall. With the arrow hanging from its neck it leapt, bounded over the stream, and plunged into the wood on the far bank. His father grabbed Old Zhou, who was about to give chase: "Don't. Chase it, and it'll run further." Then he shut his eyes and listened for as long as it takes to smoke a pipe before he said, "It can't run any more. Follow the stream down, two hundred paces, there's a bend."

The three of them went down along the stream. After a full two hundred paces the water turned a bend, and on the shallows in the bend the stag lay on its side, the arrow right through its neck, dead on the mark. It had run this far, and only here had it fallen.

His father ran over, limping, ran faster than Old Zhou, and dropped to his knees beside the deer with a thump — knee forgotten — reached out to touch the antlers, and turned round to look at his son, grinning like a child. Xiaoman grinned too. But his hand stayed pressed to his chest the whole time, and he didn't dare take it away.

Old Zhou looked at the deer, then at Xiaoman. "One arrow?" Xiaoman's father said, "One arrow." Old Zhou said, "He's only twelve." His father said, "I didn't touch this bow till I was fourteen. Nor did my father." He bent his head and stroked the stag's neck with his palm, stroke after stroke. Then he said, "He's twelve."

He said nothing more. But the voice he said those two words in was not the voice he'd said "Third Master" to Ma San in.

The door hadn't been lugged up for nothing after all: it carried a deer. His father insisted on taking the back end, and wouldn't listen to anyone. Xiaoman walked last, bow on his back, hand on his chest. The thing against his chest didn't move once the whole way.

---

By the time they reached the village gate it was evening. Under the old locust tree stood two people. One was Ahe, both toes planted side by side against the roots of the old locust tree, her body leaning out in front, her neck stretched like a goose waiting to be fed. Her mother wouldn't let her past the village gate, and the old locust tree was the village gate. Not one of her feet had gone past it. Her neck already had.

The other person standing under the old locust tree was Xiaoman's mother. She had stood there from nightfall the day before right through to this evening, and had gone home only once in between, to put a handful of wood on the stove — in case they came back and found the stove cold. That would never do.

At the back of the crowd that gathered round to look at the deer, Beansprout Liu took one look at it, turned, and ran off towards the Ma place.

His mother came up to her son. First she looked at his face, then at his swollen left wrist. It was wrapped in half a strip of shirt hem, long since soaked by mud and water into a rotten rag. She didn't say a word. She undid the rag, tore a clean strip from under the hem of her own jacket, and wrapped it on again, round and round, and tied it off in a knot, neat and square, exactly like the knots she tied when she mended clothes.

Then she turned and looked at Xiaoman's father. He was carrying the back end of the door; the mud on his face wasn't washed off yet, and his eyes were as red as a rabbit's. The two of them looked at each other, and neither said anything. She reached out and picked a pine needle off his shoulder. Just that, once.

---

That night Ma Xiaobao stopped Ahe by the well platform and traded her a piece of osmanthus cake for talk. "Beansprout Liu says the Shens brought back a great big deer. Was it really that big?" Ahe said, "He saw wrong. A little doe with a lame leg. Otherwise how could Shen Xiaoman have hit it?" Ma Xiaobao thought this made a great deal of sense, pushed the cake into her hand and left. Old Zhou came rushing out of the door, snatched the cake from her and threw it over the fence. "You'd eat something from the Mas? Tomorrow when Ma San sees it's a big stag, that mouth of yours will be the ruin of the Shens!" The osmanthus cake landed at the foot of the fence. Xiaoman looked at it through the fence for a long time, and in the end he didn't pick it up.

---

The first day of winter fell on the fifteenth of the ninth month that year — there'd been a leap eighth month, so the seasons ran ahead, and winter began in the middle of the ninth. Before it was light, Xiaoman was woken by the sound of his mother chopping bones.

*Thock. Thock. Thock.* One stroke after another, heavy and steady. He got up and looked into the kitchen. There was his mother, sleeves rolled up, working his father's short skinning knife down the seams between the bones, cut by cut, and when she reached the joint she turned her wrist, there was a *crack*, and a whole hind leg of deer came away. His father crouched beside her watching with his mouth open, watched a long time, and said, "…When did you learn that?" His mother said, "'[Follow the natural lines, strike in the big gaps, guide the blade through the big hollows, go by how it is made.]{典=zhuangzi-paoding}' Go along the seams and the knife never touches bone."

She went to the head of the bed, opened the wooden chest, and rummaged for a cloth to wrap the leg in. As she rummaged she lifted out a palm-sized bundle of old cloth from the bottom of the chest and put it to one side; when she'd found the cloth she put the bundle back where it had been, very gently, as gently as laying down a sleeping child. In the bundle were the seven or eight pieces of that bowl.

She cut again at the bend of the knee and split the leg in two, wrapped the pieces in the cloth, took the lid off the crock of pickled mustard root in the corner, buried the two pieces of leg under the mustard, pressed it down firm layer by layer, and put the wooden lid back on. Xiaoman said, "Ma, can you pickle deer leg too?" His mother said, "You can pickle it." Xiaoman said, "Does it taste good?" His mother said, "Don't know. Never pickled one." She bound the crock with straw rope and tied it onto his back. "Take Granny Wu a crock of pickles. Go round the back of the houses, not past the well. While it's still dark."

When Xiaoman went out with the crock on his back, his mother had already laid out the rest of the meat, the hide, the antlers and the offal in the yard, all laid out very neatly — and the string of offal, of all things, right in front of the door. Then she washed her hands, let her sleeves down, sat inside the threshold, and waited quietly.

---

At the house at the east end of the village, Granny Wu was already sitting on her doorstep, her face to the thin fish-belly white in the east. Before Xiaoman could open his mouth she said, "The deer leg's come?"

His mother hadn't said a word about any deer leg. Granny Wu said, "Your father promised it. By the mountain stream, crouching there touching that hoof print." That stream was inside Black Pine Ridge, a good thirty or forty li from this doorstep. She showed her three teeth and laughed. "I've got good ears." She patted the doorstep. "Sit down."

So Xiaoman sat. Granny Wu sniffed in the direction of the hill fields outside the village and said to herself, "When Granny was little, come spring, you turned the soil with one stroke of the hoe and it smelled sweet. Back then, this land smelled good." With that she fell silent, and reached out her bony hand to touch his head. When it came to the "scarf" round his neck, her hand stopped, perfectly still, as if afraid that if it moved it would wake something. Then she drew her hand back, and towards that patch of silver light nobody could see she bowed her head, very, very slowly, so low it almost touched her knees, and slowly raised it again, without any expression on her face at all. "Off you go. The sun's coming up. The Ma people will be along."

---

Ma San came at noon. Ma Xiaobao followed behind him, gnawing a soy-braised duck leg.

Ma San stood in the Shens' yard looking at the deer laid out all over the ground, and his eyes narrowed. He picked up the deer hide, gave it a few shakes, and asked, "Black Pine Ridge?" Xiaoman's father said, honestly, "Black Pine Ridge." Ma San slung the hide over his shoulder. "The hide covers last year's debt. Twenty catties of meat covers this year's." Then he poked through the meat on the ground with his club, and his eyes narrowed: "One deer, four legs. Why are there only three?"

Behind his father, Ma Xiaobao piped up, "Pa, Ahe said it was a little doe with a lame leg!" Someone at the gate snorted with laughter, then quickly coughed twice and swallowed the laugh back down. Ma San looked at the antlers, wider than the doorframe, then at his son, and the veins on his forehead throbbed. He waved a hand. "A doe with antlers? …Weigh the meat!" The missing leg he couldn't be bothered to chase. Twenty catties of meat was there, anyway.

His men came forward and weighed the meat. They weighed out a full twenty catties, and the steelyard beam stuck up as high as Ma San's chin.

The weighing done, Ma San didn't leave. Hands behind his back, he took a turn round everything laid out on the ground, came to the antlers, and stopped. He tapped them with his club. "Nice pair of antlers." Xiaoman's father said, "If the Third Master likes them, take them." Ma San said, "It isn't that I like them." He turned round, looked at him, and said, taking his time: "Black Pine Ridge — whose mountain is that?" Xiaoman's father stopped short. Ma San said, "Mount Meng belongs to the Mas. Is Black Pine Ridge on Mount Meng or isn't it?" His father said, "…It's on Mount Meng." Ma San said, "Then a deer from Black Pine Ridge — what's its name?"

The yard went quiet. Half the village was looking at Xiaoman's father. His lips moved twice, and not a word came out. Those hands that hadn't shaken drawing a bow yesterday began to shake again.

Ma San said, "The trees on the mountain are named Ma, the rabbits on the mountain are named Ma, even the wind on the mountain is named Ma. And a deer that runs out of Black Pine Ridge goes by your family name?" He drew his club across the heap of meat on the ground, drawing a circle round it. "The meat, I take back. The antlers, I take back. The hide covers the rent. That's what's called fair." Then he narrowed his eyes and looked into the house — at the old bow on the wall, the one wrapped in ox sinew. "And one more thing. The Ma deer was shot with that bow. A bow that shoots a Ma deer — is named Ma too."

He ducked his head, turned sideways, stepped over the threshold, reached out and took the bow down off the wall.

Xiaoman's father dropped to his knees with a thump. "Third Master! The rent's paid! You said it yourself, one hide—" Ma San said, "A hide is a hide. A bow is a bow." He weighed the bow in his hand. "This is what's called principal plus interest."

Xiaoman stood behind his father. At some point both his fists had clenched. He was two heads shorter than Ma San, and Ma San still had that iron-bound club in his hand — if you can't beat it, run, Ma had said. But this time, if he ran, the bow was gone. One deer hide, twenty catties of meat, the cloth rope hanging down the cliff, that arrow by the stream, the ball of fur on his shoulder going cold — all of it gone on the rent, all of it for nothing, and the bow would still be named Ma. He glanced at his mother. His mother stood up.

She didn't go over to Ma San. She stood just inside the threshold and said, "Third Master. What about the offal?"

Ma San stopped short, the bow hanging from his hand. He looked down at the string of deer offal in front of the door and frowned. "The offal?" She said, "The offal came out of the Ma deer too. The Third Master's taken even the bow; take the offal as well."

Ma San looked at the offal, then at her, and the knotted flesh of his face twitched twice. If he took the offal, then from now on whenever the people of Shi'ao Village mentioned Ma San they'd add, "He even carried off their offal." If he didn't, all that talk just now about "the Ma deer" wouldn't quite hold together. He thought for a while, then waved a big hand. "The offal — you can have it, as a gift!" She said, "Thank you, Third Master." And with that she sat down, back inside the threshold, her hands tucked into her sleeves.

Ma San held the bow and looked down at its back, at the ox sinew wrapped on it turn after turn, and somehow he got lost in looking. Then he went back to Xiaoman's father and said, lowering his voice, "Black Pine Ridge… you went in, and you came out?" He paused. "What else is in there?"

His father opened his mouth to speak. His father was a man who had never told a lie in his life; whatever people asked, he answered. If he told about that valley you couldn't die falling into, the grass in it that glowed and the silver leaves that rang, then tomorrow Ma San would take twenty hounds and dozens of men in there and turn that valley upside down. Xiaoman, standing behind his father, trod lightly on his father's heel.

His father stopped, mouth open, without a sound. Xiaoman got in first — and as he spoke he hunched his neck down, clutched his arms, and shook all over like a leaf in the autumn wind — "Third Master! In there… in there it's all black, and it was pouring, and I fell in a gully and nearly died! And there's a ghost!" Ma San frowned. "What ghost?" "One in white!" Xiaoman shook harder. "No feet, it floats along, its hair's ever so long, trailing right down to the ground — Third Master, you really can't go there, that's where my grandpa died!"

Several of the women in the yard had already clapped their hands over their mouths, each pair of eyes rounder than the next. Ma San said, "…In white?" "And it eats flatbread!" Ma San stopped. His two eyebrows went one up and one down and screwed themselves into two knots. "A ghost that eats flatbread?" "Gobbles it up!" Xiaoman showed with his hands. "My ma's flatbread, this thick, and it finished it in three bites, and the whole time it was eating it kept saying it was disgusting!" At this point his eyes flicked to Ma San's hand, and his voice suddenly dropped, dropped till it was only a breath: "…And when it finished the flatbread, it asked me—"

Ma San said, "Asked you what?"

Xiaoman said, "It asked: 'Where's the bow?'"

Ma San didn't move. Sweat broke out on his forehead, a drop as big as a soybean, which rolled down the bridge of his nose to the tip, hung there, and fell — *plip* — onto the back of the bow. After a long moment, his throat a little dry, he said, "…Did you see its face clearly?"

Xiaoman said, "No face. Just a white beard."

Only then did Ma San's hand give a shake, and his throat went *gulp* as he swallowed a big mouthful of spit. Xiaoman kept shaking, shook from the top of his head to his heels, shook until his teeth were chattering too: "It said that bow is its bow. Whoever takes its bow, it follows them home." But inside, even as he shook, he was thinking: Yinyue, sorry. If you were awake you'd bite me for sure. Shaking and shaking, he himself couldn't tell any more how much of all this shaking was put on.

Ma San's face went red first, then purple, then black, and in the end it turned that colour. He looked at the bow in his hand as if it were a coiled snake. After a long moment he threw it into the arms of Xiaoman's father, threw it fast and flustered, as though the bow were burning his hand, and swore: "Bad luck!"

Ma Xiaobao, though, wasn't afraid. Following his father, he walked past Xiaoman and suddenly reached out his greasy hand, grabbed the "scarf" round Xiaoman's neck, and brought it up under his nose to sniff. "Smells like dog. The fur's soft, though — I'll take it home and have Ma sew a collar out of it." Xiaoman clamped both hands over it, and his face shook harder than ever: "Brother Xiaobao, this pelt… I picked it up in Black Pine Ridge, right under that white beard's feet." Ma Xiaobao gave a yelp. His hand shot back as if it had been singed, his fat face went as white as a bun fresh out of the steamer, and he wiped it on his clothes three times. "…Who wants it? A fox pelt — we've got as many of those as we like at home."

Ma San had reached the gate. He turned round and said to everyone in the yard, "Listen, all of you. From today, anyone who goes one step near that broken stone, I'll break his legs." With that he stamped his foot and left. Ma Xiaobao followed. Passing Old Zhou's gate, he saw Ahe crouching inside the fence and kicked over the crock of pickled cabbage she'd set by the foot of the wall: "Little doe, eh?" The crock rolled over twice on the ground, and a whole autumn's worth of cabbage leaves spilled out across it.

---

The Ma people had gone off, and the onlookers had gone too. His father hung the bow back on the wall — it took him two tries to get it to stay — then crouched and gathered up the string of offal, very carefully. He said, "…This goes to Old Zhou's family. To make up for the cabbage." His mother said, "Mm. I'll take it over." His father said, "The padded jacket…" His mother said, "No hurry." And she tucked her two hands into her sleeves. That padded jacket of hers had been made the year she married into the family; its sleeves were too short by a good bit, and when she tucked her hands in, her wrists stuck out, red with cold.

She called Xiaoman over and looked at him a long time before she said, "That ghost in white, with no feet, that eats flatbread." Xiaoman had to own up. "…Ma, I made it up." "Mm. You made it up badly." His mother paused. "Ghosts don't eat flatbread. Next time say it eats incense." Xiaoman stopped. "Why?" His mother said, "A ghost that eats flatbread — Ma San will think it's a starved ghost, and starved ghosts are easy to get rid of; throw them a couple of flatbreads and that's that. A ghost that eats incense is one that somebody's worshipping. A ghost with somebody worshipping it is one Ma San can't afford to cross."

His mother reached out and straightened the "scarf" round his neck. She couldn't see Yinyue — and yet her hand came to rest right on Yinyue's back, and rested there a moment. Xiaoman looked down and saw the little unconscious fox in the scarf twitch an ear, very slightly. His mother said, "Xiaoman, that scarf's nice and warm, isn't it."

She didn't ask anything. His father, holding the string of offal, stood in the doorway and looked off towards Black Pine Ridge. "We're not allowed near the broken stone now. This winter…" His mother said, "This winter has a long way to go yet."

---

When the sun was sinking west, his mother sent him to the east end of the village to bring the crock back. Granny Wu's face was already turned to the west. Hearing the crock rattle, she said, "The bow — it's come back?" Xiaoman said, "It's come back." Then she lifted her face and told a very short story.

In the old days, the very first emperor under heaven cast a cauldron at the foot of a mountain. On the day the cauldron was finished, a dragon came down from the sky to fetch him. The emperor climbed on, and his great officials and his ladies climbed on after him, seventy-odd of them. One minor official was left who couldn't squeeze on; he threw himself at the dragon's whiskers, caught hold, and hung there in mid-air. He had a daughter, only seven or eight, down below with her face turned up, calling "Pa" over and over. The whiskers snapped, and the people fell down, and along with them fell the emperor's bow. People after that called the place [Cauldron Lake]{典=shiji-dinghu}, and called the bow Crow's Cry. When she got to the daughter with her face turned up calling for her father, her own face was turned up too, towards the sky.

Xiaoman said, "And the daughter?" Granny Wu said, "…Cried, what else?" Then: "The ones who went up went up. The one left behind kept a bow." Xiaoman asked, "That bow — did anyone ever draw it after?" Granny Wu turned her face towards him and slowly let a smile come. "That's a good question." And she said no more, only added, to herself: "Cultivate, cultivate. Once you've cultivated yourself into someone who wants to go up to heaven, you've cultivated as far as you'll go."

---

That night Xiaoman carried the "scarf" up onto the woodpile behind the house. There was nobody on the woodpile, only the moon.

It hadn't woken all day. Its body was still cold, and the eight stumps at the root of its tail had started oozing blood again, silver, dropping one drop at a time onto the brushwood, like a layer of frost. He held it on his knees and shielded it from the wind with his body. In the middle of the night it opened one eye, and the first thing it said was, "…What a racket. Why do you breathe so loud?"

Xiaoman let out a long breath and nearly cried. There was a question he'd been holding in all day. "Granny Wu said an old man who rode a white crane once lived on top of Mount Meng and lived to eight hundred. Pa says that's made up. Immortals — are they real?"

"Your father's wrong," Yinyue said. "Immortals are real. This queen has seen more immortals than you've seen chickens." She snorted. "Every one of them was a mortal once, just like you; hungry, they gnawed flatbread. Later they learned to take the spirit qi of heaven and earth and store it in their own bodies, bit by bit, the way your mother stores rice in the crock. Store enough and you don't age, don't sicken, and you're strong enough to move a mountain. — That's all there is to it. It's called cultivating immortality."

It was very quiet on the woodpile. After a good while Xiaoman asked, "And riding a dragon… is that like the emperor in Granny Wu's story?" Yinyue's ear twitched. She looked at the moon, looked at it so long Xiaoman thought she'd gone back to sleep, before she said, "…This queen doesn't remember." What she said was "doesn't remember", not "doesn't know". At the time Xiaoman didn't hear any difference between the two.

"Then… could I do it too?" "You could. But it's hard. Out of a hundred, ninety-nine just die of old age and that's that." "What's spirit qi? What are meridians?" "Ask them. There's a whole crowd of people in this world whose job is teaching people to cultivate. More patient than this queen. …Not as good at teaching as this queen, of course." Her voice got softer and softer, until it wasn't there at all. She was asleep, the tip of her tail lying across his wrist, across the square, neat knot his mother had tied.

---

The next day she didn't wake. Every little while Xiaoman looked down at his chest. His mother asked if his chest hurt. He said no, he was just checking it was still there.

That night it was Yinyue who woke him. Xiaoman slept on the woodpile — he'd told his mother it was crowded in the house, and his mother had given him a look and then given him a ragged old quilt — and in the middle of the night his chest suddenly felt cold, as if a lump of ice he was carrying had melted all at once. He sat bolt upright. The ball of silver fur had already crept out of his jacket and was crouched on top of the woodpile, facing the moon.

She looked worse than yesterday. The silver blood had stopped seeping, but her whole body was as thin as a sheet of paper, and the moonlight shone right through her. Thinner, and thinner again — so this was what she'd meant in the valley. She said, "Listen. This queen has to sleep for a while." "How long?" "Don't know. The wounds are too deep. I have to go into seclusion." She looked at him. "Until this queen wakes up, you have to go on by yourself."

From a little cloth bag under her neck, so old it had gone black, she spat things out one by one. The first was a pill, dark and murky, as big as a pigeon's egg, smelling like rotten cabbage leaves soaked for three days. She said, "[Marrow-Washing Pill]{注=xisui-pill}. Soak in it tonight. However much it hurts, don't cry out." Xiaoman held his nose. "Wash… wash what?" "Wash all the channels in that body of yours." She drew a breath, like someone reciting a saying she'd recited for thousands of years: "[Mortals have these eight channels; all belong to the yin spirit; they are shut and do not open]{典=qijing-bamai}." Xiaoman said, "What are the eight channels?" "Eight water channels in your body, and they're all blocked." "And blocked ones — how do you open them?" She didn't answer. Her eyelids drooped, and she went on spitting things out.

The second was a roll of bamboo slips. The cord was nearly rotted through, and the slips had wormholes in them. But the moment it landed on the brushwood the night air around them cleared, as if after a light rain, and when Xiaoman picked it up the slips were actually warm. She said, "[*The Breathing Classic*]{注=tuna-jing}. The crudest beginner's practice; every three-year-old fox cub in the immortal realm knows it. Even if you can't understand it, learn it by heart." Then she looked at him and said, one word at a time: "**Hide.**" Xiaoman said, "Hide what?"

"Hide this queen, and hide yourself," she said. "Until you can beat everyone, nobody is allowed to know about this queen, and nobody is allowed to know you're cultivating. Play the fool wherever you can; look weak wherever you can. — That ghost of yours that eats flatbread was very good. From now on, you're the silly boy who saw a ghost and got the shakes." "Can't I even tell my ma?" Yinyue said, "Your ma doesn't need you to tell her."

Xiaoman remembered his mother's hand straightening the scarf, and her "That scarf's nice and warm, isn't it", and didn't ask any more. "Then when can I stop hiding?" "When you've put everyone who ever bullied you under your feet, one by one."

Her eyelids were getting heavier and heavier, and her voice lower and lower. "One more thing… this queen is looking for a place… a name…" She frowned, trying very hard to think. "…Can't remember. When you… hear it, this queen will know…" Xiaoman said, "What place? What name? Just tell me roughly, I'll keep an ear out for you—" But she had stopped talking. Her body was fading in the moonlight bit by bit, fading like the last wisp of blue smoke in the stove.

Xiaoman panicked. This time she really was going, going somewhere he couldn't reach. That word — if he didn't say it now, it would truly be too late. He opened his mouth. "Yinyue—" She looked at him. She'd faded so much she was nearly invisible; only a pair of golden eyes still shone in the moonlight, waiting for him to go on.

His mouth was open, and the word was on the tip of his tongue. But somehow, when it came out, it turned into: "…You still owe me a flatbread, you know." The golden eyes blinked once. Her body turned into a thread of silver light. The light circled him once, brushed his face lightly, cool, and slipped inside his jacket, into the fox-carved wooden tablet Granny Wu had given him. On the tablet, of the little curled-up fox's nine tails, one lit up, silver, glowing, dimming, as if breathing very, very slowly. Xiaoman looked down at the tablet and said quietly, "…Hey, are you still there?" The tablet didn't answer. He said again, "You still owe me a flatbread, you know." The tablet grew warm, for a moment.

He sat on the woodpile a long time, then lifted the tablet up to his lips and said, very, very quietly, "Yinyue." He paused.

"Thank you."

The tablet stayed cool. It didn't warm. The silver tail went on glowing and dimming as before. She was sleeping too deeply. She couldn't hear.

---

The night Yinyue turned to light and went into the tablet, Xiaoman soaked in the Marrow-Washing Pill she'd left. He hauled seven buckets of water up from the well and poured them into the big wooden tub his mother did the washing in. He dropped the pill in, it dissolved with a *tsss*, and the clear water at once turned dark green and started bubbling.

He took off his clothes, hung the wooden tablet on a forked stick in the woodpile, and said to it, "You watch. I won't yell." Then he set his teeth and got in.

It was a pain that drilled outward from the cracks between his bones, drilled inch by inch from his toes to the ends of his hair. He bit down on his own arm, bit till it bled, and didn't make a sound. Things kept seeping out of his body, black and sticky, and the tub of water went from dark green to pitch black. The pain made everything go white in front of his eyes, again and again, and in the white, sometimes there was his mother crouched on the floor picking up the bowl, and sometimes there was the hand his father had reached down from the cliff.

When the pain stopped, the moon had moved from this end of the woodpile to the other. He hauled himself out by the edge of the tub. There was a faint layer of light under his skin, like a piece of jade under the moon. The wrist that had been swollen for days wasn't swollen at all. He tried a little jump — *thunk* — and his head had already hit the ridge beam of the woodshed. "Your grandpa's—" That beam was a good ten feet off the ground. He crouched holding his head, buried his face in his knees, and laughed. When he'd laughed enough, he took the tablet down from the forked stick, clutched it in his palm, and said, "Yinyue. I didn't yell." The tablet didn't answer.

Early next morning the big wooden tub had gone. He ran to the well platform and found his mother crouched there scrubbing the tub with a handful of straw dipped in wood ash, stroke after stroke. The black water from the tub had been poured into the drain below the well platform, and overnight all the grass in the drain had turned yellow. She finished the third scrub, set the tub upside down on the well platform to dry, and only then looked up at his left hand. Xiaoman hid the hand behind his back, then brought it out and waved it, grimacing: "Ma, it still hurts." His mother looked at it a while. "Then keep it wrapped two more days."

She got up and went indoors. At the door, without turning round, she said, "That tub. If you want it at night from now on, fetch it yourself. When you're done, scrub it yourself."

It was the first time in her life his mother had hidden something for him.

---

He stuffed the bamboo slips at the very bottom of the woodpile, and it wasn't until the night of the eighteenth of the ninth month — three days after the first day of winter — that he took them out for the first time. He waited until his father's snoring had reached its third round — his father's snoring came in rounds, and once it reached the third, the sky could fall and he wouldn't wake — and then carried the slips into the kitchen. By the oil lamp, its flame no bigger than a soybean, his mother ran her finger along the characters one by one, reached the first line, and stopped. That line read: "[Blow and puff, exhale and inhale; breathe out the old, take in the new]{典=zhuangzi-keyi}." She said, "This line… your grandfather used to recite it."

Xiaoman had never met his grandfather. His grandfather had been a poor village schoolmaster in town and had died before he was born. His mother looked up. "Where did… where did this come from?" "Found it in the mountains." "…Found it along with that scarf?" Xiaoman remembered Yinyue's "Your ma doesn't need you to tell her" and said, "…Mm. Found them together." His mother didn't ask any more.

She read on, very slowly; half the characters further on she didn't know herself. At one place it said that for breathing in there's only one door, [but for breathing out there are six]{典=yangxing-liuzi}. She said, "One way in, six ways out. Like our family's money."

She came to the oldest slip, the bamboo so dark it was almost black. That slip didn't have many characters on it; [they ran down one step at a time, like a ladder]{典=xingqi-ming}, and when the lamp flickered, the ladder almost seemed to be climbing. She copied the first character of each step onto the last page of her account book, ten of them, and said, "These are the ten steps one breath takes going into the body. Each step you reach, Ma draws a circle." Then: "No rushing."

Xiaoman said impatiently, "But how do you actually practise it?" His mother turned the slips to just before the end. In the whole text there was only one line with a number in it: [count to a hundred and twenty in your heart, hold a wild goose's down feather under your nose, breathe out, and the feather must not move]{典=baopu-shizhi}. Down like that comes off a wild goose, and the wild geese had long since flown south. She said, "We'll make do with one of Old Zhou's goose feathers for now."

Xiaoman counted on his fingers. "A hundred and twenty… Ma, people aren't fish." His mother couldn't help it; she snorted with laughter.

Just then, in the inner room, his father's snoring suddenly broke off. Not the kind of break between one round and the next: halfway through there was a *click*, and it stopped dead. Mother and son turned their heads together and looked at the door curtain. After a good while the snoring started again, and very hard-working snoring it was, jumping straight from the first round to the third in one breath.

Xiaoman looked at his mother. His mother looked at the lamp, pinched the wick a little shorter, and said, "Read the next line."

---

The nineteenth of the ninth month, midnight. The coldest place in Shi'ao Village was the top of the woodpile behind the Shens' house. Shen Xiaoman sat there cross-legged, a thin-shaved strip of bamboo held across under his nose, with a goose feather stuck to it. The goose feather came from Old Zhou's big white goose. It had been plucked that afternoon, and the goose had stretched out its neck and gone "Hawnk — hawnk —" to half the village. Ahe, standing by, tapped her forehead with a finger: "One goose feather. The goose was hurt. Interest is separate."

Xiaoman quietly drew a breath in through his nose, filled himself up, held it, and counted in his head: one, two, three… Counting had to be like his mother working the abacus, one bead at a time, no rushing. At nine his chest felt tight; at eleven it was like there was a wad of cotton stuffed in his throat; at twelve the breath burst his mouth open by itself with a *pff*, and the goose feather flew off the bamboo strip, turned three somersaults in the moonlight, and landed in the chicken coop at the foot of the woodpile. There was an uproar in the chicken coop.

Only then did he realise he'd been breathing in by "swallowing" with his throat. It got stuck in his chest; his shoulders went up, and the count broke. So he let his belly take it instead, and the breath went down smoothly, a good long way deeper than his chest. On the eighth of the tenth month his mother drew a circle round the character "Deep" on the last page of her account book and said, "Xiaoman doesn't snore at night any more." His father objected: "I still do." His mother said, "You do. The harmony's one voice short." By the eleventh month he'd gone from carrying four loads of water to six, and didn't get out of breath going uphill, so he had to pretend to; a circle went round "Store" as well. Sitting on the woodpile at night, his hands and feet weren't cold any more either, so he made his mother wear his old padded coat — she was up at the fifth watch rinsing rice — whether she would or not, and wore just a thin unlined jacket himself.

---

On the twenty-second of the eleventh month Ma San came to collect mountain wind money, fifty coppers a head, knocking on the doorframes house by house. Ma Xiaobao followed behind, wearing a new fox-fur coat, bright fiery red, with a fur collar that came right up to his chin; his forehead was shiny with sweat under it.

This time, there was someone else behind Ma San as well.

The man was dressed all in white, white the same colour as the snow; standing in the snow, he looked like a piece of the snow had stood itself up. There wasn't the faintest smell of hearth smoke about him. Anyone who came near him felt a chill, as if someone had pushed open a window in the depths of winter. He didn't speak, and he didn't go in. Each house Ma San knocked at, he would stand at its door a moment and glance inside — and what he looked at wasn't the people, but the roof beams, the walls, the head of the bed. Ma San knocked on doorframes more lightly than in other years, and as he went he kept glancing back from the corner of his eye.

Only later did the villagers piece together roughly what had happened. Once winter came, this man had been going round Mount Meng village by village, and in each village he went first to whoever collected the rent and asked just one thing: in the autumn, had anyone on the mountain seen anything white? Every other village said no. When he got to the Mas, Ma San remembered rent day, and the ghost the Shen boy had talked about: in white, no feet, ate flatbread. That ghost had been made up to scare Ma San, and Ma San had sold it on, just as it was, to a man colder than any ghost.

When they knocked at the Shens', his father brought out a hundred and fifty coppers, which his mother had taken out of the New Year dumpling money. Ma Xiaobao came up to Xiaoman and looked him up and down. "Shen Xiaoman, a thin jacket in the middle of winter — frozen stupid?" Xiaoman hugged his arms, teeth chattering: "F-freezing, Brother Xiaobao." Ma Xiaobao stroked the ring of fox fur on his collar. "See this? Real fox. Eight taels of silver." He turned and called back to the man in white: "Immortal! That's him! He's the one who crawled out of Black Pine Ridge — he used to have a grey dog-pelt scarf round his neck, said it was a dead fox's skin!"

The man in white turned his face and looked at Xiaoman. His face was very white, so white it had no age; you couldn't tell if he was thirty or sixty. When he spoke his voice was dry and deep: "The scarf?"

The wooden tablet against Xiaoman's chest went suddenly hot.

That voice was dry and deep, like the echo that comes back up when someone leans over the mouth of an old well and shouts down it. The hairs on the back of Xiaoman's neck stood up one by one. He hugged his arms and shook harder than ever. "M-my ma took it apart and st-stuffed it in my shoes."

The man in white glanced down at the straw sandals on his feet, toes sticking out, said nothing, and raised his eyes to look into the house. What he looked at was the old bow on the wall, the one wrapped in ox sinew. He looked at it a while, said, "Dead things. No need to look," turned and left. Ma San hurried after him, then came back for the club he'd leaned against the doorframe, and as he picked it up he didn't dare look at the bow.

The tablet went hot again, and then a third time, so hot his heart flinched. When the Ma people had gone off, he took the tablet out and held it in his palm and said quietly, "…You saw it too? That coat?" The tablet slowly cooled, glowing, dimming, back the way it had been, like someone sleeping very, very deep down. He thought she was just cross about that ring of fox fur.

Xiaoman looked up and saw his mother standing inside the door, hands tucked in her sleeves. He went over, pulled her hands out of her sleeves, and held them in his own. His mother's hands were ice cold, so cold he jumped; but his mother was even more startled than he was. She tried to pull her hands back and couldn't. "…These hands of yours — why are they warm?" Xiaoman said, "The third word in the book. Stretch."

His mother held on to his hands, held them for a long time, so long that Ma San's club had knocked on two more doorframes down the way, before she said, "…Inside." That night, on the last page of the account book, "Stretch" got its circle too.

A few days later a pedlar on the southern road stopped to rest by the well platform. When he heard the women talking about the man in white from the twenty-second of the eleventh month, he set down his carrying pole and said, "In white? I heard about one like that over Min River way. Twelve or thirteen years back, in the last month of the year, he held a stone up over a poor family's bamboo basket, and the stone lit up all gold. So he put down a packet of silver and lifted out the baby in the basket — born just seven days. The mother ran out the door after him and called once, and he was already gone." Someone asked what the silver looked like. The pedlar said, "That family took the silver to buy rice, and the rice-shop man said every ingot had a mark chiselled on the bottom: a circle, with one stroke across inside it." Then: "It was snowing that day too. In the snow, he left no footprints."

Xiaoman, standing behind the women, remembered the look in the man's eyes when he'd stood at their door looking at the bow on the wall, and the back of his neck went cold again.

---

In the last month of the year there was a pair of eyes at the foot of the woodpile. Beansprout Liu set nets for sparrows behind the Shens' house on winter nights; he sold them for two coppers each. Three nights running he saw white breath rising from the top of the woodpile: one puff, then a very long stop, so long it didn't seem like anyone living. He also saw something on the chest of whoever it was, the size of a palm, glowing and dimming in the dark.

Some time in the middle of that month, Xiaoman glanced up from under the old locust tree and saw, at the far end of the threshing floor, Ma Xiaobao take a piece of osmanthus cake from his sleeve and hold it out to Beansprout Liu. Beansprout Liu stared at the cake; his Adam's apple went up and down three times, and his spit nearly ran down onto his chin. He stared a long time, then took it, bit into it, leaned close to Ma Xiaobao's ear and said something. As he talked he showed the size of a palm with his hand, and pointed in the direction of the Shens' woodpile.

---

After the New Year — by the village way of counting, a year older for every New Year — Xiaoman was thirteen.

Early on the fourth of the first month his father crouched in the snow behind the house looking at something for a long time. A line of small straw-sandal prints came round from outside the yard wall and stopped at the foot of the woodpile; and last night there'd been seven or eight pairs of boot prints besides, which had gone round the yard wall, stood behind the house, and trampled a patch into a mess. His father stood up and said only two words: "Scouting us."

On the night of the fifth of the first month Xiaoman didn't go up the woodpile. He stuffed the bamboo slips into the flue under the bed, tucked the wooden tablet against his skin, and lay on the bed with his eyes open, listening outside. In the inner room his father's snoring went round after round.

Around midnight, torches lit up beyond the yard wall. Seven or eight of them, bobbing. At the front was that fiery-red fox-fur coat, and behind it Ma San. Behind the torches there was a white shape too, carrying no torch, walking on the snow without a sound. They didn't come in through the gate; they went round along the foot of the wall to the back of the house and made straight for the woodpile. Xiaoman lay flat with his eye to the crack of the window, his heart jumping into his throat: at some point, his father's snoring had stopped.

The torches surrounded the woodpile. On top of the woodpile sat a man, wrapped in the ragged old quilt, one leg stuck straight out, the knee wrapped in sacking, sitting very properly. It was his father.

Ma Xiaobao held up his torch and gaped for a long time. "…Old cripple? What are you doing up there?" His father said, "Knee hurts, can't sleep. Came out to ask the sky." "Where's Shen Xiaoman?" "Asleep." "And that piece of wood that glows at night?" His father said, honestly, "On my son."

The white shape spoke, in the same dry, deep voice: "Bring him out."

Two of the men kicked the door open and dragged Xiaoman off the bed. He had on only a thin unlined jacket and was barefoot. They pushed him out into the snow, pushed him up to the woodpile. His mother came out after him and stood in the doorway. She didn't move, and didn't say anything, her two hands clenched in her sleeves.

The man in white took a bronze mirror from his sleeve. It was the size of a palm, its face polished bright as snow, its back cast with a ring of fine patterns, its rim crusted with green rust. The moment the mirror came out of his sleeve, the flames of the seven or eight torches all ducked down together, as if something had pressed on them. He tilted the mirror. The torchlight fell into it and came back out of it as a patch of cold white light, paler than the torches and colder than moonlight, lying on the snow like an open eye.

That patch of light moved, an inch at a time. It moved over the snow, over the chicken coop at the foot of the woodpile, over his father's outstretched leg. Then it moved towards Xiaoman.

The wooden tablet against Xiaoman's chest suddenly went warm.

Not burning hot: warm, pulse by pulse, keeping time with his heart. The faster his heart beat, the warmer the tablet got, until it was like carrying a coal fresh out of the stove. What would happen when that light touched it, he didn't know, and didn't dare know. He wanted to run — if you can't beat it, run, there's no shame in running — but there were torches in front and behind and on both sides. He tried holding his breath, and the harder he held it, the louder his heart banged, and the warmer the tablet got.

The patch of light reached his feet.

Overhead, on top of the woodpile, his father suddenly spoke. His voice was pressed very low, as if talking to himself, or to that knee of his — in the very same tone he used lying in the brush in the mountains, whispering into his son's ear:

"When you're lying in wait for a river deer, don't hold your breath. Let your breath sink down to your feet." A breath's pause. "What it hears isn't your breathing. It's your nerves."

Xiaoman stopped holding his breath. He let it go down, down, further down, sinking past his chest, past his belly, right down into the snow under his heels. Then he narrowed his lips and breathed out, thin, thin, thinner than a hair, so that even his own ears couldn't hear it. The thudding in his chest slowed. And slowed again.

The patch of light climbed up his thin jacket, climbed up onto his chest, and stopped there.

The tablet went cool. Cool as a piece of ice against his heart, so cold he jumped. The patch of light stayed on his chest a good while. The mirror was grey. Nothing lit up.

The man in white put out two fingers, slid them into his collar, and hooked out the wooden tablet. Those two fingers were colder than the tablet. He laid the tablet on the face of the mirror: a dull grey piece of old wood, carved with a curled-up fox with nine tails, not one of them lit, cold to the touch as a stone at the bottom of a well. He turned it over, glanced at the back, and let go. The tablet dropped back against Xiaoman's chest.

"Dead."

He put the mirror away, but he didn't leave. He just stood there, looking at Xiaoman. A thirteen-year-old in nothing but a thin jacket, barefoot, standing in the snow of the first month — not shaking.

When Xiaoman realised, the sweat on his back went cold. He started shaking at once, shaking till his teeth chattered, hugging his arms, curled up in a ball. From the doorway his mother said, "The child's freezing."

The man in white looked at him a long time more, so long that the torch in Ma Xiaobao's hand burned down a good bit. Then he turned and handed the bronze mirror to Ma San. "Keep it. If the mirror lights up, send word."

Ma San took it in both hands and held it like a piece of red-hot iron.

The man in white walked to the gap in the yard wall, stood there a moment, and without turning round said, "…Let it grow." He went out past the wall and into the snow. Xiaoman watched his back, watched under his feet — in the snow, not one footprint.

Ma Xiaobao jabbed his torch into the snow. "Whatever Shen Xiaoman's hiding, I'll dig it out sooner or later." He took the men and left.

His father slowly worked his way down off the woodpile. His knee gave a *click* as he reached the ground. He stood a while with a hand on the woodpile, said not one word, and went indoors. Xiaoman stood in the snow, his feet frozen numb, and only then did he notice that this time he really was shaking.

The next day it snowed. Overnight the side of the woodpile facing the road had grown taller by a good bit, stacked stick by stick, tight and solid, like a wall; looking over from the road, you couldn't see a thing. No wind could have blown it into that shape.

At the well platform, in front of a circle of women doing their washing, Xiaoman said, all of a tremble, "That thing on the woodpile isn't a person. It's a ghost — white clothes, no feet, sits there and doesn't breathe. Whoever heard of a ghost breathing? My ma burns three sticks of incense for it every night, and that's the only reason it doesn't come into the house." Last year on rent day this ghost had eaten flatbread; now it had switched to incense. A ghost with somebody worshipping it was one the Mas couldn't afford to cross. From then on, once it got dark, the Ma men would sooner go half a mile round than pass behind the Shens' house. When his mother heard, she really did stick three sticks of incense at the foot of the woodpile that very night.

---

When spring came, in the second month, a notice was posted at the gate of the county town at the foot of Mount Meng.

On the third of the third month, the spring purification festival, [Uncle]{注=jiujiu} came. Uncle was Xiaoman's mother's own younger brother. He kept the accounts at the Jishi Hall, an apothecary in the county town, and was the only one in all the Shens' relations who had "made something of himself". He wore a brand-new long gown of blue cloth and carried two catties of meat and a packet of brown sugar. He stood at the door, which had been put back crooked, held his sleeve over his nose, and coughed. He looked at the roof, he looked at the row of basins in the corner catching drips, and he lowered his voice. "Sister, if you'd listened to Father back then—" His mother said, "Eat." Uncle shut his mouth. Something stirred in Xiaoman: what had Grandfather said? His father kept his head down, rubbing his knee, stroke after stroke, rubbing hard.

At the meal, Uncle took out a sheet of paper. "The notice they posted in the county town. I copied it." He cleared his throat and read: "**The Sunken Cauldron Abbey** opens its gate once every three years to take disciples. All aged twelve to sixteen and of honest family may present themselves for examination on the ninth of the ninth month at the foot of Sunken Cauldron Mountain on the Si River. For each one taken as a disciple, the Abbey shall pay the family five taels of silver every year—"

He'd only just read out the words "Sunken Cauldron" when the tablet on Xiaoman's chest went hot as a coal, so hot that he leapt off the bench with a "Yaagh—!", and a whole mouthful of food went, dead on the mark, all over Uncle's face. Uncle, his face covered in rice, blinked twice. A grain of rice dropped off his eyebrow — *plip* — into his bowl.

Nobody saw the thread of silver light leaking between the fingers of the hand Xiaoman had clapped to his chest. On the tablet the silver tail had stopped glowing and dimming. It was lit, lit and staying lit, like someone who'd been searching in a dream for a long, long time and had finally heard the name they were searching for.

His mother asked, "Five taels of silver?" "Five taels a year." His father put down his chopsticks. All the rent the Shens paid the Mas in a year, mountain wind money included, came to no more than three taels. Uncle said, "There's more. A family with a child in an immortal sect — the Mas wouldn't dare collect rent from them." The room went quiet. After a long moment his father asked, in a small voice, "And the mountain wind money?" Uncle stopped. "…The notice doesn't mention wind." He paused, then said, "Sister. Twenty years ago, I went too."

"I was fourteen that year. Three hundred stone steps, to be climbed in the time it takes one stick of incense to burn. I got to step one hundred and seventy and my legs wouldn't move. I sat there for a whole stick of incense, watching the others go past me one by one. Then I went back down the mountain and went to the Jishi Hall to learn the abacus." When he said this his voice was as flat as if he were reading out a line in an account book. Xiaoman's mother said softly, "Last time it was a hundred and sixty."

Uncle's face went at once as red as the two catties of meat he'd brought. "It goes up every year, all right!" After a while Uncle turned and looked at Xiaoman. "Your mother can read. You take after her, your head's quicker than mine. Your father hunts. You take after him, your legs are steadier than mine. Go and try. If you don't get in, you've lost nothing. If you do get in—" He didn't finish. He glanced at his brother-in-law's knee, lowered his head, and ate.

That night Xiaoman lay on the bed doing his sums, item by item. Five taels of silver, a year. His father would never again have to drag that leg into the deep mountains. Ma San's club would never again knock on the Shens' doorframe. The mirror in Ma San's jacket could never shine into the home of an immortal sect's disciple. Then there was the other sum. He took the tablet out of his collar and held it to his ear to listen. "…This queen is looking for a place… a name… when you hear it, this queen will know…" He'd heard it. He said quietly to the dark, "All right. I'll go."

That night his mother turned the bamboo slips to the very last one, came to the words "Foundation Establishment", and her finger stopped, and stayed stopped a long time. "Foundation Establishment," she said, "is like building a house: you lay the foundations first. It says here, a hundred days." She rolled up the slips and said, "Your grandfather said a person has to go upward in this life. I said there are plenty of people going upward; somebody at the foot of the mountain has to keep the stove lit." She blew out the lamp. In the dark Xiaoman counted on his fingers: a hundred days. A hundred and eighty-three days till the ninth of the ninth month. Enough to build one foundation, with eighty-three days to spare. He'd got this sum wrong.

---

After spring came, that thread of warmth went down with the breath, sinking past his chest, and when it got to just below his heart it stopped. Further down, however he pushed it, it wouldn't go, like a bucket stuck halfway down a well rope. It stayed stuck all spring and all summer. On the last page of his mother's account book, "Down" stayed empty.

Xiaoman knew perfectly well why it was stuck. The Ma men didn't dare come behind the Shens' house any more, so Ma San came himself — it was an errand from the Immortal, and he didn't dare not do it. Every few days, once it got dark, a lantern would swing past outside the yard wall, going very fast, swinging as if it had the shakes, and what it was carrying was that mirror. The mirror had never once lit up in Ma San's jacket. Pointed at him it was grey; pointed at the wall it was grey too. But the Immortal had said keep it, so he kept it. And yet, whenever the lantern came, Xiaoman held his breath, and the more he held it, the tighter the bucket stuck.

In the eighth month, word that the Sunken Cauldron Abbey was opening its gate spread through all the villages round about. Old Master Ma prepared a gift of three hundred taels of silver, no less, and sent Ma San up the mountain overnight to beg a "letter of recommendation" for Ma Xiaobao. Three hundred taels was more than a hundred years of the Shens' rent. At the drinking table at the village gate, Ma San went red in the face with drink. "With a letter, they'll take you even with a bit of a poor spirit root! My cousin there has kept the register at the Abbey for twenty-odd years. What roots and bones anybody's child has, one look and he's got it right — up on Mount Taihua there's the Taiyi Palace, the number one immortal sect under heaven, and the Immortals who come down from the Palace all go to him — that one who came to our village twice, that's one of them! My Xiaobao's going to be an immortal master one day!" Ma San thumped the table and went on, "And then, the wind on Mount Meng—" The villagers chimed in all together, very well practised: "Is named Ma!"

The letter was for Ma Xiaobao alone, but the register was for the whole village. The county had sent an old runner round the villages to collect the register, and the big household of each village had to put its mark to it as guarantor. In Shi'ao Village, the only big household was the Mas. When Ma San got to "Shen Xiaoman", his brush stopped. "Owes the Ma family rent. Not honest." His mother stepped forward and, in front of the runner, went through it line by line: which year's debt was the deer hide, which year's rent was the twenty catties of meat, whose men had weighed it with their own hands. Of all the people there, not one said it wasn't so. The runner still had another village to get to. When he'd heard her out he said, "The account's clear. Put your mark." Ma San put his mark, and set the brush down on the table. "Marked or not, he's still got to climb it. Three hundred stone steps — that boy of yours gets out of breath carrying two buckets of water."

That night Xiaoman sat on the woodpile, gritting his teeth. "Gets out of breath carrying two buckets of water" — the words went round and round inside his chest, and every time they went round, the breath pushed back up. Beyond the yard wall, Ma San's lantern came swinging by again. He remembered that night in the first month, and what his father had said in a low voice from the top of the woodpile: What it hears isn't your breathing. It's your nerves. So he unclenched his teeth. He didn't press it down, and he didn't count. He just breathed out thin, thin, and when he'd breathed out, breathed in thin, thin. The lantern paused behind the house, then swung off again. He didn't know how many watches he sat there, but the bucket under his heart that had been stuck all summer suddenly went *clunk* and moved down by itself: that thread of warmth sank past his heart, sank to his navel, and settled there, steady. Further than that, there was nothing.

Early the next morning his mother drew a fourth circle round "Down" and laid the account book open on the bed table for the ink to dry. His father dragged his leg over and looked down at it for a long time. Of those ten characters, he only knew this one: down the mountain, down the slope, down under the tree, the "down" in setting a snare down. He pressed a thick finger beside the new circle, careful not to touch the ink, and asked, "Sunk down, has it?" Xiaoman said, "Mm. It's sunk down." His father said, "Then it's steady."

Those few days, Beansprout Liu went to herd sheep for the Mas — old Liu's family owed the Mas two pecks of rice, and with interest on interest they couldn't pay it back. He drove the sheep past the Shens' door and didn't look up.

Towards evening Ahe stopped Xiaoman by the well platform. "How many days to the Si River?" "Three." "How many flatbreads a day?" "One." Ahe took a stick of charcoal from behind her ear, wrote something in a little notebook with a blue cloth cover, turned and left. Xiaoman had never seen that notebook before.

---

On the night of the fourth of the ninth month his father sat on the edge of the bed tapping the bed table with a finger, tap, tap, for a long time. Then he stood up, limped over to the wall, took down the old bow wrapped in ox sinew, and set it in front of his son. "Your grandpa's. You take it." Xiaoman said, "…Pa, that sounds like swearing." "Don't talk nonsense." His father pushed the bow into his arms. "Your grandpa's bow."

His father said, "When you get to Sunken Cauldron Mountain, the stone steps are three hundred in one stick of incense. Your uncle got to a hundred and seventy — he says." He taught Xiaoman: breathe in for three steps, breathe out for three steps; when you breathe in, the breath sinks down; when you breathe out, your feet carry you forward; don't look at the top of the slope, only at the step under your feet. "Chasing a rabbit is about speed. Chasing a deer is about steadiness. Every road up a mountain gets walked that way." He paused, then said, "From now on, when you draw a bow on your own, there'll be nobody behind you saying first read the wind. You'll have to say it yourself."

His father went on, "When you get to the immortals' sect, keep their rules. Be honest. Don't fight with people—" His mother said, "Husband." His father stopped.

His mother took out a sewing kit from inside her jacket: a thimble, three needles, two reels of thread, one black, one white. And a purse of blue cloth, its stitches fine and close, with a red cord threaded through the mouth. The purse was empty. "Needle and thread: when your clothes tear, mend them yourself." Other mothers, as the old poem has it, sew close stitches before a son sets out; his mother gave him the needle. She tapped the purse with a finger. "The purse is empty. You fill it yourself."

She looked at him and went on, "What your father says, listen to half of it. Keep the rules — right. Be honest — honest with good people. Don't fight with people—" Here she stopped, reached out, turned his collar down properly and smoothed it flat, and only then said:

"Fight when you can win."

The flame of the oil lamp jumped. Her hand, once it had finished turning down his collar, rested on his shoulder for a moment.

---

The night before he left, Xiaoman went to the east end of the village. Granny Wu was sitting on her doorstep as usual. Before he could open his mouth she said, "Off, then." Xiaoman sat down beside the doorstep and waited for her to say the nine things that were nonsense, so he could fish out the one that could save his life and take it with him. But Granny Wu said, "Granny won't talk nonsense today. Just one thing. When you're hungry, eat. When you're full, put it down." Xiaoman waited a while. "…That's all?" "That's all. Off you go. Your ma's making flatbread." He'd gone a few steps when Granny Wu said behind him, "That tablet — keep it against your skin. Don't let it get cold." He looked back. She grinned, showing her three teeth. "Granny's three teeth are waiting for something. Once it comes, they'll be willing to fall out." And with that she turned her face to the west and paid him no more attention.

It was his last night on the woodpile. He sat for two hours and did one count: ninety-six. The goose feather trembled under his nose, trembled very slightly, like what's left on the tip of a blade of grass after the wind has gone. It didn't stop. A hundred and twenty: still twenty-four short.

---

Before he left, his mother pushed a cloth bundle into his hands with four flatbreads in it, made the night before. Xiaoman had woken in the middle of the night and seen the oil lamp lit in the kitchen — for the first time the wick hadn't been pinched short, and the flame was as big as a finger — and his mother standing at the stove making them one by one. "For the road. One a day, until the Si River." Xiaoman counted. "Ma, it's three days to the Si River. That's one too many." His mother said, "If you can't beat it, run. When running makes you hungry, eat that one." She pushed a little notebook into his hands as well. She'd bound it from the wrapping paper of the brown sugar Uncle had brought, and the cover still had a faint sweet smell. "Going into an immortals' gate on your own, you can't go without an account book. It's empty. Keep it yourself." An empty purse, an empty notebook. Xiaoman put the goose feather between the pages of the notebook, on the first page.

The day he left was the sixth of the ninth month, before it was light. Uncle had taken seven days' leave and arrived the night before, to see him as far as the Si River; his father would see him as far as the county town. His mother didn't see him off. She said someone had to stay home and mind the stove. She stood under the old locust tree, hands tucked into her sleeves, perfectly still, the way she had last autumn. Xiaoman, with the bow on his back and the needle and thread, the empty purse, the empty notebook and four flatbreads inside his jacket, looked back as he passed the old locust tree. One of his mother's hands lifted out of her sleeve and stopped in mid-air. It didn't wave, and it didn't go back down. That day the bowl broke, when his mother reached out to catch it, her hand had stopped in mid-air just like that.

He'd thought Ahe would be there. She wasn't. On the sheep slope, Beansprout Liu, driving the Mas' sheep, raised his whip to him from a long way off.

He turned and walked out of the village after his father and Uncle. Three days left till the ninth of the ninth month. Half a mile out from the village gate the sun came up, and he suddenly felt that the tablet on his chest was warm. In the daytime it was always cool, and his two hands had been tucked into his sleeves the whole time; he hadn't touched it once. He stopped, pressed it through his clothes, and for the first time a thought came up in him:

…Who's been keeping it warm?
