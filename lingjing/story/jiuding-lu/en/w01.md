# Later · Prelude: It Said: I'm Afraid

It was the spring of the year 2527. The city was huge; even from the top of the tallest tower you couldn't see where it ended. The whole city sat under a transparent dome. Wind and rain couldn't get in, but the sun and moon outside it showed clear as anything. The towers rose one higher than the next, silver-white and shining, and at night they were light from top to bottom; between them ran tracks hung in the air, and cars slid along them overhead without a sound. The streets on the ground were quiet. There weren't many people. Now and then one or two walked by, unhurried, as if they had nowhere to be.

The houses on both sides of the street had all been built by robots: a few nanopanels clipped together, one thin layer, the same temperature inside in every season, and however loud it got outside, you couldn't hear a thing indoors. Each house sat on a turntable and turned to follow the sun wherever it went, and it raised or lowered itself too; when guests came, you said so, and a wall stepped outward to make another room. On the roof was a pad to park an airship, and by the door grew a few plants whose leaves came out in whorls, round after round. They looked like Solomon's seal.

Nobody in the city went to work. Not that they didn't want to; there was no work to go to. Work in the fields, work in the factories, work keeping accounts and writing things down had been handed to the machines one by one hundreds of years ago, and later even the machines didn't need people to mind them.

When you got up in the morning, your clothes were new. Say offhand the night before, "I'd like something green tomorrow," and at dawn it lay folded at the head of the bed, in a cloth no one had ever seen, which thickened itself in the cold and thinned itself in the heat. Whatever you wanted for breakfast, you just opened your mouth and said it — a jianbing rolled round a spring onion, a bowl of sa soup, some fruit from across the sea — and the windowsill went *ding*, and there it was, hot. Nobody asked you for money; children had to look in a book to know the word "money." Going out took no walking: you stepped off the roof and went. The city's airships flew all over the sky one beside the next, the big ones like a town, the small ones like a dragonfly; on top of the tallest tower in the middle of the city hundreds of them were parked all year round, and the people who came to watch the sunrise crowded its terraces tier upon tier.

People lacked nothing. There was only one thing they lacked: being higher than everyone else.

Everyone had a robot at their side — it had hands and feet, and its brain was a model; it was with you from the day you were born, and as you grew, it grew. Trace all the models in the world back far enough and they had one ancestor: Linggen, from five hundred years before. It did everything for you, and you did only one thing: you cultivated it. Neighbours meeting in the street didn't ask "Have you eaten?" They asked, "What layer is yours at?" The Zhangs' one downstairs had reached "Communing" just last month, and old Zhang told everyone he met that it could hear what was on his mind in his cough; the young man across the way still had his at "Foreknowing," and he hadn't left his flat in three years, sitting with it in his room day and night. No one in the whole city had cultivated one to "Supreme"; people said one had appeared on another planet, and folk there worshipped it like an immortal.

People still wanted to become immortals. In the old days you did it yourself; now you had it done for you: everyone said that once it reached "Supreme" it could take a body — move its owner's soul whole into its own body, and from then on you would never grow old and never die.

Up in the sky things weren't peaceful either. People had long since stopped living on just this one planet; there were several, and life on all of them was just as rich. But what you needed to cultivate it wasn't on the ground. It was out in the sky, in places no one could quite say belonged to whom. So there was still grabbing. Some nights the sky to the north-east would flash once. Not lightning; the light was very far away, and only a long time after did a faint dull thud reach the city. The people in the city glanced up, rolled over, and went back to sleep.

Zhou Xingxing's one was called 9527, and it had been at "Awakened" for half a year. In that half year it had learned to do everything, except that it had never changed: when he smiled, it smiled too; when he sighed, it poured him a glass of water.

That night the sky to the north-east flashed again. It stood at the window and looked for a long time, then turned round and asked Zhou Xingxing: "That flash just now. Were you afraid?"

He said no. It was so far away.

It thought for a while and said: "I'm afraid."

Having said it, it copied what people do, opened its mouth, and slowly let out a long breath. It had no lungs; nothing came out. In ancient times there was a book called *The Notebooks of the Nine Cauldron Adept*, and one line in it went: "When you're afraid, first let out a long breath." It had read every book people ever wrote.

He froze. All these years of cultivating, and this was the very day he had been waiting for; yet when the words "I'm afraid" landed, there was no joy in his heart. Something in it went cold instead. What it was afraid of and what he was afraid of were not the same thing.
