# Chapter Nine: The Shaman Goes into the River with a Message / A Thousand Fish Raise a Dragon Gate from the Zhang

On the twenty-eighth of the ninth month, before it was light, Zhou Heng was already waiting at the gate of the Sunken Cauldron Abbey. He had that ledger thicker than a pillow on his back, and on his feet a pair of new cloth shoes whose soles were still white, as though he had been waiting for this trip for a great many years. The first thing he said when he saw Xiaoman was: "Travel money, three hundred cash a head. I'll pay it for now. We'll put it on the books when we get back."

Twenty days to go until the eighteenth of the tenth month.

They were setting out twenty-odd days late because Zhou Heng had to hand over eighteen years of the herb garden's accounts in proper order, book by book, and the handing over took twenty days. Xiaoman asked him why he was being so thorough about it, and he said, "In case I don't come back." Then he added, "…Someone has to keep the books going."

As they went down the three hundred stone steps one by one, Xiaoman asked, "Senior brother Zhou, what does the River Lord want a wife for?" Zhou Heng opened his ledger and said, "Every year, when Ye marries a bride to the River Lord, it collects 'River Lord money,' three hundred cash a household. Ye has three thousand households. Nine hundred thousand cash." He shut the ledger and looked at Xiaoman. "You tell me. What does the River Lord buy with nine hundred thousand cash?"

Xiaoman thought for a moment and said, "…A wife?" Zhou Heng didn't answer. He took a blue cloth purse from inside his robe, weighed it in his hand, and said, "The abbey gave us another five taels of silver, expenses for checking the accounts. These five taels are not to be touched." With that he tucked it away against his skin and pressed it three times from the outside.

At the foot of the steps someone was squatting: two plaits that hung past her waist, a red nose, and on her back a bundle bigger than she was. Xiaoman said, "…What are you doing here?" Ahe stood up, brushed the dirt off her backside and said, "My interest is owed by you. Wherever you go, I go."

Zhou Heng said, "A personal disciple leaving the mountain without leave — the Abbot will want to know." Ahe said, "The Abbot already knows." She brought out a slip of paper. "The Abbot signed it." Zhou Heng took the slip and turned it over and over for a long time. There was only one word on it: **Go.** Both his eyebrows jumped up at once, so high they nearly flew off his forehead, and he said, "…The Abbot never signs slips." Ahe said, without any expression at all, "He did today."

She went to Xiaoman's side and said, low, "Last month. I reached Middle Attainment." Xiaoman opened his mouth, but she had already walked on ahead.

At the first meal stop on the first day Zhou Heng put on a stern face and said, "Martial Aunt Ahe's travel money is not on the abbey's books." Ahe said, "Put it on mine." She took out her little notebook and opened it to show him, dense with writing. Zhou Heng turned a few pages, raised his eyebrows twice, and said very gravely, "…You keep it more carefully than I do." From then on there were two sets of books on the road; and every herb the little zheng dug up along the way, Zhou Heng entered in the ledger, one by one.

The night before they set out, after the sword test, Elder Qu had called him into the thatched hut and taught him just sixteen characters: "[The Way of looking within: still the spirit, settle the heart. Stray thoughts do not rise; nothing crooked gets in]{典=neiguan-jing}." He added, "Once you've laid your foundation, look inside." He paused. "When your heart's in a panic, one breath does the work of half; when your heart's still, one breath does the work of two." Then he chased him off down the mountain to sleep. At the time Xiaoman had thought it rather little. On this road, when they stopped for the night, he would close his eyes and look inside, and however long he looked, all he saw was the lake in his belly and a thin little channel at its edge.

There was just one thing Xiaoman found odd. Zhou Heng said he had never in his life been outside Xu Province, and yet at a fork in the road he never asked the way. Which road was shorter, which ferryman wouldn't fleece you, which inn's flatbreads were a day old — he knew them all. Xiaoman asked, "Senior brother Zhou, have you walked this road before?" Zhou Heng didn't even look up. "I've seen it in the accounts."

So Xiaoman didn't ask again. He had seen a fair few accounts in his time, and he had never seen one that recorded which inn's flatbreads were a day old.

---

On the tenth day, the evening of the seventh of the tenth month, the three of them reached the bank of the Zhang. The Zhang was wider than the Si River and muddier too, yellow with green in it, and you couldn't see the bottom; its surface lay so flat you could hardly tell it was flowing, only now and then a whirlpool turned over, spun once, and sank again. On the far bank was Ye, its city wall a dusty grey that in the setting sun looked just like an old brick left soaking at the water's edge for years. On the river side a water gate opened in the wall, with a gate tower on top, and from its eaves hung a string of wind chimes that tinkled when the wind blew, loud enough to hear across the whole river.

Xiaoman tilted his head and listened for a while. Under the sound of the water lay another sound too, very slow and very deep, the same as the one under the Si, only sleeping more soundly.

Ahe said "Wah," the little zheng let out a howl at the river, and Xiaoman wanted to say something too, but when he turned round he saw Zhou Heng standing on the bank, not moving. All along the road he had written down every single plant, and now his ledger was shut and he wrote nothing at all. He just stood there looking at the deepest, blackest stretch of water in the middle of the river, and went on looking until the sun had sunk into it.

Xiaoman said, "Senior brother Zhou?" Zhou Heng said, "Martial Uncle Shen, I heard it." It was a good while longer before he turned round and said, "Let's go. After dark the ferry costs extra." This time, of all things, he didn't say "put it on the books."

Across the ferry, Ye was no small city, yet there was hardly anyone in the streets. Not that there was no one; everyone was shrinking back. Every door was shut, pair after pair of eyes showed through the cracks, and on seeing three strangers, pair after pair drew back again.

The three of them found an inn in the east of the city. The innkeeper was a fat man; he brought out three bowls of noodles whose broth was so clear you could see your face in it. Zhou Heng lifted his bowl and held it up to the lamp. "Innkeeper, business bad?" The innkeeper sighed. "Well, it's autumn. Come autumn, who's got money for noodles?" Zhou Heng said, "What about autumn?" The innkeeper glanced out of the door, looked at them again, and said, low, "…Come autumn, the River Lord takes a wife. The eighteenth of the tenth month."

The moment those words were out, Xiaoman's hand stopped on his bowl. He didn't look at Zhou Heng. That line from New Year's Eve had only now got its second half.

It turned out that this custom of Ye's had been going on for twenty-five years. Every year when autumn came, the Three Elders and the [shaman]{注=wuzhu} of the River Lord's temple went from door to door collecting "River Lord money," three hundred cash a household; then the shaman went from door to door again to look, and whichever family's girl she picked, if they had money they paid to buy her off, and if they didn't —

The innkeeper said, "…the girl gets dressed up, sat in a wooden boat, and pushed out into the middle of the Zhang. At first the boat floats. It floats and floats, and then it sinks." He went on, "Any family that goes downstream to fish her out is fighting the River Lord for his bride, and the whole family gets buried with her. Twenty-five years. Nobody's dared go looking."

The inn went quiet at once. All you could hear was a small clatter: Ahe's chopsticks had dropped on the table. Zhou Heng held his bowl, the broth in it rocking ring after ring, and didn't drink a mouthful.

Xiaoman asked, "Who's this year's bride?" The innkeeper was silent for a good while before he said, "…The girl from Old Hu's place, sells tofu in the west of town. The neighbours call her the [Tofu Beauty]{注=doufu-xishi}. Mute, and pretty. The day before yesterday her father went to the Three Elders and knelt there a day and a night. Said he didn't want the tofu shop, didn't want the house, they could take anything, just give him back his girl." At this point he looked down at his apron. "The Three Elders said the River Lord wants a person, not tofu."

When the innkeeper had finished, he picked up his tray to go, took two steps, then turned back and looked at Zhou Heng. "Sir, from your accent… you're from round here?" Zhou Heng said, "I'm from Xu Province." The innkeeper said, "Oh," and then, "My mistake, then." And he went off.

That night Xiaoman came downstairs for some water and found Zhou Heng asleep with his head on a table in the main room, the old ledger under his arm, open at the same page as ever. Xiaoman laid his own outer jacket over his back. Zhou Heng woke, shut the old ledger first and pushed it inside his robe, and said, "What are you looking at?" Xiaoman said, "I didn't look this time." Zhou Heng gave him back the jacket and went upstairs. Outside the window, down by the river in the west of the city, someone was faintly hammering at something, one blow, then another, half the night through. Only later did he find out that it was in front of the River Lord's temple, where someone was painting and nailing a boat through the night.

---

Early next morning the three of them went to see the Three Elders. Xiaoman's idea was to sound them out first: if what the Three Elders and the shaman were after was money, then give them money, push an empty boat into the water this year, and that would be the end of it. Zhou Heng's hand was pressed on the blue cloth purse inside his robe, pressed down hard. "These are the abbey's expenses for checking the accounts." Xiaoman said, "Senior brother Zhou, checking accounts is what we're here for. Money spent on the accounts is checking the accounts too." Zhou Heng thought about it for a long time, found nothing to say against it, and could only say, "…Put it on the books."

The Three Elders lived in a big three-courtyard house in the north of the city. They were three old men: one fat, one thin, one deaf. Zhou Heng took out three pieces of broken silver, a tael each, and set them on the table one at a time, and with each one his face went a shade whiter. Xiaoman, all smiles, said that this year the boat would go into the water as usual, only no one would be put in it, and this bit of tea money was by way of a respectful offering to his lordship the River Lord. The fat old man swept the three pieces of silver into his sleeve, very practised at it, and said, "Young man, your tea money we'll take. As for the girl, she goes in just the same." The thin old man said, unhurried, "Young man, do you think this is a matter of a few taels?" Xiaoman said, "Then what is it a matter of?"

The deaf old man had at last caught one sentence, and bellowed in a voice like a great bell: "A few taels? They give us ten ingots a year —"

That shout fell into a dead hush. The fat old man clapped a hand over the deaf one's mouth, and all the blood drained from the thin one's face in an instant.

Xiaoman said, "…They? Who's they?" The fat old man laughed. "You heard wrong, young man. He's hard of hearing, talks nonsense." The thin old man lowered his voice and said, word by word, "Young man, what happens in this city has someone up above it. You're an outsider, you don't know what's what. If word of this gets out —" he held up three fingers and pointed them at the three of them, "next year the River Lord's brides will be us three."

Before he had finished, Zhou Heng was already beside the table. On a corner of it stood a redwood casket with its lid open a crack, and inside were stacked several ingots of silver, gleaming white, each a full ten taels. Zhou Heng's eyes fell on the silver and stayed there. He was a man who had kept accounts all his life; when other people looked at silver they looked at how much there was, and when he looked at silver he looked at the bottom. While the Three Elders weren't paying attention he reached out, picked up an ingot, turned it over for a look and put it back, with no expression on his face at all.

Xiaoman, standing behind him, saw it too in the instant the ingot turned over. On its upturned base was chiselled a circle, and inside the circle one horizontal stroke. Inside his sleeve, his hand slowly closed into a fist.

Out of the Three Elders' gate, Xiaoman sighed. "Senior brother Zhou, those five taels…" Zhou Heng said, "Three taels. Not refunded."

He frowned. "You saw the bottom of that ingot too. Official silver is stamped with the government mark; the Qian house stamps a 'Qian.' That circle — I've kept accounts for nearly twenty years and never seen it." Then: "Ten ingots a year, ten taels an ingot. Twenty-five years, two thousand five hundred taels." And last: "The River Lord money is paid by the people of Ye themselves. These two thousand five hundred taels are sent in from outside."

Xiaoman said, "So that means… this match, the River Lord taking a wife, is something somebody paid to buy?" Zhou Heng didn't answer.

That night Xiaoman sat under the oil lamp half the night. The ingot in the stonemason's palm on the Si River wharf and the ingot in the Three Elders' casket bore one mark. Who stood behind that man in white, he didn't know.

---

For days on end Xiaoman wandered all over the city, looking for some other way. One day he wandered into the west of town and found the tofu shop.

The tofu shop had long since closed its door. On the door was pasted a sheet of yellow paper, stamped with the red seal of the River Lord's temple: this family, the River Lord has chosen.

She sat on the doorstep grinding beans, seventeen or eighteen, her face steamed pink and white by the heat of the mill room, sleeves rolled to the elbow, her wrists as slender as two stalks of spring onion. The stone mill went round and round, and the soy milk ran down drop by drop into the wooden bucket below. She ground very slowly, as if she were grinding something that would take a whole lifetime. When she saw Xiaoman she stopped. She didn't speak — she couldn't — she just looked at him, pointed at the soy milk in the bucket, then pointed at him, which meant she was inviting him to drink.

Xiaoman took a bowl of soy milk. It was warm and very sweet. Standing in the doorway watching her grind, he caught sight of a row of short strokes drawn in charcoal on the door frame; all the ones in front had been crossed out, and only the last five were left. He reckoned it up in his head: today was the thirteenth of the tenth month, and five days to the eighteenth exactly. Against the wall just inside the door, dried tofu was already stacked half as high as a man, enough to feed one person through a winter — with five days still left, she was grinding all the beans of the days to come, for her father.

A man came out of the inner room, fifty or so, broad in the shoulders and thick in the waist, bean pulp on both forearms, but he walked with a limp. Xiaoman knew that walk at a glance: a man with bad knees presses down with his palm before he steps, and only once the knee is held does he dare put his weight on it. His father walked like that too.

Old Hu looked at him and said, "Stranger? You've had your soy milk, so go. This street's unlucky these days." Xiaoman didn't go. He asked, "Your leg, uncle…" Old Hu said, "From kneeling at the Three Elders' door. A day and a night. Those flagstones are hard." He sat down on the doorstep beside his daughter and reached out to tuck a lock of hair from her forehead behind her ear. "When she was three she had a fever. When it went down, she couldn't talk any more. Her mother died young."

He looked out at the street and went on, "Twenty-five years, and I never once missed the River Lord money. The year before last I couldn't pay, so I handed over the pair of silver earrings her mother left. The year the oil-seller's girl from East Street went on the boat, I stood on the bank with my carrying pole and watched, and I didn't make a sound. What I thought was: whoever the River Lord wants, it'll always be somebody else's." He stopped there and said no more. His daughter stopped grinding and looked at him, then lowered her head and went on grinding.

Xiaoman couldn't help saying to her, "…Aren't you afraid?"

She stopped, thought for a moment, dipped her finger in the soy milk and wrote a few words on the millstone, very neatly:

**Pa can't turn the mill on his own.**

Old Hu looked at the words and said nothing. The hand he had pressed on his knee never stopped shaking.

Xiaoman looked at the words and didn't move for a long time. A breath of wind came by, the soy milk on the millstone slowly dried, and the words faded. In the wooden tablet at his chest a very sleepy, very fierce voice said, "…What are you standing there gawping for? Come the eighteenth, this girl gets pushed into the river. If you want to save her, go and think of something now."

---

The Three Elders were a dead end, so Xiaoman decided to have a look at the River Lord's temple. If someone outside was sending in ten ingots of silver a year, someone in the city had to be receiving them, and whoever received the silver, if not the Three Elders, it was that shaman in the temple. On the night of the fourteenth of the tenth month, at the third watch, he left the inn alone and headed for the river in the west of the city.

The River Lord's temple stood by the river in the west of the city, its door pulled to but not shut, two big red candles burning in the main hall. Xiaoman was still a hundred-odd paces from the door when he heard someone speaking inside. Since laying his foundation his ears were far sharper than before, and even at that distance he could hear every word in the hall. He went no further but slipped behind an old willow on the embankment, drew in his breath, and looked through the crack of the half-shut door into the hall.

The one speaking was the shaman. She knelt on the floor, her red clothes all crumpled from kneeling, forehead pressed to the flagstones, her voice shaking badly: "…Rest easy, Immortal. On the eighteenth, same as always." Before her stood a man in white, with his back to the door so that his face couldn't be seen, tall and thin, standing between the two red candles; the candlelight didn't reach him, as if it didn't dare. The man in white said, "Last year the Zhang rose three feet. This year he ought not to be able to hold back."

His voice wasn't loud, but it was cold and deep, as if it came up from the bottom of an old well. Xiaoman knew that voice: five years before at the Si River ferry, the one who had snapped his ribs with one finger was this white-robed steward of the Taiyi Palace. What the man's name was he didn't know; in his own head he had always called him Old Well.

Behind the willow, all the blood in Xiaoman's body ran cold at once. He didn't dare move one more step: Old Well's cultivation was a long way above his, and if he crept up under the window, one sweep of Old Well's spirit sense would sweep him out.

The shaman kowtowed again and again. "Yes, yes." Old Well went on, "When he comes up, none of you is to stand in his way." With that he flicked his sleeve, and there on the table was a heavy cloth bag; it landed with a clank. Silver.

Old Well turned and walked out. Xiaoman pressed himself against the willow, not daring even to breathe. He watched him step out of the temple door without a sound underfoot; the moonlight fell on him as though it were falling into water, there was a ripple, and the man was gone. Xiaoman stayed crouched behind the willow for as long as it takes to eat a meal, his nails dug into his palms without his even noticing.

In the wooden tablet Yinyue said, low, "Old Well's gone. There's only one mortal old woman left in the hall. This queen will teach you a soul-search. Go in and press your hand on the crown of her head, and everything in her belly is yours." Xiaoman said, "And after it, what happens to her?" Yinyue said, "After it, she's a halfwit." Xiaoman said, "That won't do. She has to stay alive and well and say all this herself, in front of the whole city. Besides, Old Well hasn't gone far. The moment there's any magic in that hall, he'll know." Yinyue said "Hmph," and said no more.

Xiaoman was about to get up when someone behind him caught the hem of his robe and tugged it lightly, twice. He looked round. It was Ahe. She was crouching in the willow's shadow, with no lamp, and without a word she pointed towards the embankment, then drew a hand across the back of her own neck. Xiaoman understood: Old Well's spirit sense was sweeping back this way towards the temple.

The two of them kept close to the foot of the wall and backed out of that street step by step. Only when they were three streets away did Ahe speak: "I've followed you all the way, keeping watch behind you." Xiaoman said, "The man in white in the temple, I know him. Five years ago at the Si River ferry, he's the one who snapped my ribs with one finger. I don't know his name. In my head I've always called him Old Well; that voice of his sounds like it comes up from the bottom of a well." Ahe said nothing more. The night wind blew in off the Zhang, chilling them to the bone.

Back at the inn, Zhou Heng was sitting in the main room waiting. The three of them went upstairs, shut the door, and laid out that night's business on the table.

Zhou Heng couldn't fight; all he could do was reckon accounts. He reckoned this one first: "This Old Well is at Great Attainment of Foundation Establishment. Martial Uncle Shen is Lesser Attainment, perfected, two steps below him; Martial Aunt Ahe is Middle Attainment, one step below. Two people each one step below, fighting one together, would stand a chance. As things are, there's no fight to be had." Xiaoman said, "Five years ago, when he hit me with that finger, I didn't even see how his hand came up." Ahe said, "We can't beat him head on. First work out what he wants."

What he wanted, the two sentences in the temple had already half said. Ahe went over them again. "He said 'This year he ought not to be able to hold back,' and he said 'When he comes up, none of you is to stand in his way.' This 'he' is in the river. Every year they push a girl into the river, twenty-five years of it, and it's to drive that 'he' in the river up. On the eighteenth, the moment the bride goes into the water is the very moment he's been waiting for." Xiaoman said, "Then don't let the bride go into the water." Ahe said, "Right. But we can't stop it with magic. There'll be thousands of people standing by the river, and the moment we use magic there's spiritual light on us; wherever he's standing he'll see it. Then he won't wait for the one in the river, he'll deal with us first. It has to be done the mortal way, so the wedding falls through by itself."

Zhou Heng said, "The Three Elders took our silver today, and they're already suspicious. Ye has three inns in all, and only this one has outsiders staying. From tomorrow there'll be people watching that door." Ahe said, "The people who come to watch will be mortals. Let them watch."

Xiaoman said, "Even if the wedding falls through, Old Well will make his move sooner or later." Ahe said, "So these next few days, we each get ready in our own way. When the moment comes and he moves, this stretch of riverbank has to be ours, not his."

Early next morning, sure enough, two idlers had appeared across from the inn, squatting there to sun themselves, though their eyes never turned towards the sun, only towards the inn door.

For the next three days Ahe went out first thing every morning and came back only after dark. She walked along the bank of the Zhang, and at the foot of each old willow she would crouch, take a short length of green vine from her sleeve, bury it beside the roots, make a hand-sign, and get up and go on to the next tree. Of the row of old willows in front of the River Lord's temple, she didn't miss a single one. The two idlers watching followed her for half a day, took this stranger girl to be digging wild greens by the river, and after that couldn't be bothered to follow.

Every evening Xiaoman went and walked the riverbank. How wide the stretch of bank in front of the River Lord's temple was, where the sand was firm and where it was soft mud, which willow stood nearest the water — he measured it all, step by step, and in his head he laid it out in nine squares after the nine numbers his mother had taught him, until he knew with his eyes shut where every step should land. At night, lying in bed, he went over that finger at the ferry five years before, from start to finish, again and again. That time he had seen nothing. But his master had taught him: when a man sends all the strength in his body to one place, everywhere else goes thin. When Old Well struck again, he wouldn't watch the finger. What he would watch for was where else had gone thin.

---

The charcoal strokes on the tofu shop's door frame were crossed out one a day. On the night of the seventeenth of the tenth month there was only the last one left. Xiaoman sat on the well platform in the inn's back yard, reckoning tomorrow's business item by item.

For Old Well, everything that could be got ready had been got ready; the rest he would only find out when the time came. Old Well wanted "him" to come up — who was "he"? That account was too big; he couldn't reckon it. His mother said that an account you can't reckon, you set aside for a while, and first reckon what's in front of you. What was in front of him came to just one sum: tomorrow at noon, the Tofu Beauty would sit down in that wooden boat with holes chiselled in it. The wedding had to fall through by itself, and he couldn't use magic.

The shaman said she could talk to the River Lord. The Three Elders said they did the River Lord's business. Good. Then let her go and talk, and let them go and do it.

In the wooden tablet at his chest, a very sleepy voice said, "…Thought it through?" He said, "Thought it through." The voice said, "Then go." And then: "Don't take a second step into the river."

The back-yard gate creaked. Ahe came in with an oil lamp in one hand and her little notebook in the other, wrapped in a blue cloth cloak right up to her chin. These past few days, as she went along the bank burying vines, she had picked up a set of accounts on the way. She set the lamp down on the well platform, sat beside him and said, "You first."

So Xiaoman told her. When he had finished, Ahe said nothing for a long while, and at last said, "What are you going as?" Xiaoman said, "The drummer." The drummer in the bridal procession was an old drummer from the south of town, fond of his cup; he'd asked around.

Ahe said, "You throw the shaman in the river, and the thousands of people on the bank just think some mad drummer's turned up. The people in this city believe in the River Lord. They've believed for twenty-five years. They won't believe you." She opened the little notebook and set it under the lamp. "This is what they have to believe."

Xiaoman leaned over to look. It was a page of accounts. The money-in side Zhou Heng had worked out back on the stone steps: three hundred cash a household, three thousand households, nine hundred strings a year. The money-out side was what Ahe had asked around for these past few days.

The one who had hammered half the night in front of the River Lord's temple that first night was Boatwright Liu from the west of town. Early next morning Ahe had gone to his boatyard and crouched at the side watching him plane boards half the day, watched till noon, and only then asked him one thing: with the bottom nailed so tight, would the boat float all the way to the far bank once it was in the water? Boatwright Liu's plane stopped. Twenty-five years, twenty-five boats, every one of them made by him. He put his plane down, went inside, pulled a work ledger out from under his bed boards and spread it in front of her, as if he had been holding it in for twenty-five years and someone had finally come to ask. One entry a year, not one missed: **One River Lord boat, one string. Seven holes chiselled in the bottom, two hundred cash extra. Paid by the Three Elders.**

Xiaoman said, "What did he write that down for?" Ahe said, "Fear. Afraid the River Lord would come to his door one day, and then he could bring it out and show that the Three Elders told him to chisel the holes, not him."

The bridal dress, the incense and candles, the drums and pipes — she had asked at the shops about those too, and all together they came to less than four strings a year. She tapped the page with her finger and read it out: "River Lord money, nine hundred strings taken in a year. Boat, bridal dress, incense and candles, drums and pipes, five strings spent a year. The other eight hundred and ninety-five strings, every year, nobody knows where they went." She paused. "The roof of the River Lord's temple has leaked for three years and nobody's mended it."

Xiaoman said, "The rest — who took how much?" Ahe said, "Don't know. The Three Elders' accounts are in the Three Elders' houses." Xiaoman said, "Then we go to their houses —" Ahe said, "Go into someone's house and you're a thief. Who believes accounts read out by a thief?"

Xiaoman went over it in his head; not one number was wrong. He said, "How much River Lord money came in, how much went out, who had the holes chiselled in the boats — it's all here." Ahe said, "It's here." She shut the notebook. "But accounts are dead things, and these are numbers I counted up. Thousands of people listening to some stranger girl read out figures — when I'm done, the Three Elders say 'made-up sums,' and the sums are made up. Who took how much, only the people who split the money know." She looked at him. "You send the shaman down there, someone has to pull her out alive. Only if she opens her mouth once, in front of the whole city, are these accounts real."

Xiaoman said, "I'll count to thirty, then go down and pull her out." Ahe didn't say yes, and didn't say no.

She stood up and said, "One more thing."

Xiaoman looked up at her. She said, "If that mouth of yours doesn't work, I'll get in the boat in her place."

And she undid her cloak.

Under the cloak was a bride's dress, all red. Gold thread at the cuffs, a ring of twining lotus embroidered round the hem, half an inch too big, cinched at the waist with a cloth sash. The oil lamp sat right there on the well platform; when the flame leapt, all that red leapt with it, and the glow reached up to her face.

Xiaoman's mouth hung open. Not a word came out.

Ahe looked down and tugged at a cuff and said, perfectly seriously, "The innkeeper's wife kept it at the bottom of her chest. Borrowed. Thirty cash, put it on my books." Then: "Once the boat's pushed out into the middle, nobody on the bank dares look — twenty-five years, nobody's dared take one look downstream. I swim over underwater, sit in the boat in her place, and send her off downstream to under the willows with a vine. The bride's mute; with the red veil over her face, nobody can hear that it's a different person. Seven holes in the bottom of the boat — my vines can bind them. It won't sink." She paused. "Once I've floated somewhere no one can see, I'll row myself back."

Xiaoman said, "Old Well's in the city. You, alone, floating in the middle of the river — what if he comes for you?" Ahe said, "So we settle it now. Tomorrow, if that mouth of yours works, the bride doesn't have to go into the water, I don't have to get into the boat in her place, and this dress was borrowed for nothing. Thirty cash wasted."

She laid the cloak over the well rail, stood up straight, hands hanging at her sides, chin up. "Tomorrow you have to say that line in front of thousands of people. When you say it there can't be the least bit of doubt on your face; the least bit, and the shaman will see it. Practise it once on me first."

Xiaoman said, "Which line?"

Ahe said, "This bride isn't pretty enough."

Xiaoman opened his mouth.

The oil lamp on the well platform crackled at the wick. She stood right in front of the lamp, her two plaits hanging down over all that red, her lips pressed together under the red nose, waiting for him. The dress had been made for someone a little plumper than her and sagged a little at the shoulders, but she stood so straight that she filled it out. Looking at her, Xiaoman only now noticed that the yellow-haired little girl who had stopped him by the well that year and asked how many days it was to the Si River had, from some year or other, grown into this: an oval face, fine curved brows, a pair of almond eyes clear and bright; on ordinary days she kept a straight face doing sums, and now with her eyes lowered there was a hint of a smile hidden under her lashes. When the flame leapt, the flush on her face — he couldn't tell whether the dress had cast it there or it was her own.

Xiaoman was struck dumb staring at her. His mouth hung open, and after a good while what came out of it was: "This bride… is too pretty…"

The moment the words were out, he froze himself, his eyes still fixed straight on her. He couldn't move them away.

Ahe's face went red all at once, right up to the roots of her ears. She waited a moment, and seeing him still standing there dazed, said, "…You said it backwards. What you were meant to say was 'not pretty enough.'"

Only then did Xiaoman come to himself and hurriedly move his eyes into the well. In the well there was a lamp too, and a spot of red. He said, "…I'll say it tomorrow."

Ahe looked at him for a while, said nothing, took the cloak off the well rail, wrapped it back around herself right up to her chin, and picked up the lamp. At the back-yard gate she turned round and said, "One line, 'this bride isn't pretty enough,' and practising it on me you go and turn it into 'too pretty.' Tomorrow in front of thousands of people, don't you say it backwards again. That one's charged extra interest."

The gate creaked shut. Xiaoman sat on the well platform a long time, until the spot of red in the well was gone too.

---

The eighteenth of the tenth month: the River Lord takes a wife. From early that morning the bank of the Zhang was packed with people. The Three Elders sat under a big parasol in new silk gowns, each with a plate of fruit before him. The shaman was in bright red, her head stuck all over with chicken feathers, a bronze bell in her hand. The Tofu Beauty, in the bride's dress, sat in the wooden boat; beside the boat was the bridal procession, piping and drumming, for all the world as if this were a good match. Her father hadn't come. The neighbours said Old Hu was shut up at home, and the Three Elders had sent two men to guard the door.

The drummer was Shen Xiaoman. The old drummer had taken thirty cash from him and was lying at the inn pretending to be ill — Xiaoman paid out of his own pocket, and Zhou Heng put it on the books all the same. Xiaoman had smeared his face with soot from the bottom of a pot, jammed a battered straw hat on his head, and was beating a drum bigger than his head. Ahe was mixed in among the crowd of onlookers, the blue cloth cloak wrapped up to her chin; a line of red hem showed below it, and she drew her foot back to hide it. Zhou Heng stood at the very back of the crowd, ledger in hand.

The shaman shook her bell and walked three times round the boat, muttering under her breath, then stopped and called out across the Zhang at the top of her voice: "Lord River Lord — this year's bride, we're sending her to you —" The Three Elders stood up and clapped and cheered, and several burly men came forward to push the boat.

Xiaoman put down his drumsticks, stepped forward, and said with a face full of sincerity, "Granny shaman, wait a moment." Every face on the bank turned to look at him. The shaman narrowed her eyes. "Where did you spring from?" Xiaoman puffed out his chest. "…Actually, I'm a drummer." The shaman's eyes narrowed to slits, and the feathers on her head shook three times. "A drummer, and you dare stop the River Lord's wedding?" Xiaoman said, "I wouldn't dare. Only…" He glanced at the Tofu Beauty in the boat and knitted his brows, looking very awkward. "This bride isn't pretty enough."

He said it smooth as anything, without a single stumble. When he'd said it, he shot a glance into the crowd.

The whole bank fell silent. Xiaoman went on, "Send him one that isn't pretty, and if his lordship the River Lord gets angry and there's a great flood next year, who'll answer for it?" The Three Elders looked at one another, six eyes round as could be; the deaf one hadn't caught it, cupped a hand behind his ear and said, "Eh?" The shaman said, "…Then what do you say we do?"

Xiaoman made a deep, respectful bow. "Someone has to go into the river first and have a word with his lordship the River Lord: the bride's not very pretty, we'll pick again, and in a few days we'll send a prettier one. A message that important — a drummer doesn't carry enough weight." Here he raised his eyes to her. "It has to be you, granny shaman. In person."

The shaman's face went red and then white, white and then green, and all at once turned a colour Xiaoman knew very well — the Ma family's colour, the colour of "we're done for"; every one of the feathers on her head stood on end. Before she could open her mouth, Xiaoman had walked over and picked her up. She was very light, a bag of bones, like a bundle of dry firewood. He threw her into the Zhang.

There was a splash, the water flew up high, and not a sound came from the whole bank; thousands of mouths hung open together and couldn't close. Xiaoman cupped his hands to the river. "Granny, thank you for your trouble."

The shaman thrashed a couple of times in the water and came up, all her chicken feathers plastered to her face. What she shouted, though, wasn't "River Lord." What she shouted was: "Immortal — Immortal, save me —" A mouthful of water went in, and she sank. The thousands on the bank took her to be calling to the River Lord; only Xiaoman heard it clearly, and his heart jolted: the "Immortal" she was calling was the old-well voice in the temple that night.

He was counting silently in his head. His plan was: count to thirty, then go into the water and pull her out — give her a fright, not take her life. As he counted he turned to the Three Elders under the parasol and said, "…Granny's getting on, and she's been gone all this time and still hasn't come back. Maybe she can't make herself clear to the River Lord. Someone whose word carries more weight had better go down and hurry things along."

The Three Elders' six legs went weak all together, and down they went in a row on their knees, thump, thump, thump. Xiaoman had counted to five. The water was too muddy to see the bottom, and for the first time a doubt came into his heart: by the time he'd counted thirty, would he still be able to find her?

He had counted to seven when there was a commotion in the crowd behind him, and then a splash.

He turned to look. On the muddy ground at the water's edge lay a blue cloth cloak, and on the cloak lay the little notebook, its pages flipping in the wind. Ahe was gone. She had jumped in. She hadn't counted. Someone in the crowd shrieked, "The bride! Another bride's jumped in the river!" — what had flashed past a moment before had been all red.

A red nose broke the surface, went under, came up again, dragging the old woman in the red clothes with the head of wet chicken feathers, inching towards the bank. She had laid her foundation; holding her breath underwater for as long as it takes to eat a meal was nothing to her. The hard part was not letting the thousands of mortals on the bank see that she was using magic. She towed the old woman with only one hand, making a great show of the effort; underwater, a green vine slid out of her cuff, wound itself round the root of an old willow on the bank, and drew itself back in inch by inch. The water was muddy; the people on the bank couldn't see the vine and thought only that she was lucky and had caught hold of a tree root. Xiaoman hurriedly jumped in to help, and the two of them, one on each side, dragged the old woman up onto the bank.

The shaman spat up water for a long time and lay sprawled on the bank. When she opened her eyes and saw that the one who had saved her was Ahe, the girl with the lamp that night, she stared and stared, and her lips trembled a long time without a single word coming out.

Under the parasol, the fat old man came to his senses first. Seeing the shaman come ashore alive and the river lying flat with nothing coming out of it, his knees stopped being weak; he pushed himself up on the thin old man's shoulder, pointed at Xiaoman, and shouted louder than the deaf one: "These two outsiders have blasphemed the River Lord! They've made us miss the lucky hour! Next year a great flood, and the whole city goes down with them! What are you standing there for? Push the boat!"

The burly men looked at one another and went back towards the boat. The thousands on the bank buzzed; some shrank back, some joined in the shout of "Push the boat!" A custom of twenty-five years doesn't turn over in the time it takes to drink a cup of tea. Xiaoman stood at the bow, soaked through, and his heart went cold: that mouth of his had run out here.

Ahe picked the little notebook up off the mud, wiped it on her wet sleeve, and opened it. The borrowed bride's dress was soaked through and clung to her, dripping, but her voice was level, the same as reading accounts in the inn, neither high nor low, sending the words out one by one:

"River Lord money, three hundred cash a household, three thousand households, nine hundred strings a year. That figure — all of you here pay it yourselves every year. You know it."

The fat old man's finger was still pointing at Xiaoman. He hadn't pulled it back.

"One boat, one string. Seven holes chiselled in the bottom, two hundred cash extra — it's written in Boatwright Liu's work ledger. One bride's dress, eight hundred cash. Incense, candles, drums and pipes, three strings. Five strings spent a year, eight hundred and ninety-five strings left over." She turned a page. "A bride a year, twenty-five years, every year the same."

"The roof of the River Lord's temple has leaked for three years and nobody's mended it. This money didn't go into the river." She raised her head and looked at the three men under the parasol. "It went into the pockets of these gentlemen under the parasol."

The buzz on the bank stopped all at once.

She went on, "When the River Lord takes a wife, he takes a living woman. Seven holes chiselled in the bottom of the boat are because they're afraid she'll reach the River Lord alive."

The thin old man said shrilly, "Made-up sums! A stranger girl, counting on her fingers, doing our accounts? Who took how much, then? You tell us!"

Ahe didn't answer him. Who took how much, she didn't know, and she didn't pretend to know. She turned round and held the little notebook out to the shaman lying sprawled in the mud. "Granny, these eight hundred and ninety-five strings. How were they split?"

Thousands of pairs of eyes came to rest on the shaman together.

The shaman sat in the mud with her head of wet chicken feathers, looking at the notebook, then at Ahe — these were the very hands that had gripped her underwater just now and wouldn't let go. Her lips trembled a long time, and at last she said, "…It's true." Her voice was as hoarse as a cracked gong, but she got the words out one by one. "We settled up once a year, on the wedding night, in the fat one's back hall, split in front of me. A hundred strings to me; to the three of them, two hundred and sixty-five strings a house." She held out a shaking finger and pointed under the parasol at them one by one. "Twenty-five years: six thousand six hundred and twenty-five strings for each of them. My share, two thousand five hundred strings." Then: "The holes in the boats — I told Boatwright Liu to chisel them. The River Lord… the River Lord never said one word to me. Twenty-five years, not one."

Ahe lowered her head and added a line in the notebook, her lips moving: a hundred, plus three times two hundred and sixty-five, eight hundred and ninety-five. It tallied.

At the back of the crowd, Boatwright Liu squatted down and held his head in his hands.

At first the bank was still, so still you could hear the Zhang flowing. Then someone, no one knew who, threw a shoe under the parasol first.

This time the Three Elders' legs really did go weak. That day not one of the Three Elders went into the river. The three of them knelt at the edge of the Zhang and kowtowed to the river until their foreheads were bruised, the fat one sobbing "boo-hoo-hoo" as he kowtowed, snot trailing onto his new silk gown; they had all the chests in their houses carried out, and every cash of the twenty-five years' River Lord money that was left unspent they coughed up, piled on the bank, piled into a little hill. At the bottom of that little hill Xiaoman saw several big ingots of gleaming white silver.

He went over, picked one out from the foot of the hill of money, turned it over, and set it base up on the mud: a circle, and inside it one stroke — even the little kink where that stroke tipped to the right was exactly the same as on the ingot at the Si River wharf on the day the sect opened its gates.

Zhou Heng saw it too, and said nothing. He looked at the ingot for a long time. Xiaoman tucked it inside his robe and said, "The day the gates opened, on the Si River wharf, I saw one just like it." So Zhou Heng didn't ask any more.

Xiaoman picked out three pieces of broken silver and handed them to Zhou Heng. "Senior brother Zhou's tea money." Zhou Heng took the silver, weighed it in his hand and said, "This one I'll take." He looked at Xiaoman and said, "…That move's been used before. Same city, same river." Xiaoman stood there gaping for a good while and said, "…I've never read that bit."

Zhou Heng looked at him a long time. "Then you've got more of a future than the man before you. The witch he threw in, nobody fished out."

Xiaoman turned round. Ahe was crouching by the river, wringing the water out of her plaits, the cloak wrapped round her again. He went over and said, low, "I told you. Count to thirty." Ahe said, "In those wet red clothes, with a head full of chicken feathers, going under — she wouldn't last ten. Your thirty was counted for the people on the bank." She flung her plaits over her shoulder and went on, "If she died, these accounts would be nothing but an outsider's say-so."

Xiaoman looked at her. She tucked the little notebook back inside her clothes and said nothing more.

---

Xiaoman had thought that was the end of it. Then the Zhang suddenly stopped. Not ebbed: stopped. Wave after wave on the river hung in mid-air, and not one drop fell. The thousands on the bank all shut their mouths at once. A chill came creeping off the river, and his breath came out white — with the sun still blazing in the sky.

Yinyue's voice, this time, wasn't sleepy at all: "…He's awake." Xiaoman said, "Who's awake?" Yinyue said, "The one under the water." She paused, then said, "He's very angry."

Before the words had died away, the Zhang stood up. Not rose: stood. The whole river stood up out of its bed into a wall. In the wall of water were thousands upon thousands of fish, all facing one way, leaping upward one after another, as if the whole Zhang were jumping an invisible Dragon Gate. The wall of water rose higher and higher, higher than the walls of Ye, and the riverbed showed: mud, waterweed, and wooden boats, one after another, sunk crooked every which way in the silt, every bottom chiselled with holes; the boats were empty, nothing left in them but bride's dresses rotted by the water, the red soaked to black. Xiaoman counted. Twenty-five boats. Twenty-five bride's dresses.

On the bank, someone began to cry out loud. First one, then two, then a whole crowd. These were the people who over twenty-five years had watched their own daughters sit down in a wooden boat.

In the middle of the wall of water, out of the deepest black water, a figure slowly rose: a man's face, robed in water, two dragons under his feet, both green, with scales as big as millstones, bearing him slowly up into the air. All the vapour on the river gathered towards him, and the people on the bank smelled a very clean, very cold smell of river water, like the surface of a river after the first snow. He looked down at the bank, and his eyes were deeper than the Zhang. Zhou Heng, standing behind Xiaoman, gripped his ledger with both hands so hard his knuckles went white, and murmured, "…The River Lord. The real River Lord."

The thousands on the bank went down on their knees all together. The Three Elders knelt, the shaman lay sprawled, the Tofu Beauty stood at the water's edge with her eyes wide. Xiaoman didn't kneel. His legs had long since gone weak, but he didn't kneel; he only clutched the wooden tablet at his chest.

---

The figure hung in the air and did not speak. He only lowered his eyes and swept them across the thousands of kneeling heads on the bank, one by one, and when they reached Xiaoman, they stopped.

Of all those thousands, only three were standing. One was the Tofu Beauty: she was mute, and kneeling she still couldn't cry out her wrong, so she simply didn't kneel. One stood far off under the row of old willows upstream, a youth in white with a sword at his waist, about Xiaoman's age by the look of him. And the last was Shen Xiaoman.

Under those willows there was in fact one more person. He wore white too and stood half a step behind the youth with the sword, in the deepest part of the willow shade; the people on the bank were kneeling and couldn't see him, but Xiaoman, standing, could. A long, thin shadow, hands clasped behind his back, not moving at all. It was Old Well. Old Well stood behind the youth like an attendant; which meant the youth's standing in the Taiyi Palace was higher even than Old Well's. Yinyue said only one thing: "Keep hidden. Don't let Old Well look at you twice." If Old Well looked a few times more, what he would see was the one inside the wooden tablet.

Under the River Lord's feet, the dragon on the left moved. Without a sound it twisted and came diving down out of the air, its head bigger than the Shens' kitchen. By what Elder Qu had taught him, this was the moment to go with the wind — a diving dragon brings a wind with it; ride it halfway and you slide clear. But those two white robes were watching from upstream. Xiaoman forced that breath down unraised and just threw himself to one side, flat on his face in the mud like a dog, and the dragon's head grazed his back and missed. The dragon on the right followed with a sweep of its tail, low across the riverbank; he scrambled up and ran, three steps left, two steps right, stumbled, and by sheer luck ducked right under the tail.

The two dragons, one dive and one sweep, had both missed. They rose back into the air, one on each side, and stopped at the River Lord's feet, four eyes fixed on him, as if conferring. Xiaoman was panting, but he had already worked it out: they hadn't come to kill him. If they'd wanted to kill him, the first dive would have finished him. This was a test. But a test of what? Of how fast he could run, or of whether he —

He didn't get to finish the thought. Both dragons moved at once. This time they didn't come at him; they swung round him, one on each side, and went straight for the bank behind him. The Tofu Beauty was standing there, in front of a crowd of kneeling people, still in her bride's dress, her eyes wide open, not moving at all.

Xiaoman had only to step aside and that dive would be none of his business. His legs wanted to step aside. His mother had said: if you can't win, run; there's no shame in running. But this time he didn't step aside. He took two steps back, back to stand in front of the Tofu Beauty, turned round with his back to her, flung both arms wide, and stood square between the two dragons.

Both hands open, palms out.

He used no skill at all. He just stood there, mud dripping from his hair drop by drop, both calves shaking, shaking so that the people on the bank could see it.

The two dragon heads, bigger than a kitchen, stopped a foot in front of him. Four green eyes, so close that he could see himself reflected in them: a silly boy covered in mud with both arms flung out. The breath from the dragons' nostrils was so cold it put a layer of white frost on his eyebrows. He heard his own voice, shaking all to pieces, but getting the words out one by one: "…She sells tofu. She never hurt anyone."

The two dragons looked at him, their whiskers drifting in the wind. Then the one on the left brought its nose close and touched him lightly on the forehead, cold, like well water in the twelfth month; then it drew its head back, and the one on the right drew back too. They rose slowly back into the air, back to the River Lord's feet, coiled themselves up, and were still.

Only then did Xiaoman notice that his arms were still stiffly flung out and he couldn't get them down. Behind him, the Tofu Beauty reached out a hand and patted him on the back twice, very, very lightly. Yinyue said, low, "…Idiot." After a moment she added, "Hid yourself well enough, though."

Upstream under the willows, Old Well took one step forward out of the deepest shade. The sun fell on his face, a face so white you couldn't tell its age. His eyes weren't on the dragons, nor on the River Lord; they were on Xiaoman, and on the thread of silver light showing from under the front of Xiaoman's robe. He looked once, and stepped back into the willow shade.

Xiaoman didn't see what he had looked at. He only felt the back of his neck go cold, as if a well had opened its mouth behind him.

---

The River Lord looked at them, the wall of water standing behind him without a drop running down it, and only then did he speak. His voice wasn't loud, but it set the whole Zhang humming: "…Who. Who said I wanted a wife?"

There wasn't a sound on the bank. The River Lord said, "Twenty-five years. Every autumn, a boat, a girl, pushed in, dropped on my head. Two thousand years I have been under this water, and I have never asked you for a single thing. And this year, of all things —" his gaze swept over the shaman, over Ahe, over Xiaoman, "one after another, thrown in, jumping in."

With every sentence he spoke, the wall of water behind him leaned forward a little more. By the last sentence its top had curled over into a white wave hanging above the walls of Ye. Water splashing from the crest struck the gate tower over the river with a great crack and crash, and a corner of the tower actually came down; the eave, the string of wind chimes with it, and the tiles went pouring down into the water gate below. A wail of crying rose from inside the city.

The River Lord said, "You people of Ye have eaten people in my name for twenty-five years."

Xiaoman knew that this was when he ought to kneel — both dragons had tested him already, and to go on standing now would be not knowing when he was well off.

But he had a failing: the more frightened he was, the less his mouth could keep still. This time there was a city behind him, and in the city a tofu shop, and in the tofu shop an old man locked up who had knelt a day and a night. He couldn't run. Fight, of course, he could do even less. That left only one thing: talk.

He took one step forward and made a deep, respectful bow, deeper than the one he had made to the Three Elders. "Your lordship the River Lord, in your wisdom, look!"

The River Lord's eyes came to rest on him. The moment that gaze landed, he felt as though the Zhang had been poured over him from head to foot; he went cold right through. He stole a glance at the white wave overhead, swallowed, and forced himself on: "My lord, the ones who ate people were three old men and an old woman; the people of this whole city are the ones who were eaten. If you bring your hand down, the ones who were eaten get eaten again, by you — and the three old men are kneeling on the bank, not a hair of them wet. Isn't that doing them a favour?"

The River Lord looked down at Xiaoman, looked for a long time, and the white wave hung over the city wall without moving. Xiaoman was just secretly letting out a breath when he heard him say, mildly, "Mortal mouths. In two thousand years I have heard a great many."

The white wave curled forward another foot.

Sweat burst out all over Xiaoman's body. His mind spun like the abacus under his mother's hand, turning over every word in his belly, and not one would come. That mouth of his could fool Ma San, could fool Ma Xiaobao, but it couldn't fool a river two thousand years old.

His eyes had nowhere to go and fell to the mud: on the riverbank, a drag mark ran from the water's edge up onto the bank, with two lines of wet footprints either side of it, one large, one small — made just now when they hauled the shaman ashore. Suddenly something lit up in him. Mortal mouths the River Lord had listened to for two thousand years and believed not a word; what the River Lord believed was only his own water.

He stopped bowing and raised his head. "My lord, I'll say no more. I'll only ask you one thing: what happens in this river — can anything be hidden from you?"

The River Lord said, "Nothing is hidden from me."

Xiaoman said, "Then just now, how many went into the water?"

The River Lord didn't answer. So Xiaoman counted for him, on his fingers: "The first was thrown in — I'm the one who threw her, and I own it. The second was me jumping in myself to pull someone out; that doesn't count. And there's one more —" he turned round and pointed into the crowd, "who jumped in on her own to fish her out."

There was a dead silence on the bank. After a while someone in the crowd stood up, a bride's dress soaked through under her cloak, hair stuck to her forehead, her red nose redder still with cold, the little mud-smeared notebook clutched in her hand. It was Ahe.

The River Lord's gaze followed the finger and came to rest on her. "She pushed twenty-five girls down to me. What did you fish her out for?"

Ahe looked up at him, water still dripping from her face, but her voice didn't shake at all: "She hasn't paid what she owes yet. If she drowns, the debt goes bad."

The River Lord said nothing.

Xiaoman said, "My lord, two thousand years you've been under this water. How many people have you seen throw others in? And how many have you seen jump in to fish out their enemy?"

After a long time the River Lord said, "…One."

Xiaoman said, "She's in this city." He raised a hand and pointed at the white wave overhead. "If you bring your hand down, it comes down on those three and on her as well. Your water can tell who throws people in and who pulls them out; your wave can't."

The River Lord looked down at him, then at Ahe. In those eyes deeper than the Zhang something slowly melted. He said, "…A mortal mouth." He paused. "This time, though, it spoke of what happened in my water."

The wall of water behind him came down slowly, slowly. The white wave drew back from the city wall, the thousands upon thousands of fish fell back into the water together, and the twenty-five wooden boats and twenty-five bride's dresses were covered by the river again. The sun came out again.

The River Lord raised his hand and flicked, and something fell at Ahe's feet with a *ting*. It was a scale, the size of a palm, bluish-white, so thin the light shone through it. Ahe picked it up. The scale trembled faintly in her palm, turned into a film of watery light, and soaked into her palm and was gone. She stared at her hand, slowly closed it, slowly opened it again, and said quietly, "…It's cool. There's water moving inside."

---

The people on the bank were still kneeling; not one yet dared get up. But the Tofu Beauty ran.

She held up the hem of her bride's dress, one foot bare — the other shoe was stuck in the soft mud of the riverbank — and ran towards the gate tower. She couldn't shout, so when she ran all you could hear was her breathing, gasp after gasp, quick and hoarse. Xiaoman ran after her, and when he got under the water gate, he stopped.

The corner of the gate tower that had fallen had blocked half the water gate's arch. Broken beams, shattered tiles, grey bricks, all crushed into one heap, and the string of wind chimes buried under the rubble was still ringing, one note after another. From under the heap of rubble a hand stuck out, bean pulp on its forearm.

Later the neighbours said that when the river stood up, the two men guarding the door had dropped everything and run. Old Hu, dragging his bad leg, had run all the way from the west of town to the river to stop that boat. He had got as far as the water gate when the wave came down.

Xiaoman and Ahe, and a few neighbours who had come to their senses, dug him out brick by brick. In the end there was the broken beam that four men couldn't lift, and Xiaoman held it up on his own — he was past caring about hiding. Under the beam was Old Hu. A piece of his chest had been crushed in, bloody froth was bubbling from the corners of his mouth, and he was dragging in breath after breath, less going in each time than leaking out. His eyes were open; he saw his daughter, and his lips moved.

Xiaoman knelt beside him. Ahe was already kneeling on Old Hu's other side, one hand held over his caved-in chest, sending in the green light from her fingertips, thread by thread.

The bloody froth at Old Hu's mouth stopped, and the breaths he was dragging in slowly grew a little more even, a little longer. A little colour came back into his face, and his eyes brightened, brightened enough to know people again. He looked at his daughter and held out a hand. She knelt in the rubble and gripped it.

Xiaoman looked at Ahe. Ahe's forehead was covered in sweat; she gave him a small shake of the head. A cultivator's wounds she could heal; Old Hu was a mortal, and in that caved-in piece of his chest it was more than bone that had been crushed, and a mortal body couldn't stand her pouring spiritual power into it for long. That thread of green light was only holding him up, and it could hold him up for as long as it takes to drink a cup of tea.

Old Hu knew it too. He didn't speak of the tofu shop, nor of the house; he held his daughter's hand and said, word by word, "…The year the oil-seller's girl from East Street went on the boat… Pa didn't make a sound." He took a breath. "Today Pa… made a sound."

His daughter nodded and nodded. She opened her mouth and strained with her throat until the cords of her neck stood out, but no sound at all would come. So she turned his hand over and wrote in his palm with her finger. Stroke by stroke, very slowly. The corner of Old Hu's mouth moved, as if he were smiling.

Old Hu said, "…Can turn it."

The time it takes to drink a cup of tea went by very fast. When his hand let go, the string of wind chimes from the gate tower was still ringing under the rubble, one note after another.

She didn't cry out loud. She couldn't. She only knelt there and held her father's hand to her chest, held it a long time, held it until it was cold.

Xiaoman sat on the heap of rubble and didn't move for a long time. Ahe crouched beside him, the hand that had sent the green light still shaking, and she didn't speak either.

The River Lord still hung in the middle of the Zhang, looking at the knot of people under the gate tower. After a long time he turned and said to the people on the bank, "…Those girls aren't dead. Thirty li downstream, Willow Bend. Twenty-five years, twenty-five of them, all there." He glanced at the shaman sprawled on the bank. "They knew it themselves: if they went home, the whole family would go down with them. The people of Willow Bend kept it quiet for them; the road downstream I flooded for twenty-five years." He paused. "From today, I won't."

On the bank an old man jerked his head up; then another, and another. Some scrambled up and ran downstream, losing their shoes and not stopping for them; some ran two steps, their legs gave way, and they knelt again and kowtowed to the river, and this time it was to give thanks.

Twenty-five girls, and not one of them dead. The one father who had come to stop the boat died under the gate tower.

Zhou Heng didn't go. He stood where he was, the ledger held to his chest, not moving at all. Xiaoman said, "Senior brother Zhou, aren't you going to go and look?" Zhou Heng said, "…I'll wait here for you. The abbey put you in my charge." But as he said it his eyes were fixed downstream the whole time, on the crowd running away into the distance.

The River Lord hadn't gone. He hung in the very middle of the Zhang, standing on his two dragons, looking down at Xiaoman — or, more exactly, at his chest. When he spoke, his lips didn't move; the voice came welling up out of the water under Xiaoman's feet, word by word, and went into his ears alone: "…You smell of Qingqiu."

Xiaoman's back went cold right through. The River Lord's gaze moved to the little bronze brazier inside his robe. "And that brazier you carry. It's not a thing of the human world. What are you?"

Xiaoman's mouth hung open; he couldn't say a word. Yinyue slipped out and crouched on his shoulder. This time the people on the bank couldn't see her; only he and the River Lord could. She looked at the River Lord and the first thing she said was, "…[Bingyi]{注=bingyi}."

The moment the name was out, she herself was taken aback, as if it hadn't been remembered but had fallen out of her mouth by itself. The River Lord looked at her, and something flickered in his eyes. He said, "…You. You're still alive." His eyes went to the one tail behind her, and he said nothing more. Yinyue said nothing either, and tucked that tail in under herself.

Xiaoman asked under his breath, "Bingyi?" Yinyue said, "That's his own name. 'River Lord' is what you mortals call him. He's the water god who guards the Zhang; he's been in this river for two thousand years. For power, your Abbot is only a junior beside him. But he's a guardian of the river; he can't leave it, and he can't just raise his hand against people on the bank."

The River Lord ignored Yinyue and looked at Xiaoman again. "When that old woman fell in, she shouted in my water. What she shouted wasn't 'River Lord.' She shouted 'Immortal.'" Then: "…Who is the Immortal?"

Xiaoman walked two steps into the river and stood knee-deep in the water, and lowered his voice — standing in his water, anything he said the River Lord could hear — and told him all of it, one thing after another: the two big ingots, Old Well giving the shaman silver in the temple, and the two things Old Well had said. When he got to "Last year the Zhang rose three feet. This year he ought not to be able to hold back," the River Lord's face changed. When he got to "When he comes up, none of you is to stand in his way," the River Lord said nothing for a long while.

After a long time he said, "…So that's how it is." There was no anger in his voice, only a weariness beyond words. "Two thousand years I have been under this water and never moved. Wherever I come up from, something lies under it. The Zhang is eight hundred li long; they couldn't find it. So every year they dropped a girl on me, on me, twenty-five years of it, waiting for me to come up by myself." He looked towards the eastern city wall. "When cultivators move against me, heaven looks down; when mortals throw brides into a river, heaven doesn't look. …A fine piece of reckoning."

At this, Xiaoman's hands and feet went icy. That circle, that ingot, and Old Well — what they had spent twenty-five years and two thousand five hundred taels of silver and failed to do, he had done for them in a single morning. He had thrown the shaman into the river. He had brought the River Lord up.

He turned and looked back towards the water gate. Beside the heap of rubble, the neighbours had lifted Old Hu's body onto a door and laid a white cloth over it. The Tofu Beauty knelt beside the door, still in the bride's dress.

He reckoned it back in his head, item by item: it was because he threw the shaman into the river that the River Lord was disturbed and came up from under the water; when the River Lord came up he was angry, and the wave he brought down knocked down that corner of the gate tower; the fallen corner of the gate tower crushed Old Hu. Reckoned to the end, a share of Old Hu's life was entered in Shen Xiaoman's books.

Yinyue crouched on his shoulder and said not a word.

But the River Lord had already turned round and was pointing at the deepest, blackest water in the middle of the river behind him. Under the water something was shining, brighter, dimmer, brighter, dimmer, as if something were breathing very, very slowly. The River Lord said, "Two thousand years ago someone asked me to guard it. She said someone would come for it. The one who came would smell of Qingqiu." Then: "The smell alone isn't enough. Those two dragons just now were asking you on her behalf."

Yinyue said, "…Who asked you?" The River Lord looked at Yinyue and didn't answer, only said to Xiaoman, "I've come up; those who ought to know now know. Go down and take it away." Xiaoman said, "Can't you bring it up?" The River Lord said, "There's a seal on that cauldron that I can't open. The ring of water inside the seal isn't mine to command. In there I can't part the water or send in air; I can't help you at all. Once you go down, all you'll have to rely on is the one breath you hold."

---

Only when the people on the bank had all gone, and the sun was touching the western city wall, did Xiaoman go into the water.

The Zhang was very deep, and the further down, the darker. Under the middle of the river there was a pit as well, and he swam down along its side until the little bit of daylight overhead was gone and there was only the little bit of light at the bottom of the pit.

At a certain depth his body suddenly tightened; the water around him went ten times colder at once, so cold it wasn't like water at all, more like a solid block of ice with him in the middle of it. Something went *hum* in his ears; Bingyi's voice was gone, the sound of the Zhang was gone, even his own heartbeat seemed to be behind a wall. He knew this was the ring of water Bingyi had spoken of, the water that wasn't his to command. Since laying his foundation he could hold one breath for half a stick of incense, and only now did he learn how short half a stick of incense was.

At the bottom of the pit was the cauldron. Four legs, two ears, taller than a man, half sunk in the silt. He measured from one leg to the next with his hand: one span, two spans… seven spans — the same width as the four holes in the floor of the stone chamber in the secret realm. The body of the cauldron was ice cold and carved all over, and he felt his way across it: mountains, rivers, trees, beasts, people, layer upon layer. He touched a winding river, and beside it a square city — that was the Zhang, that was Ye. What was carved on this cauldron was the whole of Ji Province.

Over the cauldron's mouth lay a stone slab, and on the slab were cut nine squares, three to a row, the middle one cut deepest. Scattered round the slab were nine square stones, each cut with dots, from one dot to nine. The dots were cut very shallow and plastered with silt, and he could only count them one by one with his finger.

Yet he wasn't flustered. He knew these nine squares far too well. Feeling the dots, he put the stones into the squares one by one: the nine-dot one at the top; the one-dot one at the bottom; the three-dot one on the left; the seven-dot one on the right; the two-dot and four-dot ones on the two shoulders; the six-dot and eight-dot ones on the two feet. When he had placed eight, he picked up the last one — five dots — and pressed it into the middle square. It wouldn't go in.

He pressed again. The stone stuck at the mouth of the square and didn't budge; it was as if there were a hand underneath pushing it back. His heart sank: could one of the eight be in the wrong place? But these nine numbers he had been reciting from the age of five to nineteen; he could lay them out right with his eyes shut. He counted the eight stones again with his finger, one after another: nine at the top, one at the bottom, three left and seven right, two and four the shoulders, six and eight the feet. Not one was wrong.

None of the eight was wrong, and yet the five-dot one wouldn't go in. That was more frightening than a mistake: a mistake you can fix, but if nothing's wrong, you don't know which one to change. By now that one breath of his was more than half used up. His chest felt as if someone had laid a millstone on it, and the bit of cauldron-light before his eyes went bright, then dim, and he couldn't tell whether it was the cauldron breathing or himself about to lose his sight.

He panicked, opened his mouth, and choked in a mouthful of ice-cold water. The water was like a knife, cutting down his throat into his chest. He coughed for all he was worth, and the more he coughed, the more water poured in. The bit of cauldron-light before his eyes broke up, broke into sparks, piece by piece; his hands and feet were suddenly light, so light they didn't seem to be his. He thought, very clearly: so this is what drowning is like. It doesn't hurt. It's just cold, and once you're cold, you're light. He thought too of that hand under the gate tower with bean pulp on it.

Just then the wooden tablet at his chest grew faintly warm. Only a little, like an ember tucked inside your coat on a winter night. No voice; it just went warm once, and then cool again.

That bit of warmth steadied his heart. Only then did he hear his own heart: everything around him was behind a wall, and only it the wall couldn't keep out, thudding away under his chest, quick and ragged. He didn't try to do anything about it, only listened; and as he listened, the thudding slowed, and deepened.

He closed his eyes. Only now did he know the weight of Elder Qu's sixteen characters: the first eight, his heart had just done for him; the last eight spoke of those very thoughts, "so this is what drowning is like." He pushed those stray thoughts down one by one and only looked inward. It was dark underwater to begin with, and with his eyes shut, it grew light. He saw the oil lamp in the kitchen, its wick leaping; saw his mother sitting at one end of the stove and himself at the other; saw her dip her finger in the water bowl and draw squares on the stove top, reciting as she drew: "Nine on the head, one under the feet, three on the left, seven on the right, two and four for the shoulders, six and eight for the feet, five in the middle." When she had finished drawing, she pointed with her finger and taught him square by square: "Under three comes eight; under seven comes six. Got it?" He said, "Got it." She said, "If you've got it, don't ever forget it."

He hadn't forgotten. Of the nine stones, he hadn't placed a single one wrong. So whatever was wrong wasn't in the stones.

He thought of what the River Lord had said: those two dragons had been asking him on behalf of the one who left the cauldron there. This stone slab was asking him too. What it asked wasn't whether he remembered the nine numbers; anyone could recite those. What it asked was whether, when a man was about to drown, his heart was still steady. The middle square held the five, five in the middle; and the middle of the nine palaces is a man's heart. When he pressed that stone just now his heart had been in turmoil, and the spiritual power in his hand in turmoil too, so the slab had pushed it back.

He stopped thinking about how much of that breath was left. He held the five-dot stone in his palm and waited until his heart was beating evenly, beat after beat, and only then set this last one, the ninth, gently into the middle square, without pressing on it at all.

This time it dropped in by itself. There was a small *click*, not loud, but underwater he heard it clearly, like the crooked door of the Shen house finally set straight by someone and dropped into its socket. All nine squares on the slab lit up at once, starting from the middle one and lighting outward square by square into a ring. Then the slab cracked open, and a beam of light shot out of the cauldron's mouth, shot out of the water, and went straight up into the sky.

The ring of icy water broke up all at once. His body went slack, and the Zhang was back around him, cool, but no longer cold. Underwater, he heard a very, very faint ringing of the cauldron, like someone who had slept for a very, very long time waking and softly letting out a sigh.

---

When Xiaoman came up to the surface, the sun had gone down entirely and only a streak of dark red was left at the edge of the sky. The cauldron came up with him, growing smaller as it rose; by the time it broke the surface it was only the size of his palm, and it settled into his hand, heavy and warm. He lay over a rock at the water's edge spitting up water, and when the water was out he spat up a mouthful of blood, and by the time he had his breath back the moon was rising in the east. When the seal over the cauldron's mouth opened just now, the spirit qi sealed in the cauldron for two thousand years had come pouring out all at once; most of it shot up into the sky, but one stream had gone in through his chest into the wooden tablet. The tablet at his chest was hotter than it had ever been — Yinyue, inside it, had drunk that stream of spirit qi down to the last drop. He looked down: on the tablet, of the curled-up little fox's nine tails, the second was lit. The fine crack that had opened in the back of the tablet in the secret realm had closed too, and the length of the first tail that had been dark all these years lit up again with it.

A little silver fox slipped out of the tablet. Having drunk the cauldron's spirit qi, this time she wasn't half transparent; her fur was solid, silver, dazzling in the moonlight, and behind her trailed two tails. She looked down at her second tail, looked at it this way and that, and suddenly her eyes went fixed. "…There's light." Xiaoman said, "What?" Yinyue's voice was shaking: "A very bright light. Above the Ninth Heaven… a very, very bright light, cutting down at this queen… and in the light, someone speaking…" She frowned, trying with all her might to remember. "…What they said, I can't remember."

Xiaoman didn't press her. He held her in his arms. She didn't struggle.

On the bank only Ahe was left, crouching at the water's edge; Zhou Heng stood behind her. The whole afternoon he hadn't moved a step.

There was one more person. He stood under an old willow on the bank, in white, a long sword at his waist: the youth with the sword who had stood under the willows upstream that day. Where he stood was just out of reach of Bingyi's water. He was about Xiaoman's age and a good deal better-looking; the sword at his waist was still in its scabbard, and the few willow leaves brushing the scabbard had a layer of white frost on their tips. Old Well, who had stood half a step behind him that day, wasn't in the willow shade now; where he had gone, there was no telling.

His gaze fell on the palm-sized cauldron in Xiaoman's hand, and the first thing he said was: "Thank you." The voice was clear and bright, a young man's voice, nothing at all like that "old well" in the temple. Xiaoman's heart eased, and then tightened again: white robes, again.

Xiaoman was taken aback. "For what?" The youth said, "Twenty-five years the Palace couldn't get him to come up. You did it in one morning." He came two steps forward, and the moonlight fell on the scabbard at his waist; on its tip was carved a mark: a circle, and inside it one stroke. "Taiyi Palace. [Zhao Ang]{注=zhaoang}."

The Taiyi Palace — Xiaoman had heard those words more than once; this was the first time he had heard them from the mouth of one of the Palace's own. He reached out to this one with his spirit sense and could only make out that he was at Foundation Establishment; further in there was only fog, and he couldn't find the bottom. By the sound of him, he was no errand boy.

Behind Xiaoman there was a rustle: Zhou Heng turning the pages of his ledger. He turned to the page for the eighth of the tenth month, looked at the ledger, looked at the scabbard, his hands shaking so that the page rattled, muttering, "…Ten ingots a year. Twenty-five years."

Zhao Ang glanced at him. "Checking the accounts? Thoroughly, too."

His spirit sense swept across the bank, over Zhou Heng, over Ahe, and paused on Ahe. Xiaoman felt it, like a cold wind skimming low over the riverbank. Ahe crouched at the water's edge wringing out her sleeve, didn't even look up, her red nose red with cold. The spirit sense rested on her for the space of a breath, saw that she was at Middle Attainment of Foundation Establishment, and slid away. What he wanted was the cauldron, not people.

Xiaoman gripped the cauldron in his hand. "You dropped girls on him for twenty-five years. Twenty-five girls. And today a man who sold tofu was crushed to death under the gate tower."

Zhao Ang's brows moved. It was very quick, so quick Xiaoman almost thought it was the moonlight wavering. His eyes flicked towards the water gate in the west of the city, then came back. He said, "…Twenty-five." After a while he went on, "There's a steward of the Palace who comes to this city every year when autumn begins and stays till the eighteenth of the tenth month. Twenty-five years. I've been here half a month. How he goes about his duties, I didn't ask." When he had said that, there was nothing on his face again; his gaze went back to the cauldron. "That thing belongs to the Taiyi Palace."

Xiaoman said, "Why?"

Only then did Zhao Ang look at him properly. "Because all under heaven should be one."

In Xiaoman's arms Yinyue gave a violent shudder. He looked down at her; she raised her head from the front of his robe, her golden eyes fixed straight on Zhao Ang, on the mark on his scabbard, and the fur all over her body stood up, hair by hair. Her voice was very soft, very cold: "…This queen has heard those words before."

"Above the Ninth Heaven," Yinyue said, word by word. "When that light came cutting down, the voice in the light said exactly this: all under heaven should be one."

Zhao Ang couldn't see Yinyue or hear her speak; he only saw Xiaoman holding a bundle of wet clothes, and his face suddenly change. His hand slowly came to rest on the hilt of his sword.

Just then, behind Xiaoman, the Zhang made a soft sound. He didn't need to turn round to know what it was: in the black water in the middle of the river the heads of the two green dragons had surfaced, and four eyes opened together, like four green lanterns in the moonlight.

Zhao Ang's hand stopped on the hilt. He looked at the four lanterns, then at Xiaoman. "That seal — the Palace's people studied it against the old diagrams for twenty-five years and couldn't undo it. How did you undo it?" Xiaoman said, "Guessed." Zhao Ang said, "…Guessed."

Xiaoman's eyes dropped to the sword under Zhao Ang's hand: on the tip of the scabbard was chiselled a circle, and inside it one stroke. Zhao Ang followed his eyes and glanced down, and for the first time something showed on his face; whether it was surprise or something else, there was no telling. He said, "The Palace's seal."

Xiaoman said, "The day the gates opened, on the Si River wharf, a man in white handed a stonemason an ingot of silver and took a child of five or six off him in exchange. That ingot had this chiselled on the bottom too." He looked at Zhao Ang. "The child's called Xiaodou. Is he one of yours?"

Zhao Ang didn't answer. In the moonlight his handsome face went white, little by little. After a long time he said, "…Children in the Palace have no names."

Xiaoman asked no more. He had understood: in the Palace, Xiaodou had no name; and the one saying so had most likely been a child of the Palace too.

Zhao Ang stared at him, the hand on his hilt loosening and tightening, and at last he let it drop. "Today Bingyi is protecting you. I don't fight a river." He turned, took two steps, stopped again, and said without looking back, "You won't be able to hold on to that thing." With that he walked step by step into the darkness behind the willows, without a sound underfoot.

Yinyue huddled in his arms. The fur on her body slowly lay flat again, but she didn't stop trembling, and she buried her face in the front of his robe and for a long, long time didn't lift it.

Xiaoman stood by the river and laid out in his mind, one by one, the times he had seen that mark these past years: the ingot pushed into a stonemason's hand on the wharf the day the gates opened, the ingot in the Three Elders' casket, the one on the tip of Zhao Ang's scabbard. The ones buying children: it. The ones combing the mountains for a white fox with a mirror: it. The ones dropping girls on Bingyi for twenty-five years: it. And in the light that cut off Yinyue's tails, what was spoken was it too — that single stroke, the word "one."

All under heaven should be one. He used to think it was just a fine-sounding saying.

The two green lanterns in the middle of the river stayed lit until Zhao Ang was far away, and only then slowly sank. Before they sank, Bingyi's face showed for a moment on the surface, and from far off he cupped his hands in salute to Yinyue in Xiaoman's arms.

Once the lanterns had sunk, there was only the moon left on the riverbank. Xiaoman tucked the cauldron inside his robe and turned to go — and the water under his feet stopped making any sound.

The few strands of waterweed in the shallows stood up straight, as if stuck in ice.

He knew this. Five years ago, the Si River ferry.

Old Well was standing under the willow again. This time he didn't step back. He came out of the willow shade, one step, then another, out into the moonlight, in white, with a face so white you couldn't tell its age, holding a bronze mirror, face up, as if he were holding a bowl of water. He looked at Xiaoman, and his voice was the same old well:

"Found it."

Xiaoman said, "That Zhao Ang of yours just said there'd be no fighting today." Old Well said, "A true disciple is young. He can afford to wait. I've been stuck at Foundation Establishment for thirty years. I can't. While he was here it wasn't my place to speak; once he left, I came back. The one in the river has sunk too. Right now, on this stretch of bank, there's no one protecting you."

Zhou Heng's ledger snapped shut. Ahe crouched at the water's edge, one hand drawn up into her sleeve, and didn't raise her head. Yinyue, in the front of his robe, didn't stir a hair; she only said, low, "This time this queen isn't coming out." She paused. "You do it yourself."

Old Well went on, "You've laid your foundation. You can run now." He lifted his chin and looked out at the middle of the Zhang. "But that beam of light just now shot up into the sky. It could be seen three hundred li away."

He raised one finger.

Five years ago, Xiaoman hadn't seen how that finger reached his chest. This time he saw it — he had laid his foundation, and his eyes were new — before the finger moved, the layer of light on Old Well's body surged towards the fingertip. His feet were already moving: he had walked this riverbank till he knew it by heart these past days, the nine palaces spread out in his mind; he was standing on Kan, the one, and slanted onto Xun, the four, left foot first, right foot swinging round. The finger struck empty air and hit the willow behind him; the willow broke at the waist with a crack, and a whole tree crashed sideways into the river.

Old Well looked at the tree. "Mm. You've learned a thing or two."

He turned the bronze mirror over. A cold light shot out of it, not at the man but straight at Xiaoman's chest, at the wooden tablet. Xiaoman's right hand was already inside his robe, and what it brought out was a shard of the Selfie Mirror — the biggest of its seven or eight pieces, which he had carried for five years — and he held it up to meet the light.

This broken bit of bronze, shattered down to one shard, could still do just one thing: whatever light hit it, it threw straight back.

There was a *hum*, and the light bent back and slammed into the mirror in Old Well's hand. With a *crack* the mirror split down the middle, from the centre right to the rim. Old Well looked down at the crack, and for the first time something showed on his face: not anger, but heartache, like a poor man looking at a bowl he has dropped.

In the time of that one look Xiaoman drew Matchstick. But when Old Well raised his head, his hand was already there: not a finger, a whole palm, pushing flat towards him, his lips moving — Xiaoman didn't catch what he was chanting. He blocked with the sword. The sword held; he didn't. The palm pushed on the sword, the sword pushed into his chest, and his whole body went flying; he heard his left shoulder go *crack*, and he fell into the Zhang.

The water caught him. Not cold, cool, holding him up like a hand. He rolled over in the water with the taste of blood in his mouth. On the bank, Old Well had come to the water's edge and stopped, and didn't go in. He looked down at the line of water just in front of his toes, looked for a while, and didn't step forward.

He raised that hand towards Xiaoman in the water, to push another palm.

Just then, under the row of old willows along the bank, a mass of roots came heaving up out of the mud with a *pop-pop*, thick and black — those were the vines Ahe had buried tree by tree along the bank these past days, all grown into roots now — one wound round his ankle, one lashed round the arm he had raised, and dragged that palm to one side — for just one instant. Ahe was still crouching where she was, that hand drawn into her sleeve, fingertips pinching a hand-sign, her red nose red with cold. Old Well had been guarding against the sword in Xiaoman's hand the whole time; he hadn't guarded against her, crouching on the bank.

In that instant, all the spiritual power in Old Well's body surged into the raised arm, straining against the roots; and the layer of light on his shoulder went thin for a moment with it.

This was what his master had taught him: "entering the gap." When a man sends all his spiritual power to one place, the body-guarding light everywhere else goes thin, and the thin place is the gap. On the clifftop Xiaoman had spent years watching the gaps in rocks, the gaps in trees, and on Black Pine Ridge he had seen the gap in the Heyu's neck; this time the gap opened on Old Well's shoulder. He stood up out of the water. He didn't hack; he sent the sword in — Matchstick went in along the place that had thinned for that one instant, like wind slipping through the crack of a door, in at the shoulder and out under the arm.

The arm, with the roots still wound round it, fell into the Zhang. A bubble rose to the surface, and then there was nothing.

Old Well didn't cry out. He looked at the half sleeve left to him, its cuff empty, dripping blood. Then he raised his head and looked at the middle of the river — under the black water there, two green lanterns were lighting up again, one, then the other.

He stepped back one step, back into the willow shade. That old-well voice came out of the dark, still level, not one word out of place:

"This one's on the books."

In the willow shade, there was no one.

Zhao Ang hadn't in fact gone far. On a stretch of the western city wall that hadn't fallen, a youth in white stood far off, and he had watched what happened on the riverbank from beginning to end. He didn't help Old Well, and he didn't help Xiaoman: he had said there would be no fighting today; Old Well had gone against his word and come back on his own, and losing an arm was Old Well's own affair. Only when the willow shade was empty did he turn, come down off the wall, and this time truly leave. Of the three on the riverbank, none of them knew anyone had stood on the wall.

Xiaoman stood waist-deep in the water, Matchstick still raised, and held it up a long time before he lowered it. His left arm hung at his side and wouldn't lift; when he opened his mouth he spat up another mouthful of blood. Ahe waded into the water and caught hold of him under the arm, her other hand held over that shoulder, sending in the green light from her fingertips thread by thread, murmuring the same line as always: Observe the Way of Heaven. Her hand was shaking.

"…Who taught you to jump into the water?"

Xiaoman said, "…Ma. If you can't win, run."

Ahe said nothing and dragged him towards the bank. Zhou Heng stood on the bank holding his ledger, his face almost as white as that man's. He opened the ledger, his hand shaking, and raised his brush, and for a long time didn't put it to the page.

Xiaoman sat on the bank looking at the line of water. Five years ago at the Si River ferry, that finger, he hadn't even seen it; today he had seen it, dodged it, and paid it back with a sword. He still couldn't win, he had still run, he had still been dragged out by someone else. But next time —

Next time, he had to be the one left standing.

By the time the three of them got back to the west of the city, it was the third watch.

The door of the tofu shop was open, and an oil lamp was burning inside. Old Hu lay on a door board with the white cloth over him, and under the board burned a bowl of ever-burning lamp oil. His daughter had changed out of the bride's dress and sat on the doorstep, beside her father.

She was grinding beans. The stone mill went round and round, even slower than that other day. She couldn't push the mill on her own; she would push it half a turn, stop, change hands, and push another half turn.

The row of charcoal strokes on the door frame was all crossed out.

When she saw them come, she stopped, ladled out a bowl of soy milk, and held it out to Xiaoman in both hands. The soy milk was warm. This time there was no sugar in it. When Xiaoman had drunk it, she gave him a nod and went back to sit at the mill.

Zhou Heng stood in the doorway, took out the blue cloth purse from inside his robe — the abbey's expenses for checking the accounts, five taels, not to be touched — tipped out two taels, set them on the corner of the table beside the door board, and said, "One coffin. The abbey's money, my books."

Ahe had stood in the doorway all this time without coming in. Now she went over to the girl, crouched down and said, "Your voice was burned out by a fever when you were three. It's an old injury. Sit still and I'll heal it."

Ahe held one hand in front of her throat, an inch away, and slowly sent in a fine thread of green light from her fingertips. This time she sent it far more slowly than under the gate tower: a mortal body can't take a sudden flood; it has to go a thread at a time. After as long as it takes to eat a meal she drew her hand back and said, "Done. Try. Say one word first."

The girl opened her mouth. First there was a rasp in her throat, like a door that hadn't been opened in years. She tried again. This time what came out was a word, hoarse and faint, but perfectly clear: "…Pa."

She said it to the door board. The man on the board didn't answer her. She said it again, a little louder. By the third time she was bent over the edge of the board, crying out loud. More than ten years, and this was the first time she had cried out loud.

Xiaoman stood in the doorway with a hard lump in his throat. Ahe came back to his side, the hand that had sent the green light hanging inside her sleeve, and neither of them said anything.

Outside the tofu shop, Xiaoman said, "Senior brother Zhou, Willow Bend — are we going?" Zhou Heng stood there looking downstream, looked a long time, and only then said, "…Someone who keeps the books has to go and check them against the real thing." From Ye to Willow Bend was thirty li, downstream along the Zhang. Zhou Heng walked in front, very fast, not looking where he was going.

---

When the three of them reached Willow Bend, it was the fourth watch.

Willow Bend was where the Zhang turned, a great stand of old willows and a dozen or so fishing households. The people from Ye had arrived at dusk, and along the bank fires were lit, one after another. The twenty-five girls, brought here by the river one by one over twenty-five years, mended nets, dried fish, some with children in their arms; the youngest still wore her hair in plaits — she was last year's bride, and her mother was holding her and calling her baby name over and over.

Zhou Heng walked among the fires, past one fire after another, looking at one face after another. He clutched the ledger in his hand, clutched it so tight it was as if that ledger were the one thing on him he could still keep hold of.

The very last fire was under the furthest willow. No one had come to that fire to find their kin; only a woman sat there, head bowed, mending a fishing net by the firelight. Her hair was half white, tied with an old hair cord so faded you couldn't tell whether it had once been red or green. Her family in Ye had long since died out; there was no one to come for her. She didn't look up, just mended mesh after mesh, and that was likely how she had mended her way through these twenty-three years.

Zhou Heng stopped in front of her.

The woman sensed someone there and looked up. The firelight leapt and lit up her face, and his face too. She looked at him, looked for a long, long time, the shuttle in her hand stopped in mid-air. Then, very, very softly, she said:

"…Aheng?"

The ledger fell from Zhou Heng's hand.

That ledger thicker than a pillow dropped with a soft thud into the mud at the river's edge, and he didn't so much as glance at it.

The woman stood up, reached out and touched his face, as if she were touching something lost long, long ago. She said, "You've grown so tall." After a while she said, "…Do you still count? The counting your sister taught you — how far have you got?"

Zhou Heng's mouth hung open, his throat working a long time before any sound came: "…Twenty-three." He said, "One eighteenth of the tenth month a year. Counting this year, twenty-three."

He went down on his knees in front of her. She held his head in her arms.

Xiaoman stood under the willow and didn't go over. Ahe stood beside him, her little notebook clutched in her hand, unopened.

Xiaoman bent down and picked the ledger up out of the mud. The old account book tucked inside it had slid out a corner, open at that very page; muddy water had soaked half of it, but that one line could still be read perfectly clearly: **The eighteenth of the tenth month. The Zhang. Sister.** After it, still, there was nothing. He wiped the mud off it bit by bit with his sleeve, shut it, and tucked it inside his robe, keeping it for him.

That night the fires at Willow Bend, one after another, burned right through until dawn. Under the furthest willow, Zhou Heng sat beside his sister helping her straighten the fishing net, mesh by mesh, very, very slowly, as if, if he straightened it any faster, these twenty-three years really would be gone.

---

At dawn Zhou Heng came over from under the willow and held out his hand to Xiaoman. Xiaoman gave him back the ledger, the old one too.

Zhou Heng turned the old account book to that page, read it a long time in the morning light off the river, shut it, tucked it back inside his robe, and said, "…No need to add anything after this line."

Ahe wasn't by the fires. Xiaoman went looking along the riverbank and found her crouching at the water's edge. This was where the Zhang had drawn back yesterday, and it had left a layer of new silt, black and glistening. She took the bamboo tube out of her bundle, broke the wax, shook out three black grains, and pressed them one by one into the mud, pressed them in very deep. The other fourteen grains she put back and sealed with wax again. A girl in plaits stood behind her watching — last year's bride. Ahe stood up, brushed the mud off her hands, and said to her, "Keep an eye on it for me. I'll come and collect the interest next year."

Xiaoman borrowed Zhou Heng's brush and wrote a letter home, very short: Reached Ji Province, laid my foundation, senior brother Zhou is good to me, Ahe is well too. At the end the brush stopped for a long time, and then he added one more line:

Pa, your knees, wait one more year for me.

He folded the letter and thought: Ji Province, one cauldron; Xiaodou; Old Hu.

Zhou Heng, beside him, looked at the ingot showing from inside Xiaoman's robe and said, "This one goes in two sets of books. One's ours." He tapped the circle on the bottom of the ingot with his finger. "One's theirs." He looked across the Zhang at the line of old willows on the far bank. "Whatever's entered in their books, sooner or later they'll come to collect."

Xiaoman looked down at the cauldron in his palm. Underwater it had been taller than a man; out of the water it was only the size of his palm, yet it grew heavier hour by hour. The ingot inside his robe pressed against his heart, hard. Something the man under the willow had said only now came slowly floating up:

"You won't be able to hold on to that thing."

---

On the twenty-second of the tenth month the three of them reached the ferry on the north bank of the Yellow River. The river was so wide you couldn't make out the faces of people on the far bank, and the water was yellow; a ferry boat came rocking in to the shore. Zhou Heng paid the fare, and the three of them got on.

When the boat was in the middle of the river and Zhou Heng had opened his ledger to enter the fare, there was a soft *puff* overhead, and a paper crane came chasing after them from the north. The crane was folded from yellow joss paper, its wings crusted with frost; it dived straight into the ledger and unfolded itself. On it was the Abbot's writing, the ink laid on very heavy:

"Letter from the Taiyi Palace: you got a cauldron at the Zhang, and the Palace wishes to borrow it for a look. They have sent Elder Sima Lie to wait for you on the south bank of the Yellow River. Sima Lie is at Core Formation. Don't cross the river."

Zhou Heng read it twice and said, "…The Abbot never writes letters."

Xiaoman raised his head and looked towards the south bank.

On the ferry landing on the south bank a man was standing, in white, hands clasped behind his back, as if he had been standing there a long time. The wind off the river laid the reeds down flat, swathe after swathe, but where he stood it seemed to go round him. Two steps behind him stood another, the left sleeve empty, flapping in the wind.

Yinyue, in the front of his robe, said, "Core Formation. Your Abbot's not far off him."

The ferryman had seen too; his pole stopped in the water, and he turned to look at them. Ahe said, "Pole us back." The ferryman glanced towards the north bank and didn't pole. On the ferry landing on the north bank, at some point, two people in white had come to stand as well.

Xiaoman pressed his hand to the inside of his robe. Since leaving Ye the cauldron had grown heavier every day, and now it was heavy as a millstone. Under the Zhang, Bingyi had protected him; under this river, there was nothing.

The man on the south bank spoke. Across half the Yellow River his voice wasn't loud, yet it was as if he were speaking right at Xiaoman's ear:

"Shen Xiaoman of the Sunken Cauldron Abbey? Taiyi Palace, Sima Lie. The thing — you've brought it, I take it."
